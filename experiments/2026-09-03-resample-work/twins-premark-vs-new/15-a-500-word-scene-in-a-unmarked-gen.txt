Waiting Room, 2 a.m.

The vending machine hummed louder than anything else in the room. Mia had been staring at it for ten minutes, cataloguing the flavors she didn't want: sour cream and onion, barbecue, something called "zesty ranch" that had faded to a ghost of its own label.

Across from her, an older man in a flannel shirt kept checking his phone, turning it face-down, then face-up again, like the act of checking might change what was written there.

"You waiting on someone?" he asked, not looking up.

"My brother," Mia said. "He fell off a ladder. Idiot was cleaning gutters in the dark."

"In the dark."

"Says the motion light kept turning off and he didn't want to make two trips." She laughed, short and tired. "That's him. Efficient right up until it isn't."

The man nodded slowly. "My wife. Chest pains around midnight. Told her four hours ago it was probably nothing and now I keep thinking about how I said probably."

"Probably's not nothing."

"Feels like nothing now."

A nurse walked through with a clipboard, scanned the room, and left without calling a name. The clock above the reception desk had a second hand that stuttered instead of sweeping — click, click, click — like it, too, wasn't sure it wanted to keep going.

"You want a coffee?" Mia asked. "There's a machine by the elevators. Tastes like it's been sitting since the Clinton administration but it's hot."

"Which one."

"Bill."

He almost smiled. "Sure. Black."

She came back with two paper cups and handed him one, and they sat there not talking for a while, the way strangers do when talking has briefly run out of use. A television bolted to the wall played the news on mute, closed captions lagging a full sentence behind the anchor's mouth.

"Do you think they tell you the truth right away," the man said, "or do they warm you up to it first?"

"Depends on the truth, I think."

"That's not comforting."

"Wasn't trying to be."

He huffed something like a laugh into his coffee. "What's your brother's name?"

"Erik."

"I'll think good thoughts for Erik."

"I'll think good thoughts for—" she stopped. "I didn't get your wife's name."

"Karin."

"Good thoughts for Karin."

The automatic doors sighed open and a young couple came in, the woman holding her wrist at an odd angle, wincing with each step. The man in flannel glanced at the clock again, at the stuttering hand, and set his coffee down on the empty seat beside him like he was saving it for someone.

"They always make you wait longer at night," he said. "Like time itself needs a permission slip."

"Or like nobody wants to be the one who tells you first."

He didn't answer that. Outside, a siren rose and fell and rose again, and neither of them looked toward the doors.
