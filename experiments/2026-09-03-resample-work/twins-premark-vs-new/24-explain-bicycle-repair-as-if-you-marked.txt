Thought for 3s

The guy next door fixed his own bikes and mine too, when I asked, though he made sure I knew he'd rather be doing something else. He talked fast and assumed I'd already understood the first half of every sentence.

He never said "first" or "next." He'd just grab the wheel and say watch, then do three things at once. Quick release lever, flip it up, wheel comes out, don't touch the brake pads, you'll get grease on them and then they squeal for a month. That's how he taught. You caught what you could and filled in the rest later, usually wrong, and he'd correct you without slowing down.

Chains, he said, are the thing people overthink. You don't need the fancy tool. Turn the pedals backward while you watch where it's dirty, that's where it's stretched, that's what's making the noise. He kept a rag that was more grease than cloth, wiped his hands on his jeans anyway. If your chain skips under load, it's stretched, buy a new one, don't bother measuring, you'll know when it's bad because it starts happening on every ride, not just the steep bits.

Brakes he was less patient about, because brakes matter and he knew I'd be lazy about them. Squeeze the lever. Does it hit the bar? Then your cable's stretched or your pads are gone, go look, don't ask me, go look. He wanted you to develop the habit of looking before you asked. He'd stand there with his arms crossed while I checked, not helping, just making sure I did it.

Flats he treated as beneath discussion. Pump it up, spin the wheel, listen for the hiss, find it, that's it, that's the whole method. He didn't believe in soapy water and a bucket unless you were desperate. Patch kits he trusted more than tubes, said tubes were for people who liked spending money. He'd pinch the tube back into the tire with his thumbs, no levers, and if you reached for levers he'd sigh audibly.

Gears he explained once, badly, and never again. Barrel adjuster, turn it a quarter turn, shift up and down, see if it's better, if not turn it back and try the other way. That was the entire lesson. When I asked what the barrel adjuster actually did mechanically he said something about cable tension and walked back inside, like the question itself was a waste of a nice evening.

What he was actually teaching, I understood later, was where the noise comes from. Not the names of the parts, not torque specs, just: this sound means this problem, this feeling in the lever means that cause. He built that into you by making you diagnose things while he stood there refusing to just tell you.

He also had rules that weren't really about bikes. Don't force it. If it's not moving, you're missing something, not being weak. And: everything's tighter than you think and looser than you're afraid of, which made no sense the first ten times and then, one day, adjusting a headset with him gone for the summer, made complete sense.
