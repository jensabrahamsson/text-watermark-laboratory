The station broadcasts out of what used to be a hardware store, and you can still see the ghost of the paint aisle in the way the back room is laid out—shelving units bolted to the wall, now stuffed with jewel cases instead of paint cans. Nobody's gotten around to digitizing all of it. There's a laptop up front that handles the scheduled stuff, the syndicated shows, the automated overnight block that plays when everyone's gone home. But the CD rack sits behind the mixing board like a filing cabinet nobody wants to throw out, and every DJ who's worked a shift there for more than a month has their own relationship with it.

Some of it is genuinely useless now—burned promo copies from bands that broke up in 2009, a compilation of local ska acts that exists nowhere else, not on Spotify, not on Bandcamp, nowhere. That's actually the argument for keeping the rack. There's a woman who does Thursday nights, plays mostly jazz, and she'll tell you flatly that half her library isn't findable anywhere digital. Some regional pressing, some out-of-print reissue somebody donated in a cardboard box back when the station moved locations. If the disc scratches, that's it, that song is gone from the world as far as the station's concerned.

There's a system to it, technically. Color-coded stickers on the spines, a laminated sheet taped to the wall with a rough alphabetical logic that only makes sense if you've been shown it once by someone who's been there fifteen years. New volunteers get a kind of oral history lesson on their first shift—this shelf is genre, this shelf is local artists, this weird overflow crate on the floor is "sort someday," and it's been "someday" for at least six years.

The dust is real. It gets into the CD trays, makes the drive stick sometimes, and there's a can of compressed air kept next to the mixing board for exactly that reason. Somebody spilled coffee on the rack once, years ago, and there's still a faint ring on the second shelf where a mug sat too long.

What's interesting is that nobody's really pushing to get rid of it. There's been talk, on and off, about ripping the whole collection to a server, doing it properly, tagging everything. But it never quite happens, partly because it's a genuinely enormous job, and partly, you get the sense, because people like reaching for something. There's a difference between scrolling a playlist and pulling a disc off a shelf, reading the handwriting on a sticker some DJ wrote in 2003, and deciding, right then, that's the next song. The rack isn't efficient. It's just theirs.
