Late September, and the allotment is past its peak but not finished. The main path between plots is soft mud in the shade, dust in the open, from three weeks without proper rain. Someone's left a wheelbarrow tipped against a fence post, one tire flat.

Tomato plants are done producing but still standing, leaves gone yellow and curling at the edges, a few green fruits that won't ripen now clinging to the top branches. The lower leaves have that dark spotting — blight, probably — and nobody's bothered to pull the plants yet because there's still squash to deal with. Bamboo canes lean at angles where the twine's worked loose.

Squash and pumpkin vines have taken over more ground than anyone planned for, sprawling out over the paths, leaves the size of dinner plates gone papery and grey with powdery mildew. Underneath, orange and pale green fruits sit heavy on the soil, some resting in the dip where a leaf has collapsed around them. You have to lift the leaves to find them all.

The runner beans are stringy now, pods gone tough and lumpy with seeds, past the point of eating fresh — the ones left on the vine are for saving seed or for the compost. Sunflowers, if anyone grew them, are bent over, heads facing down, and there's usually a wasp or two working the drying disc florets for whatever's left in there.

Brassicas are coming into their own — kale, dark and slightly leathery, better after the first cool nights; purple sprouting broccoli still weeks off but visibly bulking up. Slug damage on the lower leaves, holes eaten clean through.

Someone's onions are lifted and laid out on an upturned crate to cure, papery skins going brittle in whatever sun there is. A few plots over, potato foliage has collapsed into black slime, which means someone's about to go digging with a fork, turning up more tubers than they expected, some cut through by the tines.

Leaves are starting to come down off the trees at the boundary — usually an old apple or two on these sites — collecting in the corners of sheds and against compost bins. Apples themselves, windfalls, sit split and going brown where wasps have got into them, sweet rot smell if you get close.

Sheds have their doors open more, people sorting through canes and stakes, deciding what to save and what's rotted through. Water butts are low, sludgy at the bottom. Bindweed and couch grass have pushed back into any bed that wasn't kept on top of over summer, white roots visible where someone's half-heartedly forked one out and left it in a pile to dry.

There's a general sense of triage — figure out what's still worth harvesting, what needs pulling now before it goes to mush, and what can be left another two or three weeks. Someone's bonfire pile is building at the far end, waiting for a dry enough day and the right time of year to burn it.
