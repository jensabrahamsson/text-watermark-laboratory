The park had three entrances, and Assar always used the one by the tennis courts because the gravel there was finer and didn't work its way into the dogs' paws the way the crushed stone by the north gate did. He had six dogs on his list that autumn, though never more than three at a time, and he had learned the choreography of their leashes the way a person learns to set a table without looking — which line crossed over which, whose harness clipped where.

The labrador belonged to a woman who worked at the hospital and never seemed to be home before dark. The two terriers belonged to a couple who traveled often and left keys with a lockbox code Assar had memorized without meaning to. The old spaniel, Bruno, belonged to a man named Torkel who had broken his hip in February and still, in September, moved through his apartment with a walker that left four small dents in the hallway rug.

Assar walked them at eleven, when the park was mostly empty of children and mostly empty of the runners who came before work. There were other dog walkers, a rotating handful, and they nodded to each other but rarely spoke beyond the weather. One of them, a young man with a whippet and sometimes a second dog that changed week to week, listened to something through a single earbud and mouthed words without sound.

The lake in the middle of the park had ducks that the terriers pretended not to notice, in the studied way of dogs told many times not to notice something. Bruno, being old, noticed nothing, or seemed not to, though sometimes he would stop entirely and stand with his nose lifted for a long moment, and Assar would stand with him, because there was no reason not to.

He had started doing this work two years ago, after his own dog died and he found he did not want another one yet, only the walking of one, the specific weight of a leash in his hand and the specific attention a dog paid to the ground. It filled something in the middle of his days that his freelance graphic design work, done mostly from a kitchen table, did not fill. He did not think of it as sad. His sister thought it was sad and said so, gently, at family dinners, and he let her think it.

In October, Torkel's daughter called to say her father had moved to a care home in Alingsås and Bruno would be going to live with her, two hours away. Assar walked him one last time, the same loop, past the tennis courts and around the lake and back. Bruno stopped at the same spot he always stopped, nose up, and Assar stood with him for what felt like a long time, though it was probably only a minute.

He kept the key to Torkel's apartment for another week before he remembered to return it. Nobody asked for it back. He put it in an envelope anyway and mailed it, and that was the end of that.
