January in the fishing village comes down like a lid. The harbor at Bovallstrand freezes at the edges first, a thin gray skin along the pilings, and by noon it has not thickened much because the water underneath still moves, still remembers it is the sea and not a pond. The boats that are still in the water sit lower than in summer, weighted with slush that nobody bails until the ice threatens to grip the hull. Two of them have been hauled out already, up on blocks behind Lindqvist's shed, tarps snapping when the wind comes round from the north.

There is a particular sound the place makes in January that it doesn't make the rest of the year: halyards against aluminum masts, an irregular clicking that never quite becomes rhythm, coming from the six or seven sailboats that should have been pulled for winter and weren't, because their owners live in Gothenburg now and only remember the boat exists in May. Someone should call about it. No one does.

The co-op store opens at eight and the woman behind the counter, Berit, keeps the lights off in the back half until a customer actually walks that far, to save on the bill. She sells less bread in January—maybe eleven loaves a day instead of thirty—and orders accordingly, a number she carries in her head without needing to write down, the way she carries which of the summer cabins are occupied this month, which is none.

Old Torsten still goes out for cod when the wind allows, which in January means maybe nine days out of thirty-one. He is seventy-four and has stopped pretending the boat is for anything but this, cod and the occasional herring, sold or given away to whoever is at the jetty when he comes in around two in the afternoon, the light already leaning toward evening. His hands, when he ties off, move slower than they did, and he notices this without saying it to anyone, the way you notice the tide is a little further out than you expected.

The children go to school in Hunnebostrand now, bussed each morning at seven-forty, so the harbor in daylight belongs mostly to older people and dogs. A woman named Anita walks hers—a wire-haired thing, unbothered by cold—along the same stretch of quay every day, past the ice cream kiosk that has had its shutters down since September, past the noticeboard where a flyer for a July concert has been rained on so many times the date is unreadable.

By four the light is gone. The village doesn't so much go dark as go interior—windows lit yellow one by one, kitchens where someone is standing at a stove, the murmur of the six o'clock news audible faintly if you walk close enough beneath an open vent. The sea keeps working at the rocks below the road, unseen now, only heard, the particular flat slap of small January waves that have nowhere urgent to be and nothing to prove.
