Taket på Kv. Renen 12 hade varit tänkt som något annat. Elin remembered the plans, laminated and pinned to the corkboard in the stairwell for two years: a rendering of tomato vines heavy and red, children from the daycare below holding up baskets, a woman in a sun hat who did not resemble anyone in the building. Now the glass panels sat clouded with a film that would not wash off, not entirely, no matter how many times Bengt from the fourth floor came up with his squeegee and his radio tuned low to P4.

Half the beds had gone to chickweed. It came up fast and pale between the rows of what used to be labeled — she could still make out ROSMARIN on a wooden marker gone silver with weather, the letters sinking back into the grain. The basil trays by the east wall were the worst of it, a slick brown collapse, the smell of it sweetish and close on warm days, the kind of smell that made you breathe through your mouth without deciding to. Someone, at some point, had covered three of the trays with black plastic sheeting, as if to end the conversation rather than continue it.

The irrigation system still clicked on at six each morning. This was the part Elin found hardest to explain to visitors, when there were visitors, which was rare now. The timer had outlived the plants. Water hissed out over dead soil and empty planters and one stubborn row of chard that refused, against all reasonable expectation, to die — bolted now, its leaves gone bitter and enormous, flowering upward in a way that looked less like harvest and more like distress. Nobody picked it. It had become a kind of landmark. New residents were told, by way of orientation, that's the chard, don't worry about it.

The greenhouse committee had dissolved sometime the previous winter, not through any vote but through attrition, the way committees do — one member moving to Kungsbacka, another taking a job with worse hours, a third simply not replying to the group chat until the group chat itself went quiet. Elin kept the key. She wasn't sure why. She came up sometimes in the evening, after her daughter was asleep, and sat on the overturned crate that had once held seed packets, and looked out through the fogged glass at the city doing what it did, indifferent and lit up, the tower cranes over by the harbor still moving even at that hour.

There was a plan, someone had said in the autumn, to convert the space — solar panels, maybe, or storage for the bicycle association. Nothing had come of it. The panels stayed clouded. The chard kept flowering. Once a magpie got in through a broken pane and could not find its way back out, and Elin had watched it, for three evenings running, throw itself softly against the glass, mistaking the light for the sky it remembered.
