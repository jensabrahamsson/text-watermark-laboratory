The station occupies two rooms above a hardware store in Strömstad, and the smell of the place is the first thing you notice — dust and solder and the particular mustiness of foam soundproofing that has absorbed forty years of coastal damp. Bengt has been the technical responsible since 1987, though nobody remembers if that's an official title or just what people started calling him. He still keeps the CD rack against the far wall, floor to ceiling, alphabetized by a system that makes sense only to him — not by artist, not quite by genre, but by some private logic involving mood and decade and, he claims, the acoustics of certain rooms he used to work in.

The rack holds maybe eleven hundred discs. Local choir recordings burned onto blanks in the nineties. A complete collection of Kajsa Grytt. Promotional copies that bands mailed in decades ago, hoping for three minutes of airtime, the shrink wrap still on some of them because nobody ever got around to it. Bengt runs his finger along the spines the way other people might run a hand along a fence they built themselves — proprietary, a little tired, still fond.

The younger volunteers use the streaming setup, obviously. There's a laptop with a subscription and a mixing board from this decade, and most of the Thursday and Friday programming runs entirely through that. But Sunday mornings belong to Bengt, and Sunday mornings belong to the CDs. He has a theory, not entirely serious, that digital audio compresses something out of a recording that has nothing to do with the file size — some warmth, some proof that a human being once stood in a room and made a sound. He knows this isn't really how it works. He says it anyway, every few months, to whoever's listening.

There's a kind of ritual to it that the algorithm can't replicate: the specific two seconds of silence before a track starts, the way he has to physically walk to the rack and pull the case and blow along the top of it out of habit even though it hasn't mattered in years. The listeners — and there aren't many, this is a station with maybe six hundred regular ears across the whole region — seem to notice the difference between his Sunday and the weekday feed, even if they couldn't say what it is. Someone wrote in once to say his show felt like being handed something rather than having something played at you.

The station will probably digitize the whole collection eventually. There's talk of a grant, someone's nephew who does archival work, a spreadsheet Bengt has been half-filling for two years listing catalog numbers he can mostly still read despite his eyes going. But for now the rack stays, gathering the fine gray dust that coastal towns produce in quantities disproportionate to their size, and on Sunday mornings a man in his seventies climbs a stepladder to reach the top shelf for a Swedish folk recording nobody else remembers exists, because some argument in his head about listening — about what it costs to make someone stop and pull a physical object off a shelf for you — is still, in his telling, worth having.
