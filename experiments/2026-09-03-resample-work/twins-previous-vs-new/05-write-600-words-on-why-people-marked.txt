Thought for 2s

Half the plants I've owned are dead, and I keep buying more. This isn't a confession, it's just the baseline condition of owning plants, and I think most people who keep them are living some version of the same arithmetic. You buy a pothos because it's supposed to be unkillable, you forget about it for three weeks, it goes crispy and yellow at the tips, you feel a small specific guilt, and then you buy another one anyway. The interesting question isn't why we're bad at this. It's why the badness doesn't stop us.

Part of it is that a houseplant is a strange kind of object. It's alive, but it doesn't do anything you can point to — it doesn't greet you, doesn't need walking, doesn't make noise when it's suffering. A dying plant looks almost exactly like a healthy plant until it very suddenly doesn't. There's no feedback loop the way there is with a dog scratching at the door. So the neglect doesn't feel like neglect while it's happening. It feels like nothing at all, right up until the leaves go translucent and papery, and by then it's a diagnosis problem, not a maintenance one.

There's also the fact that plants are sold to us as decor before they're sold as organisms. You walk into a shop for a lamp and leave with a fiddle-leaf fig because it looked good next to the window in the display. Nobody at the checkout tells you it wants humidity or that it drops leaves the second it's moved to a new room out of what basically amounts to spite. You bought a shape and a color, and it turned out to have a metabolism.

But I don't think it's just carelessness or bad marketing. I think people want the plant regardless of whether they can sustain it, because owning one is a bet on a version of yourself — the version who waters on schedule, who notices when the soil's dry, who has slowed down enough to tend something. That version doesn't materialize just because a plant showed up on the windowsill, but the plant sits there as a kind of standing invitation, and even a half-dead one is evidence you tried to become that person, however briefly.

There's also something to the fact that a plant is low-stakes in a way almost nothing else in an adult life is. You can fail at it completely and the consequences are a compost bin and eleven dollars, not a relationship or a job. So people let themselves attempt it precisely because failure is cheap and mostly private. Nobody's checking in on your ZZ plant. If it dies, you just quietly replace the pot with something else and nobody audits you for it.

And then there's the specific comfort of a low bar that keeps getting advertised as unmissable. Every plant shop has a sign somewhere claiming this one's "impossible to kill," and that phrase does something to people — it turns the plant into a referendum on basic competence rather than a genuine gardening challenge. When it dies anyway, the instinct isn't usually to conclude you shouldn't own plants. It's closer to disbelief, followed by a new plant, because surely the next one is the actually-unkillable one.

I don't think any of this is really about neglect as a character flaw. It's closer to what happens with gym memberships or unread books on the shelf — objects people acquire as a stand-in for an intention, where the having of the thing does some of the work that using the thing was supposed to do. The plant on the sill is aspirational furniture. Whether it lives or not is almost beside the point, which is, admittedly, exactly the kind of thing that gets a plant killed.
