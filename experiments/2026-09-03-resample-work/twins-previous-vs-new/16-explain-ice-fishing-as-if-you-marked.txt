Thought for 4s

I went once, with a colleague's father-in-law who apparently does this every weekend from December to March, and I understood about halfway through that this was not a shared hobby so much as a thing I was being shown, the way you'd show someone a machine you built.

We drove out before six in the morning to a lake I never learned the name of. The car thermometer said minus fourteen. He had a small drill, a real one, gas-powered, and it went through the ice in maybe twenty seconds, which surprised me — I'd pictured hours of effort, something mythic. Instead there was just a hole, black water rising slightly in it, steaming faintly in the cold air.

After that it was mostly standing. He set up two rods with tiny bobbers and we sat on buckets, and he talked about ice thickness for a while — you could hear it, apparently, a kind of low groan traveling underneath us, which he said was normal and I chose to believe him about. I kept my hands in my pockets and took them out only to adjust my hat, which did nothing, because the cold wasn't coming from above, it was coming up through the bucket, through my boots, up from the ice itself like the lake had its own opinion about me being there.

We caught two perch in four hours. Small ones. He seemed satisfied. I remember being fascinated for maybe the first twenty minutes — the hole, the silence, the way sound behaved differently out there, flat and close — and then after that my attention just narrowed down to my toes, checking on them, negotiating with them. There's a specific kind of boredom that comes from being uncomfortable somewhere beautiful, where you know you're supposed to be having a moment and instead you're doing arithmetic about how much longer you can stand it.

He offered me coffee from a thermos around hour two and it was the best thing that has ever happened to me. I don't say that lightly. Ten seconds of held heat in a cup, and my whole assessment of the day shifted for exactly as long as I was drinking it.

What I remember most isn't the fish or the ice or even the cold, really — it's the drive home, heater blasting, feeling my feet come back to life in stages, an unpleasant prickling that I was nonetheless grateful for. He asked if I wanted to come again sometime and I said maybe, which we both understood to mean no.

I don't regret going. I think about it more than the day itself would suggest I should — the flat white light, the sound under the ice, that one specific cup of coffee. But I've never been back, and when people describe ice fishing as peaceful I believe them the way you believe someone describing a religion you don't practice.
