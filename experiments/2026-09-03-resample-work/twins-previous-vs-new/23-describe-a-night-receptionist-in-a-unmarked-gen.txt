The receptionist's name was Bengt, and he had worked the night shift at the Hotel Kompass for eleven years, which was longer than the hotel had been called the Kompass. Before that it was the Sailor's Rest, and before that something else he couldn't remember, because names changed but the lobby stayed the same: brown carpet worn pale in a path from the door to the desk, a fern that had died and been replaced by a plastic fern nobody bothered to dust, and a radiator that ticked at midnight like it was counting something.

He came in at ten, when the day girl Malin left with her coat already buttoned, always in a hurry, always saying the same thing — quiet night, probably, Thursdays usually are. She was often right. He liked that about her, that she didn't pretend to know more than she did.

The guests who came late were a certain kind. Truck drivers who smelled of the road and paid in cash and asked for a room away from the street. A woman traveling to see a sick relative, checking in with a small bag and eyes that had been crying earlier but weren't now. Sometimes a young couple who couldn't afford the nicer places downtown, holding hands at the desk like they'd done something brave just by walking in together.

Bengt gave out keys — real keys, on a plastic tag with a number, because the hotel had never gotten around to the electronic kind — and told them where the ice machine was, though half the time it was broken, and he'd just say it anyway out of habit, the way you tell someone good morning without checking the sky.

Between check-ins there was very little to do. He read paperbacks he found in the lost-and-found box, thrillers mostly, though he preferred it when there was a plain one about a farm or a family, something where nothing much happened. He drank coffee from a thermos his wife filled for him before he left the flat, and around two in the morning he'd eat the sandwich she packed, sitting behind the desk with his elbows on the wood, watching the street through the glass door for the taxis that rarely came.

Sometimes a guest couldn't sleep and came down just to talk. An old man once told him about his years working on the ferries between Gothenburg and Kiel, how the sea in November had a smell you never forgot. Bengt listened the way he always did, nodding, refilling silences with small questions, because that was most of the job, really — not the keys or the register but the listening, the being awake so other people didn't have to be awake alone.

At six the sky over the parking lot turned the color of dishwater, and the day girl's replacement, a boy named Erik, would come in stamping his boots and asking if it had been quiet. Bengt would say yes, mostly, and hand him the log, and walk out past the dead fern into the cold, already thinking of his bed, of sleep, of the ordinary hours waiting for him like a held breath finally let go.
