The shutter goes up at ten past seven, not seven, because Bengt's knee has been bad since March and the chain sticks halfway if you rush it. He swears at it in the same three words every morning, then again when the fluorescent tube over the paint counter flickers twice before catching. The shop smells like it always does before the door's been open long enough to air out — linseed oil, wet cardboard, the faint metal tang of the key-cutting machine, and under it something like turpentine that no one can trace to any single tin.

The radio is already on, tuned to P4, turned low enough that it's more company than program. Bengt doesn't listen so much as let it run while he counts the float in the till twice because the number was wrong yesterday and he still hasn't found where it went. Forty kronor. Not enough to matter, enough to bother him.

By half seven the delivery van from the wholesaler is double-parked outside with its hazards on, and the driver — a different one most weeks — wheels in pallets of birdseed and rock salt that nobody ordered yet, not for another month, but the schedule doesn't care what the weather's doing. Boxes go in the back room in whatever order fits, which means the WD-40 ends up stacked behind the extension cords again, and someone will curse about that in a week.

The first customer is Alvar from two streets over, who doesn't buy anything most days. He comes in for the coffee machine, the bad one, the one that's been "getting fixed" since before Christmas, and drinks it standing by the window looking out at nothing in particular. He and Bengt talk about a fence post, hypothetically, the way men who've known each other thirty years discuss things that may never happen.

A woman comes in wanting a specific size of dowel and leaves without it because the bins are still not properly labeled after the reorganization in January — a project half-finished, like the shelf of hinges that's meant to be alphabetized by function and instead sits in the rough order someone left it in eight months ago. Bengt says he'll check the back. He does not check the back. He knows there isn't one.

Around nine the apprentice, Elin, comes in still eating something out of foil, and gets sent straight to sort the returns bin — three items, none with receipts, one of them clearly used. She argues about the price of a padlock with a man who insists it was fifteen kronor cheaper last spring, and might be right.

Outside, the gravel lot is half in shadow still. A dog is tied to the bike rack, patient, not barking. Somewhere in the back, the radiator clanks once, the way it does every morning around now, for no reason anyone has ever figured out, and Bengt doesn't look up, because it always stops on its own by quarter past.
