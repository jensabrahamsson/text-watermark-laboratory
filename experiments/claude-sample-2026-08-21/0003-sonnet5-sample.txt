The apple tree stood exactly on the line between Ingvar's garden and Solveig's, which was the whole of the problem, and had been for eleven years.

"It drops on my side," Ingvar said. He was standing at the fence with a rake in one hand, though there was nothing yet to rake. It was only August, and the apples were still hard and green and clinging.

"It grows on my side," said Solveig, not looking up from her chair. She had brought a cup of coffee out to the garden and set it on the armrest, and now she held it there without drinking.

"The trunk," Ingvar said, "is on the line. I've measured."

"You've measured for eleven years and it hasn't moved."

He had, in fact, measured for eleven years, with a folding ruler his father had left him, laying it flat against the bark and reading the number aloud as if it were a verdict. Twelve centimeters onto her side, he always said. Sometimes fourteen, depending on where he pressed the ruler, but he never mentioned that.

"The apples that fall on my lawn," he said, "rot there. They draw wasps. My grandchildren can't play barefoot."

"Then don't let them play barefoot in August. Everyone knows not to play barefoot under an apple tree in August."

This was, he had to admit privately, true. But he did not say so.

The tree itself gave no sign of noticing the dispute. It had been planted, according to the deed records Ingvar had once pulled from the municipal archive out of pure stubbornness, by a man named Halvorsen in 1954, before either property had been divided the way it now was. Halvorsen was dead. His house had been split, sold, split again. The tree remained, indifferent, widening a little more each year, its roots doing whatever roots do underground, which is to say going wherever they please.

"I could have it looked at," Ingvar said. "A tree surgeon. Ask which side the roots favor."

"You could," Solveig agreed, "but I don't think roots care much for property lines either."

He set the rake against the fence post, which was as close as he came, most days, to giving up the argument, though neither of them would have called it giving up. It was simply a pause. Tomorrow or the day after he would mention the wasps again, or the leaves in the gutter, and she would mention the shade it threw over her tomatoes in June, which she liked, or the smell of the blossoms in May, which she also liked, and somewhere in the middle of all that they would still be neighbours, still be the ones who took in each other's post when the other was away, still be the ones who stood at this fence more afternoons than not.

"There'll be a good crop this year," she said finally, looking up at the branches. "Heavy with them already."

"There will," he said.

Neither of them moved to measure anything.
