The bus was almost empty by the time it reached Aspedalen, and Torbjörn let his shoulders drop for the first time since Backadalen. Three stops left. He knew them the way he knew the back of his own hand, though the back of his hand had changed more in twenty years than these stops had. The same low apartment blocks, the same gravel path down to the football pitch, the same bench where old Rune sat some mornings just to watch the buses go by, not waiting for anything, just watching.

The rain had stopped an hour ago but the windows still held it, streaks drying in uneven lines across the glass. He'd wiped the windshield twice already out of habit more than need. A woman near the back was asleep with her head against the pole, waking every few minutes to check they hadn't passed her stop, then drifting off again. He'd let her sleep as long as he could. There was no schedule so tight that a person couldn't have thirty more seconds of rest.

He thought, as he often did at this hour, about how strange the job was. Not hard, not exactly easy either, but strange in the way that all repetition becomes strange if you pay attention to it. Twelve hours a day he moved through the same nine kilometers, past the same laundromat with its flickering sign, the same church with its scaffolding that had been up since spring, the same crossing where schoolchildren used to run out without looking and now, this late, was empty except for a cat that crossed slow and unbothered, like it owned the asphalt.

At the second-to-last stop an old man got on with a bag of groceries and nodded at him the way regulars did, not needing to say anything because the nod said it. Torbjörn nodded back. He liked these silent exchanges more than he probably should have. There was something honest in them. You saw a person's face at the same time every day for years and you built something out of that, some thin but real thread, even if you never learned their name.

The last stretch ran along the water, and here the bus always seemed to slow down on its own, though the speed hadn't changed at all. Streetlights caught the surface of the strait in long broken lines. He rolled the window down two centimeters, enough to let the cold air in, enough to smell the salt and the diesel together, a smell he associated now with the end of every shift more than with anything else in his life.

At the depot he sat for a moment after the last passenger left, hands still on the wheel though the engine was already ticking down. The radio in his pocket crackled once and went quiet. Somewhere behind him another driver was starting the same route from the beginning, and he found that thought steadying rather than tiring, the way tides are steadying, the way certain repetitions hold a person up instead of wearing them down.
