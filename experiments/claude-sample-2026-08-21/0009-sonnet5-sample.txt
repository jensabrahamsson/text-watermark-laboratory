The streetlights were still on when Karin came out through the staff entrance, though the sky had already gone the color of skimmed milk. She always used the back door because the front one opened onto the ambulance bay, and after a night like this one she didn't want to look at it again, not even from a distance.

Her bike was where she'd left it, dew on the seat, and she didn't wipe it off, just sat down into the wet because it hardly mattered now. The chain needed oil. It had needed oil for two weeks. She rode slowly along Norra Ågatan, past the bakery where a man in a flour-dusted apron was carrying trays out to the front window, past the kiosk that wouldn't open for another hour, past a dog walker who nodded at her the way people do when they recognize a fellow early riser without knowing anything else about each other.

The night had been a long one. An elderly man in room fourteen had died around three, quietly, the way some of them do, as if they'd simply decided the room had gotten too crowded with machines and left while no one was looking directly at him. She had been the one to call it, to note the time, to help arrange the sheet before the family came in. His daughter had held his hand and thanked Karin for being kind to him, which had nearly undone her, because she hadn't done anything except what the job asked, and it felt strange to be thanked for arithmetic.

Now the town was turning inside out around her, night handing itself over to morning the way a shift hands itself to the next nurse, an unhurried transfer of information nobody quite says out loud. Sprinklers ticked on in someone's garden. A window opened two floors up and a woman leaned out to shake a rug, and dust hung there a moment in the early light before settling. Karin's legs were heavy in that specific way they got only after twelve hours on her feet, a heaviness that lived somewhere behind the knees.

She stopped at the canal because she always stopped at the canal, some old habit from her first year that had never left her. The water was flat and the color of tin, and a heron stood at the far bank with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry, which she envied without irony. Two swans slept with their heads folded backward into their own feathers.

At home, her husband would still be sleeping, the children not yet awake, and there would be a stretch of maybe forty minutes that belonged to no one but her, coffee and the particular silence of a house not yet started. She got back on the bike. Her shadow went out long and thin across the wet asphalt ahead of her, dawn light behind her shoulders now, and somewhere behind that, the hospital, already filling up again with whatever the day would bring, though it was none of her business anymore, not until tonight.
