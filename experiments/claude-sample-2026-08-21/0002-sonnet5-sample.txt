Morning starts before the doors open. Someone's already inside by 8:15 even though opening is 10—usually the branch manager or whoever drew the short straw on book returns, because the after-hours drop box has been quietly filling up all night and it needs to be emptied before the automated sorter jams again. It jams anyway. It always jams on the DVDs, which nobody weeded from the collection because someone's aunt still borrows the Wallander box set every few months.

The circulation desk isn't glamorous. Half the job is technical support that has nothing to do with books: printing a boarding pass, helping someone reset a BankID login, explaining for the fourth time this week why the library card app won't scan. There's a laminated sheet taped under the counter with the wifi password and the number for the IT guy who covers six branches and answers maybe a third of the time.

The collection itself is in a constant, low-grade state of disorder. Returns pile up on carts faster than anyone can reshelve them, so there's always a cart parked somewhere awkward—blocking the large-print section, or wedged near the windows where the sun's been slowly bleaching the spines of the same twelve paperbacks for two years. Someone reshelves by Dewey number with real precision in the morning; by afternoon, patrons have pulled things out, glanced at the back cover, and stuck them back three shelves off. Nobody polices this seriously. Correcting it is a background task, done in fifteen-minute stretches between everything else.

Then there's the people who aren't there for books at all. The library is, unofficially, the warmest free indoor space in town with a bathroom, so on cold days it fills with people who need somewhere to sit for a few hours—some reading, some sleeping upright with a newspaper held like a prop, some just charging a phone. Staff mostly leave this alone unless someone's causing trouble, which happens rarely but does happen, and then it's a judgment call nobody trained you for: do you call someone, do you just ask them to keep it down, do you let it go because it's minus eight outside.

Programming is the part that looks good on a poster and is a scramble behind it. Story time for toddlers runs every Tuesday, and it depends entirely on whether the one staff member who's good at voices is working that day. A local author's talk gets scheduled months out, then eleven people show up, four of whom are there for the free coffee. Meanwhile a homework help session for teenagers, thrown together almost as an afterthought, ends up packed every week because it turns out that's what people actually needed—quiet tables and wifi that works.

Budget conversations happen in the back office and they're unglamorous too: whether to renew a database subscription almost nobody uses, whether the broken self-checkout machine gets fixed this quarter or next, whether there's money for more copies of whatever's currently on every book club's list. There's always a stack of donated books nobody quite knows what to do with—some genuinely good, most destined for the sale cart by the entrance, priced at five kronor and still sitting there in June.

By closing, the sorter's jammed again, the toddler section looks like a small storm passed through it, and someone's left their charger plugged into an outlet by the newspapers for the third time this month. The lights go off in sections, not all at once—first the study room, then the stacks, last the front desk—and whoever's closing does one lap past the windows to check nobody's still curled up in the armchair by the biography section, which happens more often than you'd think.
