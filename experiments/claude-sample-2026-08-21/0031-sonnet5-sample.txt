The press room at Alingsås Tidning smells of solvent and hot metal, and Bengt has stopped believing in Tuesdays. It is always Tuesday when the folder jams — some private arrangement between the machine and the calendar that no one else has been let in on. He kills the motor with the flat of his hand, the way you'd calm a horse, and the great cylinders sigh down through their revolutions, slower, slower, until the paper stops moving and just sits there, half-eaten, ink still wet on the fold nobody asked it to make.

He has worked here nineteen years and he no longer curses when it happens. He used to. His father, who ran the same press before the company bought the newer one in '91, had a whole liturgy of curses, delivered in the same flat voice he used for the weather. Bengt inherited the press but not the vocabulary. He opens the access panel instead and reaches in with two fingers, feeling for the torn sheet the way a dentist feels for a root, blind and by memory.

Elin comes down from layout at quarter to eleven, already knowing. She doesn't ask what's wrong. She leans in the doorway with a proof sheet rolled under her arm — page four, the notice about the church choir's autumn concert, a photograph of Solveig Bergman holding sheet music she can no longer quite read without her glasses, though she'd never say so to the photographer. Elin watches Bengt's hands and says only, "How long."

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less."

It is never less.

The paper itself is small — twelve pages on a good week, eight in summer when there's nothing but the harbor regatta and a dispute about the new bicycle lane on Kungsgatan — and by rights a jam like this shouldn't matter to anyone beyond these two rooms. But the delivery boy, Torvald, is seventeen and waiting on his moped outside with the engine off to save petrol, and Fru Kallstenius at the corner kiosk keeps her coffee hot for the exact minute the bundle arrives, and old Erlandsson three streets over has already been to his mailbox twice this morning out of pure habit, the paper not due for another hour, checking anyway because some rhythms outlast their reasons.

Bengt finds the tear — a hair's width in the web, invisible until it isn't — and re-threads the paper through the rollers with the patience of a man stringing a very large, very stubborn instrument. Elin holds the loose end taut. Neither of them speaks. The overhead light hums its one flat note. Outside, a gull complains about something on the roof.

When the press restarts it does so gradually, a low mechanical throat-clearing before it finds its stride, and then the sheets begin to fall again in their steady rhythmic slap, page after page, Solveig's photograph flashing past thousands of times in identical grey. Bengt checks his watch. Twenty-two minutes. He writes nothing down. There is no ledger for this, no column in any spreadsheet in Stockholm that accounts for the small tearing and mending that keeps a town's Tuesday morning arriving roughly on time, smelling of ink, folded twice, slightly warm.
