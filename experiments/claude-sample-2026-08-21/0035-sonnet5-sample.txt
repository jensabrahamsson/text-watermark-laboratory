The bell above the door still works, though the tape holding the clapper has yellowed and one corner has come loose. Mirjam presses it flat with her thumb on her way past, out of habit rather than need, and turns the sign so that ÖPPET faces the glass and the street reads STÄNGT instead.

The register drawer sticks in the cold. She has to lift it slightly on its left runner, a motion her hand knows without her thinking about it, and count what's there against the receipt tape. Four hundred and thirty kronor, most of it in the fifties that people use for coats and boots, the things that still move. The scarves haven't moved in a month. She's stopped ordering more.

Outside, Storgatan is empty except for a man walking a dog that keeps stopping to smell the base of the streetlamp. Mirjam watches him through the window while she folds the sweaters that got pulled from their shelf and tried on and abandoned over chairs, over the arm of the changing-room bench, one even draped over the small bust form near the till like someone had dressed it and lost interest. She refolds them the way her mother taught her, sleeves in, then the body in thirds, so the fold lines don't show when someone unfolds them again in a week to try once more.

The heater under the counter ticks as it cools. She has learned to read the shop by its sounds at closing — the heater ticking, the rack wheels creaking where the fifth wheel from the left needs oil, the specific silence that settles once the street traffic thins after six. It is a different silence than morning silence. Morning silence still has possibility in it. This one is just tired.

She checks the racks for anything left inside out, a habit from years of previous owners' complaints, though there is no one left to complain now except her own memory of complaining. Two hangers empty on the winter-coat rail — sold, she remembers now, a green wool one and a herringbone with a chewed button that she'd meant to replace and hadn't. She makes a note on the pad by the register: button, herringbone, if it comes back.

The children's corner needs sweeping. It always needs sweeping, threads and glitter and the crumbs of crackers someone's toddler ate while the parent looked through onesies. She sweeps it into the dustpan without really seeing it, thinking instead about the invoice for the rent that came yesterday, still unopened on the counter, and about whether to open it now or let it wait until morning when she has more in her to meet it with.

She doesn't open it. She switches off the changing-room light, then the window display, then the main strip lights above the racks, until only the little lamp over the till is left, and by that light she counts the drawer once more before locking it inside the small safe under the counter.

The key sticks too, in the cold. Everything here does. She turns it twice, out of habit, and steps out into the street, pulling the door until she hears it catch.
