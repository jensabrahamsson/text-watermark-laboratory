The fluorescent lights in the truck bay hummed as Elin pulled the bay door down, its chain rattling through the dark like something clearing its throat. Outside, the sky had that thin, bruised look it got in late October in Lerum, not quite night yet but committed to the idea.

"Kartong," said Henrik, meaning nothing in particular, just testing whether his voice still worked after twenty minutes of standing in someone's kitchen apologizing for a burnt saucepan. He coiled the last length of hose over his forearm, elbow to palm, elbow to palm, the way Sven had taught him nine years ago and the way he now taught the seventeen-year-olds who joined for the résumé line and stayed, some of them, for reasons they never quite explained.

"She kept saying she was sorry," Elin said. "Like we'd rather have been somewhere else."

"Would you rather?"

"No." She meant it. That was the strange arithmetic of it — twelve minutes from the tone going off to standing in a stranger's smoke-thin kitchen, and even a false alarm left something in her chest that took an hour to settle, a low hum of readiness that had nowhere to go. She missed it a little, always, once it was gone.

They worked without needing to talk much, which was its own kind of conversation. Henrik checked the oxygen gauges on the tanks, tapping each one with two fingers like he was testing fruit. Elin logged the call in the binder that nobody had digitized despite four separate proposals, because Sten who ran the station wrote better cursive than any of them typed, and there was an unspoken sense that the binder was older than the argument for replacing it.

Falskt larm. Bränd gryta. Inga skador. False alarm. Burnt pot. No injuries.

"You want the lights or should I," Henrik said, nodding toward the panel by the door.

"I'll do it. You get the gate."

Outside, the gravel lot held the last warmth of the day in a way the air no longer did. Henrik's breath showed when he exhaled. Somewhere past the tree line a dog was barking at something that was probably nothing, the way dogs did, the way the smoke detector in that kitchen had done its one honest job and everyone had treated it, briefly, like an emergency.

Elin flicked the switches one by one — bay lights, then the office, then the sign above the door that said FRIVILLIGA BRANDKÅREN in red letters that had faded to the color of a healing bruise. The building went dark except for the small green light that meant the alarm system itself was still listening, still awake, the way it always was.

Henrik swung the gate shut and looped the padlock through, not locking it yet, just resting it there while he waited for her.

"Same time Thursday?" she asked, though she knew the answer.

"Same time Thursday."

He clicked the lock shut. Somewhere behind them the building ticked as it cooled, settling into its waiting, and they walked to their cars across gravel that sounded, in the quiet, almost like static.
