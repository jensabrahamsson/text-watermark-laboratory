Thought for 2s

I've killed two starters. Not through neglect—through attention, which turned out to be worse.

The first one I fed on a schedule I found online: every twelve hours, discard half, add flour and water, cover, wait. By day four it smelled sharp and alive, bubbles pushing up through the surface like something breathing underneath. I got excited and baked too early. The loaf came out dense at the bottom, a wet grey line an inch thick, the rest of it fine but unremarkable. I told myself the starter wasn't the problem. The starter was the problem.

The second attempt I did everything "right." I bought a kitchen scale. I used bottled water because someone said chlorine kills the culture. I kept it warm on top of the fridge. This time the starter rose beautifully, doubled in four hours, smelled like yogurt and green apple. I made the dough, did my stretch-and-folds, shaped it, put it in the fridge overnight for the long ferment everyone talks about. In the morning it had barely moved. I scored it, baked it anyway, and got something closer to a frisbee than bread—dense, gummy in the center, a crust like roofing material.

What I understand now, having failed at it twice, is that sourdough isn't a recipe you follow. It's a relationship with a population of organisms whose mood depends on your specific kitchen, your specific flour, your specific water, the specific day. Every guide gives you numbers—"rise for 4-6 hours," "ferment at 75°F"—and the numbers are lies, or rather they're true for someone else's kitchen and false for yours. My apartment runs cold in the mornings. My starter, apparently, is a slow riser compared to whatever starter the recipe writers keep. The four hours other people wait, mine needs seven. Nobody tells you this because nobody can; it's not a fact you can print, it's a fact you have to notice.

The second failure taught me something the first one didn't: that a dough can look ready and not be ready, and the only way to know the difference is to have felt both, many times, until your hands know something your eyes don't. The poke test—press it, if it springs back slowly it's proofed—means nothing until you've pressed underproofed dough and overproofed dough and ruined bread both ways and can finally tell them apart by feel.

I haven't tried a third time yet. I've been reading instead, not recipes but explanations—what gluten does, what wild yeast actually is, why cold retards fermentation, why salt matters for more than taste. I think the mistake both times was wanting the process to be legible immediately, wanting a set of instructions that would produce a result, when actually what's required is closer to what any craft requires: enough failures that you stop needing the instructions and start trusting what you're looking at.

I still don't have bread. I have two dead starters in the compost and a decent understanding of what killed them.
