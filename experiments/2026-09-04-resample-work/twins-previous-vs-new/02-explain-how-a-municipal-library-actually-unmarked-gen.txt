The library opens at ten, but someone's been there since half eight. Marianne, usually, unlocking the back door by the loading bay, turning on the lights section by section — not all at once, there's a breaker that trips if you do it too fast. Coffee first. Then the returns bin gets wheeled in from outside, the one people stuff books into overnight, and half the morning is just that: scanning things back in, checking spines for damage, noticing someone's used a receipt as a bookmark and left it in, someone else has folded down a corner so hard it's basically cut through the page.

The catalogue system crashes sometimes. Not often, maybe once every couple of months, and when it does nobody panics anymore, they just write due dates on paper slips and enter it all later. There's a laminated instruction sheet taped inside a cupboard door for exactly this situation, yellowed now, written by someone who left years ago.

Interlibrary loans are their own small bureaucracy. A request comes in for a book the branch doesn't have, and it has to be tracked down — sometimes from the county library two towns over, sometimes from a university collection, and the van that does the rounds comes on Tuesdays and Thursdays. If a book misses the Tuesday van it just sits for two more days. People complain about this sometimes, gently, and the staff have no good answer except that it's how the system works.

There's always at least one difficult patron issue running in the background. Right now it's a man who comes in every day to use the computers and sometimes falls asleep at the desk, and nobody quite knows what to do about it — he's not causing trouble, he's just there, for hours, and the staff have started quietly checking on him rather than moving him along.

Story time is Tuesday and Thursday mornings, and it eats the whole children's section for about forty minutes — chairs dragged into a circle, a puppet that lives in a box under the desk, someone's toddler always makes a break for the picture books during the reading and has to be retrieved. After it ends there's a mess of board books to reshelve and usually a child crying because they don't want to leave.

Budget meetings happen upstairs, in a room that used to be storage, and mostly they're about whether the periodicals subscription list can survive another year, or whether the second part-timer's hours get cut. Nobody likes these meetings. The head librarian, who has been there eleven years, has gotten good at making the case for things without sounding desperate about it.

Around three the after-school crowd arrives — kids doing homework, kids not doing homework, a group that plays cards quietly in the corner by the windows and has been doing this for two years running, unofficial regulars nobody's ever bothered to move on because they're not loud and they always push their chairs in.

Closing is the reverse of opening. Lights off in sections, computers logged out one by one, the returns bin wheeled back outside for the night. Someone checks the bathrooms. Someone checks that the back door actually latched, because once it didn't and a fox got into the building overnight and nobody has ever fully explained how.

It isn't the calm public image — quiet rooms, hushed browsing. It's paperwork and small crises and the same fifteen people every day and a system that mostly holds together because a handful of people know exactly which shelf, which form, which override code makes the whole thing keep running.
