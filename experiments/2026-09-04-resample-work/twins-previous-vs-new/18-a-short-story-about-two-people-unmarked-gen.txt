The alarm had gone quiet forty minutes ago, but the adrenaline was still leaving Marit's hands. She could feel it in the way her fingers fumbled with the padlock, the small tremor that always came after and never during.

"Grease fire in a toaster," Henrik said, coiling the last length of hose. "A toaster. My grandmother had a toaster that survived three moves and a flood."

"People panic," Marit said. "That's the job. Not putting out fires. Managing panic."

The station smelled of diesel and the particular mustiness of a building that was mostly empty most of the time — cinderblock walls, two bays, a kitchenette with a coffee maker nobody had descaled in a year. Henrik hung the hose on its rack and stepped back to check it was even, the way he always did, though nobody had ever told him to.

"You want the lights or the doors?" he asked.

"Doors. You always leave the side one unlatched."

"One time."

"Twice."

He laughed, short, and went to check the bay doors anyway, running his palm along the bottom edge where the rubber seal had started to peel. He'd meant to fix that for two months. Everyone had things they meant to fix for two months.

Marit walked the perimeter of the main room, killing switches. The fluorescent lights ticked as they died, one bank at a time, the room shrinking into itself with each row that went dark. She stopped at the board by the door — six names on it, six phone numbers, a laminated schedule someone had made in Word and never updated. Erik's name was still up there. Erik had moved to Umeå in March.

"We should redo that board," she said.

"You said that in June."

"I know I said it in June."

Henrik came back rubbing his hands on his jacket, though there was nothing on them. Outside, a car passed, headlights sweeping briefly across the frosted glass of the door before pulling away, and the room went dark again in its wake.

"Toaster," he said again, shaking his head, but he was smiling now, the tightness gone out of his shoulders. "I had visions. I really did. I was already thinking about the extraction."

"You always think about the extraction."

"It's a good thing to think about."

"It's a way of not thinking about the toaster."

He conceded that with a shrug and pulled his keys from his pocket. Marit checked the register — call logged, time noted, nothing else to write. It was strange how little there was to say about nothing happening. The book wanted details and there weren't any, just a woman who'd left a slice of bread in too long and a smoke detector that had done exactly what it was built to do.

They stepped out together, and Henrik pulled the main door down, the chain rattling through the pulley in that particular two-note clatter, and then the click of the padlock. The gravel lot was empty except for their two cars, parked at angles, headlights off.

"Same time Thursday," he said, meaning practice, not another alarm.

"Same time Thursday," she said.

She sat in her car a moment before starting it, watching him pull out first, taillights receding down the unlit road toward the main street, and then she followed.
