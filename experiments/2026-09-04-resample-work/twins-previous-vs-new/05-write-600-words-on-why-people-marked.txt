Thought for 2s

People buy a plant with the best intentions. It sits on the windowsill looking hopeful for about three weeks, then it starts to yellow, or drop leaves, or go crispy at the tips, and within two months it's a stick in a pot that nobody has the heart to throw away. And then, often within days of composting the corpse, the same person is back at the garden center picking out another one. This isn't a rare pattern. It's closer to the default.

Part of it is that a houseplant is a strange kind of object to own. It's alive, but it doesn't do anything you can point to. It doesn't greet you, doesn't need walking, doesn't make a mess you're forced to clean up on a schedule. Its needs are real but silent, which means the cost of ignoring them is deferred rather than immediate. You don't find out you've been underwatering a fern for six weeks until the leaves already look wrong, by which point the damage reads as sudden even though it wasn't. People aren't failing at attentiveness so much as failing at a kind of attentiveness plants specifically require: noticing something before it announces itself.

There's also a mismatch between what a plant is and what people buy it to be. Most people aren't acquiring a living organism with a light requirement and a native climate; they're acquiring a piece of decor that happens to be green. The pothos on the shelf is doing the same job as the throw pillow next to it. Once you buy something as an object rather than as a creature, you don't build the habits that keeping a creature requires. You don't ask what a fiddle leaf fig actually wants because you weren't really asking what anything wants — you were filling a corner.

And yet people keep doing it, keep replacing the casualties, which suggests the decor theory isn't the whole story either. There's something people want from having living things around that isn't really about horticultural success. A plant, even a struggling one, is evidence that a room is inhabited, that someone is trying to keep something going. It's a low-stakes way of practicing care — you can fail at it repeatedly without anyone getting hurt, without the kind of guilt that comes from neglecting a pet or a person. Killing a snake plant is almost a rite of passage rather than a real loss. That low cost of failure is probably part of the appeal: it lets people keep attempting the gesture of care without the gesture actually costing much when it goes wrong.

There's also plain optimism bias, the same instinct that makes people think this diet will be the one that sticks. Standing in the shop, holding a healthy specimen, it's easy to believe this time will be different — better light, more attention, a fresh start. The plant is a small bet on a slightly improved version of yourself, the one who remembers to check the soil. That the bet keeps losing doesn't do much to the appeal of making it again, because each new plant arrives with no memory of the last one's death. It's a clean slate in a pot.

And maybe there's something almost comforting in a relationship with something that will not reproach you. A dying plant doesn't get angry or disappointed. It just quietly leaves, and you're allowed to feel bad about it privately, buy another one, and try again — which is, in the end, a fairly gentle way to keep failing at something.
