The apple tree stood exactly on the property line, which was the whole problem. Bengt had measured it twice, once with a tape and once by pacing it out in boots, and both times the trunk came up four centimeters into his side.

"Four centimeters," said Karin, over the fence. "You want me to saw it down for four centimeters."

"I never said saw it down."

"You said the roots are cracking your patio."

"They are cracking my patio."

Bengt had photographs. He had brought his phone to the fence three times that week to show her the hairline fractures spreading from beneath the third and fourth flagstones, fractures he had traced with chalk so they would show up better in pictures. Karin had looked at the photographs each time and said the same thing: the patio was old, the ground froze and thawed every year, stone always cracked eventually.

"Not in this pattern," Bengt said. "This pattern follows the roots."

"You're not a geologist."

"I don't need to be a geologist to see a straight line."

The tree itself was old too, older than either of their houses, planted by whoever had farmed the land before it was cut into lots in the sixties. It produced apples that were small and a little sour, good for baking, not much else, and every September both of them ended up with more than they could use. Karin made a chutney her sister liked. Bengt let his rot in a bucket by the compost and felt vaguely guilty about it.

"I'm not asking you to cut it down," Bengt said again. "I'm asking who's responsible for the patio."

"It's your patio."

"It's your tree."

"It's nobody's tree, it's on the line."

This had been going on since July. Neither of them raised their voice, exactly, but the conversations had a shape now, a worn groove they both fell into without meaning to, like a song neither of them liked but knew all the words to. Bengt's wife had stopped coming to the fence with him. She said she found the whole thing tedious, and anyway she liked the tree, she liked how it looked from the kitchen window in May, white flowers and bees.

In August, Bengt called a tree service to ask what it would cost to have the roots on his side cut back, a partial root pruning, nothing drastic. The man who came out walked around the trunk, looked up into the canopy for a while, and said that pruning roots on one side of a tree that size could destabilize it enough that a strong wind might bring the whole thing down, and did Bengt want that liability instead.

Bengt did not call Karin to tell her this. He filled in the chalk lines again after a rain washed them out, and took another photograph, and put his phone away.

In September the apples came down early, a whole week of wind, and both of them were out with rakes on their respective sides, not speaking, working around the same trunk from opposite directions, the sound of rakes on stone the only thing passing between the two yards.
