The alarm had gone off at 11:40, and by 12:20 they were back at the station, alone, turning off lights.

"Kitchen," said Bengt, and Ylva went to check the stove, though nobody had used it. Habit. She looked anyway, ran a hand along the cold burner plates, then flicked the switch by the door.

The false alarm had come from a smoke detector in an apartment on Kungsgatan, triggered by a woman frying fish with the window closed. She'd apologized four times. Bengt had told her it was fine, better safe, and meant it, though his knees didn't agree at his age, going up two flights in full gear for nothing.

Now they moved through the station in the after-quiet that always followed a call, real or not. The truck sat in its bay, engine ticking as it cooled. Ylva coiled the spare hose that hadn't even been used, just habit again, checking it was ready.

"You want coffee before we lock up?" Bengt asked.

"No, I want to sleep."

He laughed, short. He was sixty-one and had been doing this since he was twenty-three, back when the station still had the old Bedford truck that overheated on hills. Ylva was twenty-nine and worked at the pharmacy during the day. Neither of them talked about why they did this, not tonight.

They checked the oxygen tanks were racked properly. Bengt noted in the log: false alarm, 23:41, no injuries, unit responded within 6 minutes. His handwriting had gotten smaller over the years, he'd noticed, more cramped, as if he was running out of room on some larger page.

"Erik still owe you the tarp?" Ylva asked, meaning the one from the barn fire in August.

"He'll bring it Thursday. Says Thursday every week."

"So never."

"So never," Bengt agreed.

They went through the equipment room together, not because it needed two people but because it was easier that way, one calling out and the other confirming. Axes, hung. Jaws of life, charged, green light on. Turnout gear back on its hooks in size order, though nobody had ever asked them to do it that way; it had just become the way.

Outside, the November air had a bite to it, and the gravel lot was empty except for Bengt's truck and Ylva's bike, leaning against the wall where she always left it. A dog barked somewhere down toward the lake.

Bengt pulled the bay door down, the chain rattling through its guide, and it landed with the flat sound it always made, no drama in it.

"Same time Thursday," he said, meaning the drill, not Erik's tarp.

"Same time," Ylva said, already swinging a leg over her bike.

He watched her ride off under the one streetlight that worked, then checked the door twice, the way he always did, though it had never once come open on its own in thirty-eight years. He got in his truck. The station sat dark behind him, waiting, the way it always was, for the next time someone's smoke detector caught something that wasn't a fire.
