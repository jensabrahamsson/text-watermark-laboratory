The Line

Mrs. Aslanovic hung her sheets first, always, because she woke at five and the courtyard was hers before it belonged to anyone else. White cotton, pegged at precise intervals, snapping faintly in the wind that came down from the hills. By the time the rest of the building stirred, her half of the shared line was already full, and everyone else had learned to work around her.

The line ran corner to corner across the courtyard, strung between a rusted hook on the Aslanovic side and a bent nail on the side belonging to the Petrossian family, who had lived there for eleven years without once fixing the nail. It was the only line for six flats. This had never been a formal arrangement. It had simply become true, the way certain facts about a building become true — nobody voted on it, nobody wrote it down, and yet everyone understood the order: Mrs. Aslanovic at dawn, then whoever got there next.

Dimitar from the second floor liked to hang his work shirts around nine, after his coffee. He ironed nothing, believed in the sun instead, and pegged his shirts by the collar so they'd dry into something like their proper shape. The Petrossians did laundry on Tuesdays and Fridays only, in enormous batches, and old Petrossian himself would come down in his cardigan to smoke while the children's clothes turned slowly in the breeze, arguing with no one about football.

Then there was the girl from the top flat, new that spring, who nobody had properly met. She hung her clothes at odd hours — sometimes midnight, sometimes noon — small dark things that the older women pretended not to notice and noticed completely. She used the far end of the line, the end nearest the wall, as if trying not to take up space that belonged to someone else.

One July the wind came up hard and unhooked the whole line at the Petrossian end. Everything fell — Mrs. Aslanovic's sheets, Dimitar's shirts, a child's yellow dress, the girl's things from the wall end — all of it into the dust and the cigarette ash and a puddle by the drain. For a moment nobody moved. Then Mrs. Aslanovic, of all people, was the first one out into the yard, gathering an armful of anything, not sorting whose was whose, just lifting fabric off the ground before it got worse. Dimitar came next, then Petrossian without his cardigan, then his wife, then the girl, barefoot, holding the fallen nail in her palm like she meant to hammer it back in with her hand.

They spent the better part of an hour rewashing things at the courtyard tap, wringing them out together over the drain, restringing the line with a proper knot this time, one that Dimitar tied because he'd once worked on boats. The order got scrambled that day. Sheets next to work shirts next to a child's dress next to the girl's small dark things, all drying side by side in no particular arrangement at all.

By the next morning, though, Mrs. Aslanovic was out at five again, and the line was hers before anyone else's, exactly as it had always been.
