Claude responded: Dear Margit,

Dear Margit,

I hope this finds you warmer than I am. It has been a week of weather such as I have not seen in some years, and I write now mostly to prove to myself, and to you, that the tower and I are both still standing.

It began last Tuesday with a low sky and a wind out of the northwest that would not settle. By Wednesday the gulls had stopped flying — you know it is bad when the gulls give up first, they are stubborn creatures and generally more sensible than I am. I battened the shutters on the seaward side and brought in enough coal and water for a fortnight, more from habit than real fear, though by Thursday I was glad of it.

Thursday night the real gale arrived. I have never heard the tower groan quite that way before. The whole structure seemed to breathe with each gust, a long shudder up through the stone and into the soles of my feet as I stood the watch. Spray was coming clean over the gallery rail, forty feet up, and freezing to the glass almost as it struck, so that by midnight I was chipping ice from the lantern room windows with the back of a spoon, the only tool at hand, cursing myself for never having brought up a proper scraper. The light did not fail, though — that much I am proud to report. Whatever else happens up here, the light does not fail.

Friday brought no relief, only more of the same, and I confess I did not sleep so much as doze in the chair by the stove, listening for any change in the wind's pitch that might mean trouble. A crate of supplies I had lashed on the lower gallery came loose in the night and I lost most of the tinned milk over the side, which I mourn more than I probably should. There is something about days like these that reduces a man's concerns to very small, very concrete things. Milk. Dry matches. Whether the door will hold.

By Saturday morning the wind had begun to back around to the west and drop, and by Sunday I could see the mainland again for the first time in four days, a grey smudge that looked more beautiful to me than it has any right to. I went out onto the gallery and just stood there a while, ears still ringing with the wind's absence, the way a room feels strange when a clock that has ticked for years finally stops.

I am well, Margit, truly — tired, and short on tinned milk, and my hands are raw from the cold, but well. The supply boat is due Tuesday if the swell allows it, and I have asked them to bring both milk and a proper ice scraper this time.

Do write and tell me how the children are keeping, and whether the harbour at home took much damage. Up here we measure storms only by what they take from us personally, and I have grown curious whether this one reached as far as you.

With all my love,
Your brother,
Elias
