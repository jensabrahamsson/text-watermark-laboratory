Tuesday Night, Fellowship Hall

Marguerite raised her hands at 7:04, four minutes late because the coffee urn had overflowed again, and the altos were still arguing about whether "Wade in the Water" needed a soloist or whether that was "showing off." She cut them off with a single clap.

"From the top. Measure one."

The piano stumbled in a half-beat ahead of everyone else. Gary, at eighty-one, had been playing since before most of them were born, and he still came in early on Tuesdays, as if the song owed him something and he intended to collect. The sopranos entered late to compensate. By measure four, the tenors — all three of them — had lost the thread entirely and were humming something that might have been the right key.

"Stop, stop." Marguerite tapped her folder against her palm. "Basses, you're rushing."

"We're not rushing, she's dragging," said Harold, not quietly enough.

Deb, at the piano bench beside Gary though she wasn't playing, sighed the sigh of a woman who had sat through eleven years of Tuesdays. She caught Marguerite's eye and gave the smallest shrug, the one that meant let it go, we have ninety minutes and a concert in six weeks.

They started again. This time the door banged open at measure nine and Priya came in shaking rain off her coat, mouthing sorry sorry sorry, and half the sopranos turned to watch her find a seat, and the half-turn was enough to derail the phrase entirely, so that "wade in the water" came out as a ragged, staggered thing, five different tempos converging on the same six words and missing.

"Okay." Marguerite set down her folder. "Let's talk about the water."

Nobody talked about the water. Instead Harold wanted to know if they were doing risers this year, and Deb reminded everyone about the potluck sign-up sheet by the coffee urn, the one currently soaked through, and Gary played a stray arpeggio that turned out to be the beginning of "Shenandoah," which nobody had asked for, and for a moment three sopranos started singing it anyway, half out of muscle memory, half out of mercy, because at least it was something they all knew.

Marguerite let them. She sat down on the front pew, folder in her lap, and watched Gary find the chords by feel, watched Priya finally get her coat off and slide in next to a bass who scooted over without looking, watched the altos, still arguing, harmonize anyway, underneath the argument, the way people do when the sound is more familiar than the disagreement.

It wasn't the piece. It wasn't even close to the piece.

At 8:31, Marguerite stood, said "same time Thursday," and began stacking chairs. Gary was still at the piano, working out the bridge of something nobody had assigned him. Deb mopped at the coffee urn with a handful of napkins. Harold and the other bass were arguing again, amicably, about risers.

Outside, the rain had let up. The hall emptied slowly, the way it always did, nobody quite ready to be the last one out, and nobody quite the first one gone either.
