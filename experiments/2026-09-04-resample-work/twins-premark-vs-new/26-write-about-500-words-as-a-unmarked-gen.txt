The last bell always leaves a residue in the air, a kind of pressure drop, and I give it ten minutes before I wheel the cart out of the closet by the gym. Ten minutes so the stragglers clear out — the ones waiting on late buses, the kid whose mom is always twenty minutes behind schedule, sitting on the concrete steps with a backpack too big for him.

Room 114 first, because Mrs. Halvorsen's fourth graders eat like a hurricane came through. Under the desks: a granola bar wrapper folded into a tight little square, three broken crayons, a single sock. I don't ask about the sock. There's always a sock. I stack the chairs on the desks, four legs up, and run the dust mop in long strips, pushing the debris toward the door in a gray commet's tail of eraser dust and dropped Cheerios.

The whiteboard trays have chalk dust even though nobody uses chalk anymore — it's just marker sediment, dried flecks that fall when you drag the eraser. I wipe the boards last period's lesson is still half-visible under my rag: long division, a fraction with a slash through it, someone's initials in the corner in different handwriting than the teacher's.

The bathrooms are the real work. Paper towels missed the bin and landed in a wet clump by the sink. One stall door won't latch — I've told maintenance twice. I mop in figure eights, starting from the far wall so I don't trap myself. The smell of the industrial cleaner, something like pine crossed with bleach, follows me down the hall afterward, clinging to my sleeves.

The cafeteria takes the longest. Tables wiped down twice — once for the crumbs, once because the first pass just smears them. Under table six there's a wad of gum, old, already gray, and I have to get the scraper for it. Someone's left a retainer in a napkin on the tray return, which happens more than you'd think. I bag it, tag it, put it in the lost-and-found bin behind the office, where it'll sit next to eleven single gloves and a trombone mouthpiece nobody's claimed since October.

By six the building is quiet in a specific way — not silent, because the heating system ticks and the vending machine in the teacher's lounge hums, but empty of the particular noise kids make, which isn't noise exactly, more like a pressure. Without it the hallways feel bigger than they are during the day.

I do the gym floor last, because it takes longest to dry and nobody's walking on it again tonight. Big slow passes with the buffer, the smell of floor wax rising up warm. The hoops are up, nets still. Someone left a shoe by the bleachers — just one, laces still tied, like they stepped right out of it.

I lock the east doors, then the west, check the boiler room, and head out through the loading dock where my truck is the only one left in the lot. The security light kicks on. Tomorrow it starts over.
