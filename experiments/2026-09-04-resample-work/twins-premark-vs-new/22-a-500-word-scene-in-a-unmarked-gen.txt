Rain hammered the tin awning outside, and every few seconds the fluorescent light over the folding table flickered like it might give up entirely. Mira sat cross-legged on top of a dryer, her sneakers dangling, watching the machine beside her tumble a single blue blanket in slow, hypnotic loops.

The bell over the door jingled. A man came in soaked through, shaking water off a garbage bag he'd apparently been using as a raincoat.

"Didn't think anyone else would be crazy enough," he said, nodding at the storm behind him.

"I have exactly one clean pair of socks left," Mira said. "Desperation beats crazy."

He laughed, dragging a duffel bag to the machine two down from hers. Quarters clinked into the slot. Outside, thunder rolled somewhere over the hills, and the lights dipped again.

"You always talk to strangers in laundromats?" he asked.

"Only during natural disasters. Otherwise it's considered rude."

"Good policy." He sat down across from her, on the plastic bench bolted to the wall, elbows on his knees. "Ben."

"Mira."

The dryer beside her chimed and stopped. She didn't move to open it.

"That's not gonna fold itself," Ben said.

"It's one blanket. I'm savoring the moment before I have to be a responsible adult again."

He smiled at that, looked toward the window, where the parking lot had become a shallow lake, reflecting the neon OPEN sign in long red streaks. "Storm's supposed to get worse before it lets up."

"Story of most things," Mira said, and immediately felt like it sounded more profound than she meant it to. She hopped down off the dryer to cover the awkwardness, opened the door, and pulled out the blanket — warm, soft, smelling like fabric softener and heat.

"You always narrate your laundry like it's a metaphor?"

"Only during natural disasters."

He laughed again, and for a second the only sounds were the rain drumming the roof, his own machine starting its cycle, water sluicing off the awning outside in ropes.

"I used to hate this place," Ben said, apropos of nothing. "Came here every week after my divorce. Hated everything about it. The smell, the buzzing lights, the guy who always left lint everywhere."

"And now?"

He considered it. "Now it's just where I do my laundry."

Mira sat back down, blanket in her lap, and studied him for a second — the way you study someone you've just decided isn't dangerous, isn't boring, might actually be worth the twenty minutes a dryer cycle buys you.

"That's either really sad or really hopeful," she said. "I can't tell which."

"Maybe it's just Tuesday," Ben said.

Lightning lit the windows white for half a second. Neither of them flinched. The rain kept coming down like it had nowhere else to be, and neither, for the moment, did they.
