The lobby smells like old carpet and coffee that's been sitting since noon. A single lamp burns behind the desk, throwing a small yellow circle over a guestbook, a stapler, and a bowl of mints nobody touches. This is where Marek spends his nights, from eleven until seven, in a chair that squeaks whenever he shifts his weight.

He's not old, maybe forty, but the job has given him the stillness of someone older. He sits with a paperback open but rarely reads more than a page an hour. Mostly he watches the door, not because he expects trouble, but because watching the door is the job. Somewhere between one and two in the morning, the hotel goes fully quiet — no elevator hum, no distant television through the walls — and that's when the hours start to stretch. He's learned to like the stretching. It's the one part of the day that belongs to him.

The hotel isn't much. Thirty-one rooms, no restaurant, a breakfast that's really just a table with a kettle and some bread. The carpet in the hallway has a worn path down the middle from decades of suitcases. The elevator groans like it's complaining about its job too. But the price is right, and that brings in truck drivers passing through, families driving somewhere cheaper the next morning, a couple of regulars who stay by the week because it's easier than finding an apartment.

Marek knows most of them by their habits rather than their names. The trucker in 14 always wants a six a.m. wake-up call and always answers the phone on the first ring, wide awake before it even finishes ringing. The woman in 22 comes down around midnight for ice, wrapped in a hotel bathrobe, and says almost nothing except thank you. He doesn't ask questions. It isn't that kind of place, and he isn't that kind of receptionist.

The desk phone rings sometimes — a wrong room number, a taxi confirmation, once in a while someone who just wants to hear a voice and pretends they need directions to a gas station that's already closed. He gives the directions anyway. It costs him nothing and the night is long.

Around three, he does his walk. Down the hall, past the ice machine that rattles louder than it should, out to check that the side door hasn't been propped open by someone sneaking a smoke. The parking lot is mostly empty cars under a flickering light. He stands there for a minute, looking at nothing in particular, feeling the cold on his hands, then goes back inside.

By five the light outside starts to turn from black to a dull gray, and he makes the first pot of coffee for the guests who'll want it soon. The wake-up calls begin. Doors open and close. Suitcase wheels bump down the hallway. Cars start in the lot. The day receptionist arrives at seven, tired-eyed the way night workers always are with each other, and they trade a few words about nothing important. Then Marek walks out into the morning, the town waking up around him, and heads home to sleep through the daylight.
