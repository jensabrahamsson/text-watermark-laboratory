The wind had been building since morning, and by the time Elsa reached the dock, the water in the sound had gone from gray-blue to a mean, chopped white. She stood under the little tin roof of the waiting shelter with her bag between her feet and watched the flag on the pole snap sideways, hard, like someone shaking out a sheet.

There were six of them waiting. An old man with a dog that kept turning circles and lying back down. Two teenagers sharing earbuds, one strand each. A woman with grocery bags who checked her phone every few minutes like the ferry might answer if she asked nicely enough. Elsa didn't check hers. She could see the boat itself, out past the point, rocking at its mooring, not coming.

A man in an orange jacket came out of the little office and stood in the doorway, hands in his pockets, looking at the water the way you'd look at a dog that might or might not bite. "Not yet," he said, to nobody and everybody. "Captain doesn't like the gusts through the gap there." He nodded toward the narrow stretch between the two islands, where the wind funneled and the water piled up into short, ugly waves that didn't roll so much as slap.

Elsa had lived here long enough to know this waiting wasn't really about a schedule. It was about a boat that held maybe forty people and no room for a bad call. She'd heard stories — not this captain, a different one, years back — of a crossing that went sideways in a gust and left everyone soaked and furious and, worse, scared. So she didn't mind the wait, not exactly. She minded the cold coming up through her boots and the ache of standing still.

The dog gave up circling and pressed itself against the old man's leg. The teenagers laughed at something on one phone. The woman with the groceries sighed loud enough for the shelter to hear, then apologized to no one in particular, then sighed again anyway.

Somewhere around the forty-minute mark the wind eased. Not stopped — eased, the way a held breath lets out slow. The flag still moved but it stopped snapping. The man in the orange jacket came back out, looked at the gap between the islands, looked at his own hands like he was counting something, and said, "Alright. She's coming in."

You could feel the little group loosen. The dog stood up. The teenagers pulled their earbuds out. The boat came around the point smaller than Elsa remembered, always smaller than she remembered, white hull streaked with salt, rocking but steady, steadier than the water around it looked. It bumped the dock twice before the ropes went on, and the man in orange caught the line like he'd done it ten thousand times, because he had.

They filed on without much talking. Elsa took a seat near the back, by the window, and watched the dock pull away, the shelter, the flag now hanging almost straight down, spent. The crossing itself was short. It always was. It was the waiting that had any size to it.
