The basement smelled like every church basement smells: coffee that had been reheated too many times, and the particular mustiness of folding chairs that live in closets. Eleven card tables were set up in two rows under fluorescent lights, and by seven-fifteen every Tuesday, ten of them were occupied.

Harriet Voss ran the club, though nobody had ever voted her into the position. She was eighty-one, played a rigid Caro-Kann as Black and refused all draws on principle, even in lost positions. "A draw is an agreement to stop trying," she said, and nobody had successfully argued otherwise in six years.

Marcus came every week with his son, Theo, who was nine and had learned the knight's move before he could reliably tie his shoes. Theo played fast, too fast, slapping pieces down with the confidence of someone who hadn't yet lost enough to be afraid of it. He beat a retired postal worker named Gene three weeks running, and Gene had taken to muttering about the Italian Game under his breath for a full day afterward like it was a grudge he was nursing.

There was a folding table in the corner nobody used, because the leg was bent and it wobbled when you moved a piece, and everyone had agreed without discussing it that table nine was cursed. Dale sat there anyway, alone, working through games from a book with a cracked spine, moving pieces for both sides. He didn't come for the company. He came because the church was three blocks from his apartment and the coffee was free and something about the particular quiet of people thinking hard, together, without needing to talk, suited him.

The pastor never came down on Tuesdays but he'd leave the side door unlocked and the thermostat up, and once a year, around Christmas, he'd stick his head in and say something about fellowship and everyone would nod without really listening because they were all mid-game.

In October, a woman named Priya showed up for the first time, sat down across from Harriet without asking whether the seat was taken, and played the only game that ended in a draw all year. Harriet stared at the board for a long time afterward, resetting the pieces slower than usual, and didn't offer a rematch, which everyone understood to be the highest compliment she was capable of giving.

Theo lost his first game to his father that December, a real loss, no let-up, and cried for exactly four minutes in the hallway by the water fountain before coming back in and asking for the moves to be gone over again from the start.

Gene finally beat Theo in February with the Italian Game he'd been muttering about, and did not gloat, which everyone found more unsettling than if he had.

The folding chairs got stacked at nine, the coffee pot got unplugged, and the lights went off in the particular order they always went off in, back row first, then front, then the one by the stairs, and the basement sat there in the dark until Tuesday came around again.
