I've killed two starters. Not dramatically—no mold, no smell of death, just a slow fade into flatness that I mistook for patience for way too long.

The first time, I did everything the blog told me. Flour, water, glass jar, rubber band around the outside to mark the rise. Day three it bubbled and I got that little jolt of pride you get when a thing you made starts behaving like it's alive. Day seven it stopped rising past the band. I kept feeding it anyway, convinced that "trust the process" applied here the way it applies to everything else, that if I just waited long enough the yeast would sort itself out. It didn't. What I understand now is that a starter isn't a single organism you're nursing along—it's a competition. Wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria fighting for the same flour, and in the early days that fight can go either way depending on temperature, on how much you're diluting it each feed, on whether your kitchen is 19 degrees or 24. Mine was cold and I was underfeeding it relative to that cold, so the bacteria won and the whole thing just went sour and inert. A dead starter doesn't announce itself. It just quietly stops being a leavening agent and becomes a jar of sour paste you're feeding out of stubbornness.

The second attempt I got further. Starter alive, active, doubling in six hours, the whole performance. I made the dough, did my stretch and folds, felt smug about the gluten development. Then I proofed it exactly as long as the recipe said—not as long as the dough said—and baked a loaf that came out of the oven looking correct and cut open looking like wet clay. Dense, gummy, no oven spring worth mentioning. That one hurt more, honestly, because everything upstream had gone right. The failure was in trusting a clock instead of the dough itself. Bulk fermentation isn't a duration, it's a state—the dough should feel aerated, jiggly, visibly larger, with that slight domed tension. Mine hadn't gotten there yet when I moved it to the fridge, and cold retard doesn't fix underproofed dough, it just pauses it exactly where it was.

What both failures taught me, in a slower and more annoying way than any recipe ever could, is that sourdough punishes people who want a formula. It rewards people who are willing to poke the dough, smell the starter, notice that today's dough behaves differently than last week's because the room is two degrees warmer. Every step is a judgment call disguised as a rule. "Feed when doubled" assumes you know what doubled looks like for your starter, in your jar, at your temperature. "Bake until golden" assumes you've learned what your oven does to color versus what it does to the crumb inside.

I haven't attempted a third starter yet. Partly fear, partly the honest recognition that I wasn't actually paying attention the first two times—I was following instructions and calling it understanding.
