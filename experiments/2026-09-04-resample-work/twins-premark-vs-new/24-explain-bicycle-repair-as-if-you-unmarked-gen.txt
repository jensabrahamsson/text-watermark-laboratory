Alright, listen — Gunnar next door fixed bikes for forty years and taught me everything in about six irritated afternoons, mostly by grabbing tools out of my hand and muttering "no, not like that." Here's what stuck.

First thing he ever said: "You don't need the manual, you need your hands." He was right, mostly, except for brake cable tension, where I definitely needed the manual and he definitely lied to me about it.

Flat tires. He wouldn't let me use tire levers at first — "fingers, use your fingers, you'll break something with those plastic sticks" — until I actually did try with fingers, failed for twenty minutes, and he sighed and handed me the levers anyway. Pop one side of the tire off the rim, work around it, don't yank. Pull the tube out, find the hole — he'd just dunk the whole thing in a bucket of water and watch for bubbles instead of explaining anything. Patch it or swap the tube, your call, he thought patches were "for people with time," which was rich coming from a retired man.

Brakes. He hated explaining brakes because he said if you got them wrong you'd know immediately, so why waste breath. Loosen the pinch bolt, pull the cable tight by hand — "tighter, no, TIGHTER, until the pads almost touch when you squeeze" — then bolt it down. Squeeze the lever a few times, check the pads hit the rim evenly, not rubbing on one side. If they rub, it's the little barrel adjuster near the lever, twist it, stop asking him which direction, "you'll figure it out by listening to it scrape."

Chains. This is where he got genuinely impatient. A chain that's slipping or skipping is almost always just stretched — worn out — and he'd size that up in two seconds by eyeballing it, no tool, which infuriated me for months until I bought a chain checker and it turned out he was right every single time. If it's stretched, replace it, don't oil it and hope. If it's just dry and noisy, oil it, wipe off the excess — "you're lubing the chain, not marinating it" — and stop putting so much on.

Gears not shifting right. Barrel adjuster again, usually. Little turns, test, little turns, test. He refused to let me touch the derailleur limit screws unless the chain was actually falling off into the spokes, because "those aren't for tuning, those are for emergencies, stop fiddling."

General philosophy, if you can call it that: he thought most bike problems were caused by people overthinking simple mechanical things. Nothing on a bike is mysterious, he said — it's cables, springs, and friction, that's the whole machine. If something's wrong, look at it, squeeze it, spin it, and the problem usually announces itself. He was suspicious of anyone who needed a diagram to understand a lever.

Honestly the biggest thing he taught me wasn't technique, it was confidence — the assumption that you're allowed to just try it, and if you get it wrong, you'll hear or feel it immediately and can fix that too. Which, credit where due, is basically true for bikes. Cars, he warned me, are a different and much angrier story.
