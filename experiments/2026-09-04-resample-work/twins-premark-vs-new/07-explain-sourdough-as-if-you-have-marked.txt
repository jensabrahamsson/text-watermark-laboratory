I've tried sourdough twice now, and both times I convinced myself the failure was a technique problem when it was actually a patience problem.

The first attempt started with too much confidence. I fed the starter for four days, saw bubbles, decided that meant readiness, and baked. The bread came out dense, almost gummy near the crust, with a sour smell that was more vinegar than tang. I blamed the flour. I blamed my oven. What actually happened is that four days isn't enough for a starter to build the kind of microbial balance that gives bread lift and flavor at the same time. Bubbles mean activity, not maturity. I wanted a shortcut and the dough gave me exactly what a shortcut deserves.

The second attempt I overcorrected. I waited two weeks, fed the starter religiously, twice a day, and got paranoid about temperature. I moved it around the kitchen chasing an imaginary ideal warmth. Then on baking day I did the float test, watched a small blob of starter sink slightly, and panicked, adding more flour to "strengthen" the dough right before shaping. That's not how it works. Adding flour late doesn't rescue a weak starter, it just changes the hydration and throws off everything else. The crumb came out tight and uneven, dense at the bottom, with big irregular holes near the top where gas had pooled instead of distributing. Visually it looked like a bread that had given up halfway through proofing.

What I've come to understand, mostly from being wrong twice, is that sourdough punishes impatience and also punishes overmanagement. The starter doesn't care how attentive you are if you're intervening at the wrong moments. It's less like a recipe and more like tending something that has its own schedule, and your job is mostly to notice, not to fix. The float test isn't a pass/fail exam, it's one data point. Dough that feels slack and spreads instead of holding shape is telling you it needs more time, not more flour. A crust that browns too fast but the inside stays raw usually means the oven's too hot or the loaf went in too soon, not that something is fundamentally broken.

I think the real failure both times was treating sourdough like a process with steps I could execute correctly, rather than a slow negotiation with something alive. Bakers who succeed at this seem to develop a kind of intuition for what the dough is doing, built from watching it fail a lot first. I haven't gotten there yet. My starter is currently three weeks old, sitting quietly in a jar, and I'm resisting the urge to do anything clever to it. Next time I bake, I plan to do less, not more. Less flour improvisation, less temperature chasing, less trusting a single test as the whole answer. If it fails a third time, at least I'll know it wasn't for lack of trying to control it.
