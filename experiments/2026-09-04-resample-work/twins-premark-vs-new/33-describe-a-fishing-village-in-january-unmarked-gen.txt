The harbor freezes at the edges first, a thin skin of ice creeping out from the pilings where the water sits still. By eight in the morning it's still dark enough that the streetlamps stay on, throwing orange light on the wet cobbles. Nobody's in a hurry. The fishermen who go out at all leave before that light comes on, their boats' engines coughing and settling into a low chug that carries across the water longer than it should, given how cold the air is.

Most of the boats don't move. They sit tied up two and three deep against the quay, tarps pulled over the open decks, ropes gone stiff. Snow doesn't stick to them for long — the salt in the air keeps it soft and grey, more like slush by noon. A few nets hang over racks behind the fish houses, frozen into their own shapes, and nobody's bothered to take them down because there's nothing to mend them for yet.

The village itself pulls in on itself in January. Shutters that stay open all summer are latched now. The bakery keeps its hours short, closing before three, and the smell of bread only reaches halfway down the main street before the cold eats it. Cats that sleep outside all summer have moved into the boat sheds, curled up on old coils of rope. You see their tracks in the frost on the planks before you see the cats.

The light itself is stingy. It comes up late, sits low and flat over the water for a few hours — not enough to warm anything, just enough to turn the sea the color of a knife blade — and then it's gone again by four. In between, people move between the co-op, the chandlery, and home, not lingering. The chandlery is the one place that stays properly warm; there's a wood stove in the back and the old men who don't fish anymore sit near it most days, arguing about prices or ice or nothing in particular. The talk isn't for show. Nobody's performing for a visitor because there aren't any.

Down by the fish auction hall, the concrete floor stays wet and cold no matter what, and what little catch comes in — cod mostly, some herring — gets weighed fast so everyone can get back inside. The gulls still show up for it, fewer than in summer, more ragged-looking, fighting over guts with a kind of tired persistence.

At night the harbor goes quiet in a way that feels almost solid. You can hear a halyard slapping against a mast three boats down. A generator running somewhere. Ice groaning faintly where it's formed against the hulls. The houses climbing the hill above the water show lit windows here and there, curtains drawn, chimneys putting out smoke that doesn't rise so much as hang. Nobody's outside unless they have to be. The whole place seems to be waiting, not dramatically, just practically — the way you wait for a season to pass because there's no shortcut through it, only the slow business of getting to the other side.
