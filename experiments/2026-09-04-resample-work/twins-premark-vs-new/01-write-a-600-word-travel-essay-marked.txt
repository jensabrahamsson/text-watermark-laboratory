The bus comes down the last hill with its engine off, coasting, and the driver doesn't bother braking until the harbour opens up below. Roofs first, red and black, then the water between them, flat and grey-gold with the sun already low. A few boats sit tilted in their moorings where the tide has gone out. Someone has hung washing on a line strung between two posts by the fish sheds, and it hangs there without moving.

The bus stops outside a shuttered kiosk. Nobody else gets off. The driver says something in a dialect I don't quite catch, points down the road, and pulls away before I've fully understood him, leaving me with my bag and the smell of diesel fading into salt and tar.

The town is built on a slope, houses stacked close, and the streets between them are narrow enough that two people have to turn sideways to pass. Most of the shutters are already down for the evening. A grocer is sweeping the step outside his shop, unhurried, dragging a few stray onion skins into a pile. He nods without looking up properly.

Down at the water the light changes by the minute. It's the kind of dusk that doesn't announce itself, just arrives in increments — the color leaving the sky sideways rather than fading straight down. The boats knock gently against the dock, wood on wood, rope creaking where it's pulled taut and slack again. An old man sits on an upturned crate mending a net, working by feel more than sight now, and doesn't look up when I walk past.

There's a bar with three tables outside, all empty, and a chalkboard with a menu half wiped clean. I sit anyway. The owner comes out in an apron that's seen a full day's work, wipes the table though it doesn't need it, and asks what I want without much ceremony. I order the fish because it's the only word on the board I fully recognize. He nods and disappears back inside, and for a while I'm alone with the sound of the water and someone's radio playing faintly from an upstairs window, a station with more static than song.

The streetlamps come on one at a time, not all together, as if someone is walking the length of the harbour switching them on by hand. Maybe someone is. The light they throw is orange and low, pooling on the cobbles, leaving the spaces between lamps dark enough that people passing through them become shapes before they become faces again.

The fish arrives with bread and a wedge of lemon, nothing else, and it's better than it has any right to be for a place with three empty tables. While I eat, a couple of fishermen come up from the boats, boots heavy on the stone, talking in short sentences that carry easily in the still air though I can't follow the words. They go into a house near the dock and a door closes and a light comes on behind a curtain.

By the time I've finished eating, the water has gone black except where the lamps reach it, a few broken lines of orange sitting on the surface and trembling slightly with each small movement of the boats. The day's heat is gone from the stone under the table. Somewhere behind me a shutter is pulled down and latched. I sit a while longer, in no hurry now, watching the harbour settle into whatever it becomes after dark.
