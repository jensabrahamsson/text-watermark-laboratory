Claude responded: The light goes first.

The light goes first. Not the overheads — those stay flat and white until the very end — but the low afternoon sun that's been sliding across the tile floor all day, shortening now, pulling back toward the door like it wants out before anyone else does.

Marta wipes down the display case glass, and where her cloth passes, sugar residue and thumbprints disappear, then reappear an hour ago in her memory, from a kid who pressed his palm flat against the case to point at the éclairs. The case itself is thinning out. Not empty — never quite empty, not with an hour left — but the trays show more stainless steel now, gaps where the good stuff went first. Three croissants left, curling slightly at the tips, the butter in them gone slack and heavy rather than crisp. A single slice of olive bread, cut wrong, too thick, sitting alone.

The smell has changed too. Earlier it was yeast and heat, that thick warm push of it every time the oven door opened. Now it's cooling sugar, faintly burnt on one tray somebody forgot at the back, and the bleach smell starting to creep in from the kitchen where Dev has already begun scrubbing the big mixer bowl, water running, metal clanking against the steel sink.

A last customer comes in at 5:40. The bell above the door sounds thinner than it did at eight this morning — or maybe that's just the ear getting tired of it. She asks what's left, and Marta gestures at the case without needing to look, already knowing: sourdough, half a walnut loaf, the croissants nobody wants at this hour, a few macarons that have gone slightly soft in the humidity. The customer takes the walnut loaf. The register drawer sticks a little when it opens, the way it always does after a long day, and Marta has to knock the side of it with her knuckle.

Outside, the street is doing its own version of closing time — shop grates coming down two doors over, a delivery van idling, someone's dog straining at a leash toward the bakery's open door, nose lifted at whatever's still in the air. Inside, chairs come up onto tables, four legs to the sky, and the mop bucket gets dragged out from the closet with that particular wheeled squeak.

Dev turns off the proofer first, then the second oven, and the kitchen goes quiet in stages, like something settling. The last thing to go is the front lights, and for a second before that happens, the bakery looks almost like it did before opening — cleaner, emptier, waiting. Marta locks the door, tests the handle twice out of habit, and stands there a moment on the sidewalk, keys in hand, while the smell of the place follows her out into the cooling evening air and then, slowly, gives up and lets go.
