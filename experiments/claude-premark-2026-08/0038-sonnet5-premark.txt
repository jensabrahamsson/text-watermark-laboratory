The hut sits low against the slope, its timber walls the color of wet slate. Fog has been moving through the valley since noon, thick enough that the ridge line above has disappeared entirely. You can hear water somewhere — a stream, maybe, or just runoff — but you can't see it. Sound carries strangely in this weather, muffled and close at the same time.

The door is heavier than it looks, swollen slightly from years of damp. It scrapes against the stone threshold when you push it open. Inside, the air changes immediately: dry, close, smelling of woodsmoke that's soaked into the beams over decades, plus something resinous from the pine shelving, and under that, the faint mineral smell of the stove's cold ash.

Someone left the shutters half-open on the north side, and gray light comes through in a flat, even wash — no shadows, no direction to it. The fog outside presses against the small windowpanes, and every few minutes a thicker patch drifts past, dimming the room further before it clears again.

A long table runs down the center, scarred with initials and dates carved by hikers over the years. Benches on either side, worn smooth in the middle where people have sat. In the corner, a cast-iron stove, cold now, with a stack of split wood beside it — some of it damp, the bark dark and swollen. A tin cup hangs from a nail near the door. Someone's wool socks are draped over a rope strung between two beams, forgotten or drying, it's hard to tell.

Outside, the fog doesn't move like wind-blown cloud; it seeps. It comes up from below in slow pulses, swallowing the nearest trees first — their outlines going soft, then gone — before reaching the hut itself. When it passes directly over, the world outside the window turns uniformly white-gray, and you lose all sense of distance. A minute later it thins, and you can make out the shape of a boulder twenty meters off, water beaded on its surface, before the next wave rolls through.

The cold comes in around the door frame and through gaps in the floorboards, not sharp but persistent, the kind that settles into your hands if you sit still too long. Your jacket, still damp from the trail, is starting to steam faintly where it hangs near the unlit stove. Somewhere outside, a bird calls twice, sharp and short, and then nothing — the fog seems to absorb the silence afterward rather than let it ring.

There's a logbook on the table, its cover soft and curling at the edges. Pages of names, dates, brief notes: reached the summit before the clouds came in, stayed the night, rain all evening, saw nothing above 1400m. You could add your own line. The pen beside it has left a faint groove in the wood from where past hands rested while thinking of what to write.

Outside, the fog keeps moving, indifferent, and the ridge stays hidden.
