The pool closed for the season sometime in late September, though "closed" undersells it—the water's still in there, just left to its own devices now. Nobody drained it. That would cost money, and refilling it in spring would cost money too, so it sits, going slowly green under a tarp that's come loose at one corner and flaps a little whenever the wind picks up.

I used to walk past it most days when I still had the dog, and there's something about a municipal pool in winter that I never got tired of looking at. In summer it's just infrastructure—loud, chlorinated, full of kids doing cannonballs and lifeguards squinting at nothing. But strip away the season and the people and you're left with this strange concrete basin, all function and no purpose, like a stage after the play has closed and they forgot to strike the set.

The diving board is the part that gets me most. Someone unbolts the ladder each autumn so nobody's tempted to climb up and do something stupid on the ice, but the board itself stays, jutting out over water that's now more slush than liquid some mornings. It looks less like sporting equipment and more like a diving board from a dream, the kind that doesn't lead anywhere useful, just out over grey water toward nothing in particular.

There's a chain-link fence around the whole complex, and somebody—kids, probably, or maybe just one particularly committed teenager—has bent back a section of it near the changing rooms. I don't think anyone's actually getting in and swimming. It's more that the fence exists as a formality now, the way "no trespassing" signs exist on land nobody's watching. The pool isn't dangerous exactly, or not more dangerous than any other body of half-frozen water, but it has that quality of official abandonment that makes it feel both forbidden and pointless to forbid.

What I keep coming back to is the smell, or the memory of it. Chlorine has a way of staying in a place even when the crowds don't. Walk close enough on a still day and you can still catch a faint chemical trace under the smell of wet leaves and cold stone, like the ghost of a hundred summer afternoons. It's the only sign that this was ever a place people wanted to be.

The lifeguard chair is still up too, empty, a little tilted from years of weather, facing out over water nobody's swimming in. I think about that chair more than I probably should—it seems to sum up the whole situation, this posture of readiness and attention pointed at absolutely nothing. Somewhere there's a budget line for reopening it in May, a maintenance schedule, a plan. But for now it's just a rectangle of cold water doing nothing, watched over by no one, waiting out the months until it matters again.
