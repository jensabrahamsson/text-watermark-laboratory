The bell above the door doesn't ring anymore — I taped the clapper last winter when the sound started grating on me by hour nine. I flip the sign, and it swings a little anyway, out of habit.

Start with the register. Count the drawer twice because the first count never matches what the tape says, and I've stopped caring why. Coins go in the pouch, bills get banded, the float goes back in the same manila envelope that's been folded so many times it's gone soft as cloth itself. I write the number on a Post-it and stick it to the inside of the drawer. Tomorrow-me will thank today-me, or won't notice either way.

The racks are a mess from a Saturday crowd that pawed through everything and bought almost nothing. I go down the line, shoulder by shoulder, turning hangers the right way — hook facing left, always left, so tomorrow I can tell at a glance what's been touched. A flannel has slid off its hanger entirely and I find it balled up under the shoe rack like something that gave up. I shake it out, fold it against my forearm the way I've folded ten thousand shirts by now, hands doing it without me.

The dressing room curtain gets pulled back and I check the corners for stray pins, forgotten price tags, the occasional earring somebody's boyfriend was told to hold and then set down and forgot. Tonight it's just a receipt from a coffee place three blocks over and a single black sock, which goes in the lost-and-found box that is really just a box of things nobody will ever come back for.

I wipe down the counter with the blue cloth that used to be white. The glass case with the vintage belt buckles gets a once-over too, though nobody's bought one of those in a month. I keep them anyway. Somebody will want a brass buckle shaped like a longhorn someday, and when they do, I'll have it.

Outside, I bring in the rolling rack from the sidewalk — the dollar bin, sun-faded T-shirts and a windbreaker nobody wanted even at that price. My arms know the weight of it before I lift it. Lock the wheels against the wall.

Lights off in sections: first the fitting rooms, then the back wall of coats, then the front, so the window mannequins are the last thing lit, standing there in outfits I put together this morning and already don't remember choosing. I stand for a second and look at the shop from the outside through the glass, the way a customer would. It looks fine. It looks like a place that's fine.

Keys in the left pocket, always the left, so I don't pat myself down like an idiot in the cold. Down goes the metal gate, the rattle of it the only loud thing left in the whole block. I lock it, tug it once to be sure, and start walking, tired in that ordinary way, the kind that just means the day happened and now it's over.
