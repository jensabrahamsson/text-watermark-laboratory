The panes on the south wall have gone opaque with algae, a green so dark it reads as black until the sun comes around at four and lights it up like a bruise. Someone propped the door open with a paint can in June and never came back to check on it. The paint can is still there, rusted at the seam, half full of rainwater and mosquito larvae.

Most of the tomato cages have collapsed under their own weight, or under the weight of the plants that used to fill them, and now lie on their sides like the ribs of something. A few stalks push up anyway, pale and leggy, reaching for the gap in the glass where a pane cracked and was never replaced. They will not fruit. There isn't enough light reaching the beds now, not since the west-facing panels fogged over from the inside, moisture trapped between two layers of glass with nowhere to go.

The irrigation system still runs, on a timer someone set years ago and forgot. Twice a day it hisses on and wets the same six square feet of dead soil, the nozzle rusted into position, aimed at nothing that needs it. Everything around that spot is dry and cracked like a lakebed. Everything under it is black with rot.

A row of herb pots along the east rail has done better than it should have, thyme and a woody, half-feral rosemary that's colonized three neighboring pots and started climbing the rail itself. Nobody planted it there on purpose anymore; it just kept going. This is the only part of the greenhouse that looks alive rather than abandoned, and it looks alive in a way that seems slightly hostile, like it knows something the rest of the space doesn't.

The far corner holds a stack of black plastic trays, seed trays, still labeled in fading marker — basil, chard, something illegible, dated for a spring that came and went without anyone sowing anything in them. Dust has settled into the marker lines. A folding chair sits near the trays, angled toward the view of the city, as though someone used to sit there and watch the light change and then stopped.

Birds get in through the cracked pane and can't always find their way back out. There are feathers under the workbench, and a small collection of bones picked clean by something else that also got in and also didn't leave. The workbench itself holds a trowel gone orange with rust, a pair of gloves stiffened into the shape of hands that aren't there anymore, a coffee mug with a skin of green mold across what's left in the bottom.

From the street the greenhouse still looks intact. Glass box, black frame, the suggestion of green through the fogged panels. It reads as a feature of the building, an amenity, a selling point. It is only up close, only inside, that the arithmetic of neglect becomes visible — how much stopped, how fast, and how little of it anyone noticed at the time.
