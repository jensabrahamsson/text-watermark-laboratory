He'd wave you over from the driveway without looking up. "You gonna stand there or hold the wheel."

That was the whole introduction. No names for tools, no this-is-a-cone-wrench speech. You learned a Phillips from a flathead by getting handed the wrong one and hearing him sigh through his nose.

The chain always came first, because it was always the problem, whether it was the actual problem or not. He'd grab it between thumb and finger, flex it sideways, say "see how it moves," and you wouldn't see anything, not the first dozen times. Eventually you did. A stiff link feels different once you know what loose ones feel like, but nobody can just tell you that. You have to run your hand down forty links and find the one that doesn't move the same.

He kept a coffee can of parts that didn't belong to anything anymore. Half-links, a crank bolt, some cone nuts gone slightly oval from being tightened with pliers because the right wrench wasn't around. "Use what's here," he'd say, like it was a rule of the universe instead of just cheapness. You learn to true a wheel with your thumb against the brake pad as a gauge because a truing stand costs money and this works, mostly, if you go slow, which he never demonstrated going slow, he just did it slow while talking fast, and you were supposed to extract the slowness from the talking somehow.

Brakes he trusted you with almost immediately, which in hindsight was alarming. "Squeeze it. Does it stop." Some deeper theory about pad angle and toe-in existed somewhere but it arrived in fragments over years, usually as a correction. "Not flush, angle the front in a hair, or it'll squeal at you every morning like it's got something to say." He never explained why. The why was for people with time.

Tires he did with his eyes somewhere else, radio on, tube half out before you'd even found the valve. You'd ask what caused the flat and he'd hold it up to the light without really looking, find the pinhole by feel almost, and say "glass, probably," already reaching for the patch kit, already annoyed that you needed a reason instead of a repair.

What actually stuck, years later, standing over your own kid's bike with your own coffee can of mismatched parts: he wasn't impatient with you. He was impatient with the bike. He wanted it fixed and out of his hands, back under a kid, in motion, because a bike standing upside down in a driveway was a kind of waste he couldn't stand to look at for long. All the shortcuts, the "use what's here," the refusal to name the tools — that wasn't him withholding anything. He just didn't experience bicycles as objects worth lingering on. Only as things that should be moving and currently weren't.

You never got the vocabulary from him. You got the hands. The vocabulary you had to go find later, in a book, alone, slightly embarrassed that you needed it.
