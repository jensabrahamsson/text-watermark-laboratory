We drove up before it was light, which felt important at the time, like the fish would know if we were late. My cousin's friend had the auger, an actual gas-powered one, and he was proud of it in a way that made me not want to ask too many questions about it. The lake was white and enormous and made a sound sometimes, a kind of long groan from somewhere underneath, and everyone said that was normal, the ice settling, but nobody explained further and I didn't ask.

The hole itself took less time than the walk out to where he wanted to drill it. He kept saying "structure," like the lake had furniture down there we needed to find. I stood next to the hole while he set up two rods and I remember looking down into it and being surprised the water was black, not blue, just black, moving slightly, like it was breathing.

I had the wrong boots. I want to be clear about this because it explains most of what I remember. They were fine boots for walking to a car or standing at a bus stop but they were not fine boots for four hours on a frozen lake in February, and by the second hour I had stopped being able to feel my toes as individual toes, just as a block, like my foot had been replaced with a loaf of something. I kept stamping them, quietly, so it wouldn't look like I was cold, though obviously I was cold, everyone was cold, that was the whole event.

There was a shelter, a pop-up tent thing, but only room for two people inside it comfortably and three of us had come, so I did the math and volunteered to be outside, which I regret slightly even now. Someone handed me coffee from a thermos and it was the best coffee I have ever had, not because it was good coffee but because my hands were around it.

We caught one fish. A perch, small, and it flopped on the ice for a second before someone's dad — not my dad, someone else's — picked it up like he'd done it ten thousand times and put it in a bucket that had a little bit of lake water in it, already skinning over with ice at the edges. I don't remember what happened to the fish after that. I assume it went home with someone. I didn't ask.

Around noon the wind picked up and someone's cheek had gone that white-grey color you're supposed to worry about, and that was when we packed up, without anyone quite saying we were packing up — just a general drift back toward the trucks, rods over shoulders, the auger guy carrying his auger like a rifle.

In the car my feet came back with that specific pain, pins fighting their way into a room that's too small for them. Nobody talked much on the drive back. I think we were all doing the same private accounting of whether it had been worth it, and not arriving anywhere close to an answer.
