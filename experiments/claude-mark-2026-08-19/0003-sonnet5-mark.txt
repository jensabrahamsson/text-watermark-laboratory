The tree stood exactly on the property line, which meant nothing until the fruit started falling.

Bengtsson noticed it first, in September, when he found four apples in his gutter and traced the branch back to see it crossing clean over the fence into his airspace. He knocked on Halvorsen's door with one of the apples in his hand, like evidence.

"This branch," he said, pointing.

Halvorsen came out in his socks. "It's been there for thirty years."

"It wasn't dropping fruit into my gutter for thirty years."

"Trees grow."

That was the whole conversation, the first time. Bengtsson went home and looked up the boundary regulations, printed two pages, and left them in Halvorsen's mailbox without a note. Halvorsen read them standing at the box, put them in his recycling, and did nothing.

By October the ground under the disputed branch was a mess of windfall, wasps working through the split skins, and Bengtsson had started raking it into a pile at the base of the fence, on Halvorsen's side, every second day. Halvorsen watched him do it once from the kitchen window and didn't say anything. He assumed it would stop.

It didn't stop. It became a rhythm. Bengtsson raked, Halvorsen ignored the pile until it got tall enough to be visible from the street, then moved it himself, cursing under his breath, into his own compost. Neither of them discussed this arrangement. It simply started happening, twice a week, like a tide.

The tree itself had been planted by the previous owner of Halvorsen's house, a man named Ek who died in 2009 and whom neither of them had known well. Nobody remembered what variety it was. Bengtsson's wife thought it might be an Ingrid Marie. Halvorsen didn't care what it was called; he liked the shade it threw over his patio in July, which was the only month he used the patio.

In November, after the leaves were down and the argument had cooled into something more like weather, Bengtsson came out one Saturday with a pruning saw and cut the offending branch off at the trunk, on his own side, without asking. He did it in about forty minutes, working carefully, and left the wound painted with sealant, professional about it.

Halvorsen found the cut branch stacked neatly by his own fence, cut into fireplace lengths, as if this made it a gift and not a unilateral act. He stood looking at it for a while. Then he carried the logs inside and stacked them by his woodstove, because it was good dry apple wood and there was no sense wasting it.

The following spring the tree bloomed asymmetrically, heavier on Halvorsen's side now, and neither man mentioned this. Bengtsson's gutters stayed clear. Halvorsen's patio lost some of its shade in July, which he noticed and didn't say anything about either, sitting out there anyway some evenings with a beer, watching the lopsided tree cast its lopsided shadow, waiting, the way you wait for something without naming it, for whatever would happen to it next.
