The hut sits where the trail levels out, a low shape of grey timber that the fog keeps almost hiding until you're within thirty meters of it. The roof is corrugated metal, dark with damp, and it holds the last of the day's heat so that faint steam lifts off it in thin ribbons. There's no view. The valley that should open below the ridge is gone, replaced by a close white nothing that presses against the windows and makes the single room feel smaller than it is.

Inside, the air smells of woodsmoke that's gone cold, of wet wool, of the sourness of a canvas pack left too long in a corner. Someone has stacked birch logs by the stove, bark peeling in curls, and a box of matches sits on top with half the heads struck and blackened. The floorboards are wide and worn pale down the middle where feet have crossed them for decades, darker along the edges where boots rarely go. A bench runs along one wall, its cushion flattened, a wool blanket folded at one end with a corner someone has chewed — mice, probably, or a dog that came through in a drier month.

The fog outside doesn't move so much as thicken and thin. At the window you can watch it swallow the nearest pine, thirty meters off, then let it back for a moment, needles beaded with water, before taking it again. Sound behaves strangely in this: a bird calls somewhere below and it's impossible to tell if it's close or thirty meters or three hundred. Water drips off the eaves in no particular rhythm, landing on a flat stone that's been worn into a small basin by exactly that dripping, probably for longer than the hut has stood.

Someone left a candle stub on the windowsill, half burned, with a ring of old wax pooled beneath it. Next to it, a logbook, its cover soft and curling from humidity, filled with names and dates and short notes in different hands — arrived 14:40, fog, no view, but the coffee was good. The pages smell faintly of mildew when you open them.

The cold comes in through the door frame in a thin, steady line, cutting across the warmer air near the stove. Your fingers, when you hold them near the unlit firebox, are stiffer than you noticed on the trail. Outside, the fog carries the particular quiet of high ground — not silence exactly, but a muffling, as if the whole mountain has been wrapped in something and set down gently.

Boots by the door are still wet. A rope hangs on a nail, coiled loosely, its end frayed. The window glass is old, warped enough that the white outside ripples slightly when you move your head, and for a moment the fog itself seems to be inside the glass rather than beyond it.
