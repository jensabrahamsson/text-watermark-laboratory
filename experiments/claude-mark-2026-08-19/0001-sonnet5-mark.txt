The bus dropped me at the top of the hill because the road down to the harbour was too narrow for anything with wheels wider than a bicycle. I hadn't expected that, and for a moment I just stood there with my bag against my leg, looking down at the roofs stacked one below the other like something spilled and left to settle. The light was doing that thing it does at the end of the day in places near water, turning soft and orange and a little apologetic, as if it knew it was leaving and wanted to make up for it.

I walked down. The street was cobbled and uneven, and my shoes made a flat slapping sound that seemed too loud for the hour. Shutters were half closed on most of the windows I passed, though behind a few of them I could hear the clatter of someone starting dinner, a radio turned low, a child arguing about something in a language I didn't have. A cat crossed in front of me without hurrying and didn't look back.

By the time I reached the harbour itself the sun was almost gone, just a orange smear behind the headland, and the water had turned the color of a bruise, purple-blue and heavy-looking. The boats were tied up for the night, maybe a dozen of them, small wooden ones with paint worn thin at the waterline, and they knocked gently against the dock in a rhythm that had nothing urgent about it. Someone had strung a line of lights along the harbour wall, the cheap kind that don't quite match each other in color, and they'd just been switched on, so they glowed weakly against the darkening sky, not yet doing much work but promising to.

There was a café at the far end, more of a lean-to with three tables outside, and an old man sat alone at one of them with a small glass of something clear in front of him. He watched me the way people in small places watch strangers, without unfriendliness, just noting. I nodded. He nodded back and went on watching the water.

I sat at the table next to his because it seemed rude not to, after that nod. A woman came out from inside, wiping her hands on an apron, and I pointed at the old man's glass and she understood and brought me one too. It tasted of aniseed and something rougher underneath, and it was cold enough to sting a little going down. Somewhere behind the café a dog barked twice and stopped.

The last color drained out of the sky while I sat there, and the string lights came into their own, throwing small coins of light onto the water that broke apart and reformed with each little movement of the boats. Nobody else came to sit at the other tables. The old man finished his drink, stood with some effort, said something to the woman that made her laugh, and walked off up a side street without a light of his own, just trusting the dark and the shape of the place he'd known his whole life.

I stayed longer than I meant to. There wasn't anywhere else to be, and the harbour had a way of making the question of where to be next seem unimportant, at least for an hour. Somewhere a shutter banged closed. The boats kept knocking against the dock, patient, indifferent, and I found I didn't mind at all that I'd have nothing more remarkable to report than this: a town settling into evening, and a stranger let, briefly, to sit and watch it happen.
