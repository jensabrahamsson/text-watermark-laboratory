The press starts at four, and by four-fifteen it has already stopped twice. Bengt stands at the folder unit with his hand flat on the housing, feeling for the vibration that means the paper is running true. Tonight it isn't. Tonight the web keeps splitting somewhere in the third tower, a thin tearing sound under the roar, and then the whole line shudders to silence and the red light comes on above station six.

He climbs the stairs two at a time, not because he's young — he isn't, not anymore — but because thirty years of this teaches you which sounds mean you have ten seconds and which mean you have ten minutes. This one means ten seconds. He finds the tear where he expects it, a ragged mouth of newsprint wound twice around the chill roller, already scorching faintly against the metal. He kills the drive, waits for the drum to stop turning, and starts pulling paper out by the armful, stuffing it into the waste chute like a man feeding an animal that has stopped being hungry an hour ago.

Lina is on the phone in the control room, telling the editor that the front section will be forty minutes late, maybe fifty, and no, moving Löfgren's column to page nine will not fix the tension roller on unit two. She has said some version of this sentence at least once a week since September. The paper is old. The paper has been old since before she started here. There is a man in Norrköping who still makes the parts, machining them by hand from drawings that predate the euro, and every time she calls him he sighs the same particular sigh before quoting a price that makes the publisher's mouth go thin.

By five they have it running again, and for eleven minutes it holds. Bengt watches the copies come off the folder in their stacked, staggered rhythm, counts them without meaning to the way he's counted them for three decades, and allows himself to think that tonight might be an ordinary night after all. Then unit two does the thing it does, a stutter in the tension, and the paper snaps clean across a photograph of the municipal council standing outside the new school. He doesn't swear. He stopped swearing at the press years ago; it never listened.

At six-forty they are still short of the run, and the delivery drivers are parked outside smoking with the patience of men who have done this before and will do it again. Astrid from bundling brings coffee up in paper cups that are somehow always too full, and nobody spills any of it, out of long practice. The dawn edition will make it out late but it will make it out, wet-inked and warm, smelling the way it has always smelled, folded by a machine that hates itself and loves nothing and keeps, against all reasonable expectation, delivering the news of a small town to the people who still want it folded on their step by seven.

Bengt goes back down to the tower with his rag and his flashlight, because there is always another tear coming, and because somebody has to be standing there when it does.
