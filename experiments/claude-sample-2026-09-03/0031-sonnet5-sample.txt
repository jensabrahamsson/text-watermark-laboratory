The presses start at eleven, same as always. Björn checks the paper reels first — three of them, waist-high, wrapped in brown plastic that crackles when he peels it back. He's done this for nineteen years and still runs his thumb along the edge of each reel before loading it, feeling for splices the supplier taped in when the mill ran short.

The first jam comes twenty minutes in. Nothing dramatic — just a stutter in the rhythm, a change in pitch that Björn hears before he sees anything wrong. He hits the stop button on the console and the whole line goes quiet at once, rollers freezing mid-turn. Somewhere in the folder unit, page fourteen has folded wrong and backed up against page fifteen, and now there's a wedge of paper jammed sideways in the gap where the blade comes down.

He and Marita work it out together, him on one side of the folder, her on the other, passing a flashlight back and forth. The torn paper comes out in strips, some of it still readable — a headline about the municipal budget vote, half a photo of the swimming hall renovation. They feed the ripped sheets into a bin that already holds four other failed pages from the run before this one. The bin is nearly full. Someone will empty it before morning, pulp it, forget about it.

Second jam, forty minutes later, is worse. This one's in the web itself — the paper tears clean across, and the loose end goes flapping through six units before anyone can kill the line. Björn swears in a way he doesn't usually swear in front of Marita, then apologizes without really meaning it, and they start rethreading the whole path by hand. It takes twenty-five minutes. Ink gets on his forearms, on his shirt cuff, on the handle of the flashlight.

By one in the morning they've lost enough time that the delivery drivers start calling. Marita answers with the phone wedged between shoulder and ear while she holds a wrench in the other hand. She tells them twenty more minutes, then thirty, then just says she'll call back. Outside, three of the drivers are parked in the lot with their engines idling, radio on low, waiting.

The third jam is small — a paper dust buildup that's been narrowing one of the rollers all night, finally enough to catch the edge of a sheet and crumple it. Björn clears it in ninety seconds without even fully stopping the machine, just easing the pressure and pulling the sheet free with two fingers.

At half past two the run finishes clean, no more stops. Marita counts the wasted stack by eye — maybe four hundred copies gone to the bin tonight, more than usual but not the worst she's seen. The good copies get bundled, strapped, loaded onto pallets still warm from the dryer.

The drivers pull up one after another, load their bundles, pull away into streets that are empty except for a fox crossing near the depot gate. Björn stays another twenty minutes checking the folder unit, running his hand along the blade edge, making a note for the morning shift about a bearing that sounds off. Then he locks the floor, turns off the lights over the press, and leaves the office lamp burning for whoever comes in first.
