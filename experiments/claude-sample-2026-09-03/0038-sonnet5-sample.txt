Thought for 3s

The line ran between two hooks bolted into brick, sagging slightly in the middle no matter how tight anyone pulled it. Number 4 used the left half, number 7 the right, and this had never been discussed, only established, the way most things in the courtyard were established: through repetition until repetition became rule.

Ingrid from number 4 hung her washing on Tuesdays and Fridays, always in the same order — sheets first, then towels, then the smaller things pinned in a row like a receding chorus. Bertil from number 7 hung his whenever he felt like it, which annoyed her less than she let on, because his shirts were always the same three colors and it gave the line a kind of rhythm even when he broke the schedule.

They spoke maybe six times a year, standing at the line with a basket on one hip, the wind doing most of the work of undressing the wet fabric from their hands.

"Rain later," Bertil said one Thursday, not looking up from a pillowcase.

"Radio said Saturday."

"Radio's wrong."

It rained Friday. Neither mentioned it again.

There was a system for the pins — a chipped ceramic bowl kept in the gap between two loose bricks, refilled by whoever noticed it was empty, which turned out to be Ingrid nine times out of ten, though Bertil bought the pins twice, plastic ones that snapped in the cold, which annoyed her enough to mention it once, mildly, and he switched back to wood without argument.

His granddaughter came on Sundays sometimes, four years old, fascinated by the pulley wheel that let the line slide out toward the far wall. She would crank it back and forth, back and forth, empty, just to watch the mechanism work, and Ingrid, hanging out her own wash on a day that wasn't Tuesday or Friday because the forecast had scared her into moving things up, let the girl do it seventeen times before Bertil called her in for lunch.

"She likes machines," he said, by way of explanation no one had asked for.

"She can crank mine too."

That was the whole conversation.

In August the line snapped — not dramatically, just a slow fraying at the left hook that gave way under the weight of a wet duvet cover, dropping half of Ingrid's wash into the gravel. Bertil heard the commotion and came out in socks, no shoes, and held the frayed end while she re-threaded it through the pulley, neither of them saying much beyond the practical: higher, no, other way, there.

They restrung it together in maybe four minutes. Afterward he went back inside without comment, and she rehung the duvet cover, now streaked with dust along one edge, a mark that would stay faintly visible through every wash after that, the kind of thing only she would ever notice or remember, hanging there most Tuesdays, most Fridays, next to whatever Bertil had decided to wash that particular week.
