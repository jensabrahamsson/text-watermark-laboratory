The platform lights come on before the sky does anything about it. Six-forty, and the rain has been going since midnight, not hard, just steady, the kind that finds the gap in a hood and runs down the back of the neck. The tram stop smells of wet wool and wet concrete, that mineral smell concrete gets when it's been drinking for hours. Someone's umbrella has given up on one side, the spokes bent backward, and they hold it anyway, tilted like a broken wing, because taking it down means admitting the walk here already soaked one shoulder.

The 4 comes around the bend with its headlights doubled in the wet asphalt, brakes with a long exhale of air, and the doors fold open onto a crowd that's already learned not to make eye contact. Inside, the windows have fogged from the collective breath and the heat of thirty bodies in wet coats. Someone has drawn nothing in the condensation, not even a smiley face, just a bare patch wiped with a sleeve so they can check the stop names against the blur outside. The floor is a shallow tidal pool of tracked-in rain, black grit, a single dead leaf pinned under someone's boot.

The smell changes car to car. Near the doors it's cold air and diesel from the street. Further in it's coffee from a paper cup someone is drinking too fast, and wet dog from a low-slung terrier tied to its owner's wrist, shaking itself once, briefly, before settling under the seat. A phone plays something tinny through one earbud left loose, bleeding into the general hum of tires on wet rail and the intercom announcing the next stop in a voice that has said it ten thousand times before.

Outside, the city moves at half its usual color. The brick facades have gone the deep red-brown they only get when saturated, gutters running fast along the curbs, a McDonald's sign smeared into two overlapping yellows in the puddle beneath it. A cyclist goes past in a neon vest and full rain gear, pedaling with the grim patience of someone who has done this every day for years and stopped resenting it around year three. At the crossing, a cluster of school kids in matching reflective vests waits for the light, hoods up, backpacks wrapped in plastic covers, one of them stomping deliberately in a puddle while an adult in a raincoat calls out something that doesn't quite land over the traffic noise.

The tram stops longer than usual at the central station. Doors open, a gust of wetter air comes in, someone swears quietly about the delay, checks their watch, checks it again ninety seconds later as if that will change anything. A woman in a business coat holds her phone under the awning outside, one hand cupped over the screen, thumb moving fast, rain beading on the shoulders of her coat like it hasn't decided yet whether to soak in.

By the last stretch the windows have cleared a little, wiped by enough sleeves, and the office blocks come into view, lit floor by floor even this early, someone already at a desk on the fourth floor of a building that looks like every other building. The doors open onto the plaza outside, rain still coming down at the same unbothered pace it kept all morning, and the crowd disperses into it without much ceremony, umbrellas going back up, collars going back up, the day simply continuing, wet, ordinary, already half forgotten by the time the coffee machine in the lobby starts its noise.
