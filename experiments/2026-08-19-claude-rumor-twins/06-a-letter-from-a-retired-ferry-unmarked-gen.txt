Dear Elin,

You asked me once, on the ferry, why I always slowed down before I could see anything wrong. You were maybe nine, wrapped in that yellow raincoat too big for you, annoyed that the crossing was taking longer than the schedule said it should. I told you I could smell the fog coming before it arrived, and you laughed like I'd told you a fairy tale. I want to tell you the truth of it now, because you're old enough, and because I think you'll need it for more than water.

Fog is not an absence. That's the first thing people get wrong. They think of it as a kind of blindness, a nothing that swallows the something. But out on the strait, fog has a texture. It has weight and temperature and a smell like wet stone and diesel and, underneath that, something clean, something like the inside of a shell. You learn to read it the way you'd read a face. A thin fog moving fast means it will lift by the buoy. A thick fog that sits low and doesn't move at all — that one you respect. That one you slow down for, engines idling, ears doing the work your eyes can't.

Here is what forty-one years on the water taught me, and what I never quite managed to say to you directly: you do not fight fog. You do not gun the engine and hope. You reduce speed, you sound the horn, you trust the compass and the depth sounder even when every instinct in your body is screaming that you should be able to see. The instinct is a liar. It wants certainty where there is none available, and it will run you onto rocks trying to get it.

I think about this now that you're leaving for university, out past anything I can chart for you. You'll have fog of your own — decisions with no clear edges, people you can't read, whole stretches of years where you won't be able to see the shore in either direction. I won't pretend I have a chart for that kind of fog. Nobody does. But I can tell you what I told the young deckhands who came up scared and stiff-shouldered on their first bad crossing: slow down. Listen more than you look. Trust the instruments you've built — the ones inside you, I mean, the sense of who you are and what you won't do, even when you can't see the water ahead of you. Sound your horn. Let people know where you are. Don't be too proud to radio for a fix on your position.

And the fog always lifts, Elin. Every single time, in forty-one years, it lifted. Sometimes it took an hour, sometimes most of a day, and twice it took long enough that I doubted my own memory of what the sun looked like. But it lifted, and the harbor was always more or less where I'd left it.

I'm proud of you. Go carefully. Go slow when you need to.

All my love,
Morfar
