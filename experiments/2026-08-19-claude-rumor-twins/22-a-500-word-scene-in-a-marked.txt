The rain started while she was folding the towels, and by the time it was hammering the metal awning outside she'd stopped folding altogether and just stood there watching the door.

Two other people in the place. An old man asleep in the plastic chair by the window, his mouth open, a green duffel bag between his feet like a dog he was minding. And a guy maybe twenty-five, headphones around his neck, not in his ears, watching his laundry go around in dryer number six like it owed him money.

The lights buzzed. One of the tubes above the folding table flickered every few seconds, on some private schedule of its own.

She went back to the towels. Wet footprints trailed from the door to the machines, drying unevenly, darker where the water had pooled.

The door opened. A woman came in soaked through, no umbrella, hauling a laundry cart with a broken wheel that skidded sideways on the tile. She swore under her breath, wrestled it straight, and looked around like she was surprised to find the place occupied.

"Is nine open?" she said, to no one in particular.

The guy at dryer six didn't look up. "Nine's broken. Sign's been there since May."

"Great." She looked at the row of machines, counting. "Fourteen?"

"Should be fine."

She started loading it, wringing out a hoodie first, water running off it onto the floor in a thin rope. The old man didn't stir. His duffel bag had a luggage tag on it, curled and illegible.

The rain kept up, changing pitch against the awning — sometimes a flat roar, sometimes something closer to static, like a radio between stations. Through the window the parking lot had gone the color of a nickel, the puddles jumping with each new gust.

"You waiting out the rain or waiting on a dryer?" the woman asked her, dropping quarters into the slot one at a time.

"Dryer already went. Just waiting for it to let up some."

"Might be a while." The woman leaned against the machine as it shuddered into its cycle. "Was clear an hour ago. I had the windows down."

"Figures."

The man in the chair woke up without any transition, eyes just open, like he'd never really been asleep, and looked at his bag, then at the room, then closed his eyes again.

Dryer six buzzed. The guy pulled his laundry out in one motion, everything still warm, folding nothing, just stuffing it all into a mesh bag that had clearly held more than it was built for. He zipped it, slung it over his shoulder, and stood at the door for a second looking at the rain like he was recalculating something.

Then he went out into it anyway, not running, just walking fast with his shoulders up, and the door swung shut behind him and caught the wind and banged twice against the frame before it settled.

The woman watched him go. "Guess he wasn't waiting on anything."

She went back to folding towels. Outside, a car passed slow through standing water, its headlights dragging long across the wet glass, and then the lot was empty again, and the rain went on the way it had been going on.
