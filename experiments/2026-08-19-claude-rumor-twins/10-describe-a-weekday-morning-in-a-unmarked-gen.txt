The bell over the door doesn't ring so much as clatter — someone glued the clapper back on wrong eight years ago and nobody's fixed it since. Gary's already been in for forty minutes, lights half up, radio on low, doing the thing he does every morning where he walks the aisles with a coffee and just looks, like he's checking nothing crawled in overnight.

The heater under the counter ticks and smells faintly of hot dust. A film of grit sits on top of the glass display case — washers, cotter pins, brass fittings nobody can name — because dusting it is always tomorrow's job. Behind the register, a corkboard three layers deep in curling paper: a lost-cat flyer from March, a handwritten sign about propane tank exchanges, a laminated card with the Wi-Fi password that hasn't worked since the router died.

Boxes from yesterday's delivery are still stacked by the paint mixer, not because anyone's lazy but because the truck came at 4:45 and Gary was alone closing. He starts breaking them down now, box cutter clicking, cardboard folding flat with that particular groan. A guy comes in for a single 3/8 bolt — just one — and stands at the counter turning it over in his fingers like he's not sure it's the right one, and Gary doesn't even look, just says aisle four, third bin from the left, and goes back to the boxes.

The coffee pot in back is the real center of gravity. It's been reused so many times the carafe has a brown ring halfway up that won't scrub out. Someone left a half sleeve of saltines by it. A regular — flannel, boots crusted with drywall dust — leans on the counter not buying anything yet, just talking about a leak under his sink, and Gary listens with his arms crossed, already mentally pulling parts: compression fitting, maybe a new trap, does he want the good washer or the one that'll last a year.

Sunlight comes in low through the front window and lights up a haze that's just always there — sawdust, WD-40, cardboard, someone's cigarette from outside drifting in when the door opens. The floor's the old kind, wood gone gray-brown and dished in a path from the door to the register from thirty years of boots. A cat that isn't technically the store's cat is asleep on a bag of deicer nobody's put out yet because it's not cold enough.

By eight-thirty there are three people in the store and none of them are talking to each other, just moving around Gary in this loose orbit, picking things up, putting most of them back. The register drawer sticks and he has to hip-check it shut. Somewhere in the back a fluorescent tube is buzzing at a pitch just annoying enough to notice and not quite annoying enough to fix. It's not a good morning or a bad one. It's just open, and it'll keep being open, mess and all, until six.
