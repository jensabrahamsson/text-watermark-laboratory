The Vestergaard County Historical Museum opens at ten on Tuesdays, which is a formality more than a promise. Elna unlocks the door, flips the sign, and then has the whole building to herself until noon, when she eats her sandwich at the admissions desk because there's no one there to see her do it.

It wasn't always like this. There's a guest book from 1987 that's nearly full — actual full, cursive crammed sideways in the margins because people kept coming even when they'd run out of room to sign. Now the current book, started three years ago, has maybe forty names in it. Elna knows most of them. The Petersen kid who has a school project every single semester, somehow. A retired geologist from two towns over who comes to look at the rock samples and correct the little handwritten labels, which nobody has the heart to stop him from doing. A couple, once, who got lost looking for a winery.

Tuesdays are the worst of the worst days, and nobody quite knows why. Elna has a theory that it's because Monday still feels like the weekend's afterglow and by Wednesday people are already thinking about Thursday, and Tuesday just gets skipped over entirely, like the museum itself. She's mentioned this theory to exactly one person, the mailman, who agreed with the seriousness the subject deserved.

The collection is what you'd expect and also not. There's the arrowhead case, obviously, and the loom that a docent demonstrates on weekends when there are people to demonstrate it for. But there's also a room in the back — technically it's the old furnace room, they never got around to renaming it on the map — full of things that don't fit anywhere else. A dentist's chair from the 1920s that looks like a torture device and possibly was one. A collection of hand-painted funeral fans from a church that burned down in the sixties. A taxidermied fox that someone donated with a note reading only "he was a good boy" and no further explanation, ever, from anyone.

Elna spends her Tuesdays doing the things you can't do when people are around. She re-labels things. She sits with the fox for a while, sometimes, just to keep him company, because it feels wrong for nobody to look at him for a week straight. She dusts the loom, which doesn't really need dusting, but the motion of it is satisfying and there's nothing else demanding her attention. Once she found a moth that had gotten trapped between the glass and the arrowheads, and she spent twenty minutes trying to free it without setting off the ancient alarm system, which is really just a bell on a string.

The museum isn't going to close. It's not dramatic like that. The county keeps funding it at the same modest level it always has, mostly out of habit, the way you keep paying for a magazine subscription you never read because canceling it feels like more effort than it's worth. Elna will be here next Tuesday too, and the Tuesday after that, keeping the lights on for the fox and the loom and whoever might, eventually, wander in by accident, looking for something else entirely.
