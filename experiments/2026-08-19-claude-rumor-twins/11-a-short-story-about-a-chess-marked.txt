The basement of St. Anders had a smell that never changed — candle wax and instant coffee and something like wet wool, even in July. Folding tables, eight of them, set up in two rows under fluorescent light that buzzed if you listened for it. The chess club met on Tuesdays at seven, organized by a retired postal worker named Gunnar who had run it for eleven years and would tell you so if you gave him an opening.

There were twelve regulars, give or take. Ingrid, eighty-one, who played the Caro-Kann against everyone regardless of what they opened with. Two teenagers, Sam and Malte, who came together and left together and rarely spoke to anyone else, hunched over the board like they were guarding it. A man named Robert who worked nights at the hospital and sometimes fell asleep between moves, chin dropping, then jerking back up to stare at the position like it had changed while he wasn't looking.

The clocks were the old mechanical kind, the ones that ticked audibly and slammed down with a sound like a door closing. Gunnar had bought six of them at an estate sale in 2015 and treated them with the specific tenderness of a man who didn't have much else to be tender about.

Elin started coming in September. She was thirty-four, worked in payroll for the municipality, and had learned chess from her father as a child but hadn't played in fifteen years. Her first night, she lost four games in a row to Ingrid, who beat her without appearing to try, moving pieces while carrying on a separate conversation with the man next to her about a hip replacement.

"You're rushing," Ingrid said, not looking up, on the third game.

"I know."

"So don't."

Elin came back the next week anyway. She started arriving early, before the tables were even set up, and helped Gunnar carry the folding chairs down from the closet by the boiler room. He never asked her to. She just started doing it, and he started expecting it, and neither of them mentioned the shift.

By November she could hold her own against most of the room except Ingrid and a quiet man named Petr who played like he was doing math homework, no hesitation, no visible enjoyment. She beat Robert once, when he fell asleep with a bishop hanging, and felt bad enough about it that she nudged the board and pretended it had been an accident, though everyone at the table knew it hadn't.

The basement had one window, high up near the ceiling, level with the parking lot outside. In winter you could see boots passing by it, cut off at the ankle, and snow collecting on the sill on the outside where no one could reach it. Gunnar kept a space heater running by his own table because the radiators in that corner didn't work and never had, not in eleven years, not in however many years before that.

On the Tuesday before Christmas, only six people showed. Ingrid, Elin, the boys, Robert, and Gunnar himself, playing against no one, resetting a board from a game he'd read about in a book. The heater hummed. The clocks sat unused, stacked at the end of the table like something waiting for spring.
