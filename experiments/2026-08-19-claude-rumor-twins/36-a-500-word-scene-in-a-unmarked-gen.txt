The rehearsal room smelled of dust and rosin. Late afternoon light slanted through blinds gone yellow with age, striping the floor where the piano sat like something wounded — lid cracked open, one leg propped on a paint can where the original had snapped off years ago.

Elin ran her fingers over the keys without pressing them. "Middle C's dead."

"Middle C's always been dead," said Marcus, from the folding chair by the window. He hadn't looked up from his script. "You're supposed to work around it."

"I can't work around a piano that doesn't have a piano in it."

"It's got eighty-one keys that work."

"It's got eighty-one keys that make sound. Not the same thing." She pressed one, low in the bass, and it wheezed out something closer to a sigh than a note. "This one thinks it's a cello."

Marcus finally looked up. He was younger than the part he was playing, and it showed in the way he held the script — too carefully, like it might correct him. "Can we just run the scene? Ingrid's going to be here in twenty minutes and I still don't know where I'm supposed to be standing when you say the line about the war."

"You're supposed to be standing wherever you feel like standing. That's the whole point of the line."

"That's not direction, that's a horoscope."

Elin laughed despite herself, a short bark that surprised her. She sat down properly at the bench, tested a few more keys — a cluster in the middle register produced something almost tuneful, if you didn't listen too closely. "Fine. Stand by the door. When I hit this—" she played the dead cluster again "—you come in."

"That's not a cue, that's noise."

"Everything's noise until someone decides it isn't." She said it lightly, but there was something underneath it, and Marcus heard the something and didn't push.

He crossed to the door anyway, script rolled into a tube in his fist. Outside, a tram rattled past on the wet street, and for a second its sound layered over the piano's broken chord in a way that was, against all reason, almost beautiful.

"Again," Elin said.

He came in on cue. Said the line about the war too fast, the way he always did, like he wanted to get past it before it could mean anything. She stopped him with a raised hand.

"Slower. You're not confessing. You're remembering."

"What's the difference?"

"One of them you can undo." She hit the dead C again, and this time, in the silence after, neither of them moved to fill it. "Try it like you already know how it ends."

He nodded, rolled his shoulders, and started over — this time letting the pause sit before the words, the way the piano let its broken note hang a half-second longer than any note had a right to.

Outside, the tram was long gone. The light had shifted another inch across the floor. Somewhere down the hall, a door opened — Ingrid, early.

Neither of them stopped.
