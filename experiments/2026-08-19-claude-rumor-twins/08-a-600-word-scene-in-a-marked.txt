The bell over the door had a crack in it, so it made a sound like a cough instead of a ring. Elin heard it from the back room and didn't look up from the box of paperbacks she was pricing.

"We close in ten minutes," she said.

"I know." The man stood just inside the door, not moving toward the shelves. He had a coat too warm for the weather and a canvas bag over one shoulder, empty-looking, the strap slack. "I used to come here. Years ago."

"People say that a lot."

"No, I mean it. There was a different owner then. Older man. Smoked in the shop, which you're probably not supposed to do."

"Bertil." Elin set down the paperback, a Maigret with a broken spine. "He sold it to my aunt. Died two years after."

"I'm sorry to hear it."

"Everyone is. He was not an easy man to like, but he knew where every book in this shop was, which counts for something." She went back to pricing. Fifteen kronor, she wrote inside the cover in pencil, because ink devalues a used book, everyone in the trade knew that.

The man drifted toward the poetry section, which was two shelves wedged between local history and a locked case of first editions nobody could afford. He ran a finger along the spines without pulling anything out.

"Are you looking for something specific?" Elin asked, because it was closing time and she wanted to know whether to hover or ignore him.

"A book my father had. Blue cover, Swedish poetry, translated into German. I don't remember the poet. I was eleven. I remember the cover more than anything in it."

"That narrows it down to about four hundred books."

"I know."

She came out from behind the counter anyway, wiping pencil dust on her jeans. "Blue like the sky or blue like a bruise?"

"Blue like a bruise. Darker."

They went through the shelf together, pulling out anything with a blue spine, setting the rejects in a stack on the floor. A Karin Boye. A Gunnar Ekelöf that made him pause, turn it over, then shake his head. A cheap anthology from the seventies with a torn dust jacket.

"This might be it," Elin said, holding up a slim volume, no dust jacket at all, just cloth boards gone soft at the corners. Tranströmer, a German edition, the title stamped in silver that had mostly worn away.

He took it and opened it without looking at the cover first, went straight to the middle, as if his hands remembered a page his eyes didn't. He read something, lips barely moving, then closed it.

"No," he said. "Wrong poet. But thank you for looking."

"You can still buy it."

"I don't think I want that one. I want the one I remember."

Elin didn't argue. She'd worked the shop long enough to know that people rarely wanted the book that would actually satisfy them; they wanted the one attached to a particular afternoon, a particular smoke-filled room, a father who was probably dead now too, though he hadn't said so and she hadn't asked.

She looked at the clock. Four minutes past closing.

"I'll keep an eye out," she said, which was what she said to everyone, and meant, this time, only a little.

He put the Tranströmer back in its slot, not quite where it had been, and left without buying anything. The bell coughed again. Elin turned the sign in the window and went back to her box of paperbacks, fifteen kronor each, in pencil.
