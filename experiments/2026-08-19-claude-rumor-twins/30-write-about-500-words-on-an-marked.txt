The pool had been drained since September, but the tiles still held their color—that particular municipal blue that looks nothing like water and everything like a swatch from a 1970s catalog. Snow had come and gone twice already, leaving grey rings around the deep end where it had melted last and pooled before freezing again. Someone had propped a single lane rope against the wall near the lifeguard chair, coiled and stiffened, like it had given up trying to be useful.

The chain-link fence around the perimeter sagged in one corner where kids had climbed over it in October, before the cold made the metal too painful to grip. Now their footprints were gone, replaced by nothing, because nobody came here in January except the man who checked the boiler room twice a week and occasionally a dog walker who used the parking lot as a turnaround.

Inside the bathhouse, the changing rooms smelled like chlorine that had nowhere left to go. It clung to the grout, to the drain covers, to the wooden benches bolted to the wall. A row of lockers stood open, all of them, numbered in peeling brass digits that started at 1 and went to 84. Number 47 had a sticker on it, half-scraped, advertising a summer camp from three years back. The rest were just empty rectangles, doors hanging at slightly different angles depending on how many times they'd been slammed.

The diving board had been removed for the season and leaned against a wall in the equipment shed, wrapped in a blue tarp that had come loose at one corner. Underneath, the fiberglass surface still showed the wear pattern from thousands of feet—two dark ovals near the tip where people planted themselves before jumping.

From the observation deck, you could see the whole basin. Leaves had collected in the shallow end, pressed flat and dark against the tile, decomposing slowly in whatever moisture remained. A plastic lounge chair had blown in from somewhere and sat upside down near the four-foot marker, one leg missing. The lane markers on the bottom of the pool—the black lines that swimmers follow—were still visible under a thin skin of ice that had formed after a brief thaw and refrozen overnight.

The boiler room hummed at a low idle, kept just warm enough to prevent the pipes from cracking. This was the only sound. No splashing, no whistle, no announcement about the pool closing in ten minutes. Just the boiler, and occasionally the building settling as the temperature dropped further after dark.

By four in the afternoon the light was already going, turning the water-stained concrete a flat grey-blue that matched the sky. The man who checked the boiler locked the gate behind him, the padlock clicking with a sound too small for the size of the space it was meant to secure. Somewhere past the fence, a car started, idled, and pulled away, and after that there was nothing to hear at all.
