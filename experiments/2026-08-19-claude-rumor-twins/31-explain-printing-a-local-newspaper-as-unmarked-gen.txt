The press room smells like hot metal and ink long before the first section rolls. Bengtsson checks his watch: 11:47 PM. Deadline for the Lerum edition was supposed to be 11:30.

"Web three again," shouts Nilsson from the control room, and everyone groans in unison, the particular groan of men who have heard this sound too many times this month. The paper feed on unit three has torn — not cleanly, but shredded, wrapped itself around the chill rollers like a snake eating something too big for it.

Bengtsson climbs the catwalk, flashlight in his teeth, and starts pulling paper. Long ribbons of newsprint, some still blank, some half-printed with tomorrow's headline about the municipal budget vote, come out in fistfuls. His hands are already black. They will still be black on Sunday.

"How bad?" he calls down.

"Twenty minutes if we're lucky. Forty if the splice failed too."

It failed too. Of course it failed too. The splice — where two giant rolls of paper are glued together so the press never has to stop — has come apart, and now there's a loose tail flapping through the folder unit, catching on a guide bar, and unit three groans to a stop with a sound like a dying accordion.

Down in the mailroom, the stackers are idle, waiting. Sofia, who drives the first delivery route out to the villages north of town, is leaning against her van in the loading dock, coffee going cold, checking the time on her phone every ninety seconds like it will change something. If the papers aren't bundled by 2 AM, half her route won't have news with their coffee. Farmers up before dawn. Retirees who still expect it folded on the step.

"We're threading it," Nilsson says over the radio, voice flat with the particular calm of someone who has done this four hundred times. Threading means guiding the new paper leader by hand through forty feet of rollers, plates, and dryers, a slow careful walk of paper through machine, and if it goes even slightly crooked it will tear again in ninety seconds and they start over.

It goes crooked. It tears again.

Bengtsson doesn't swear. He used to swear. Now he just breathes out through his nose and starts pulling paper again, and somewhere behind him someone laughs, not because anything is funny, but because at midnight, with ink on your hands and a print run behind schedule, laughing is cheaper than the alternative.

The second attempt holds. The press climbs back up to speed slowly — walk speed, then jog, then full run — thirty-five thousand copies an hour, the whole building humming again, that bone-deep vibration that means paper is actually moving. The first bundles hit the conveyor at 1:52 AM. Sofia is in her van before the ink is fully dry, headlights cutting into the dark toward roads that won't see another car for hours.

Nobody in Lerum will know, over their coffee, how close it came. That's rather the point.
