The museum opens at nine, which nobody has ever needed it to do. Marit unlocks the door, flips the sign, and by nine-fifteen she's already refilled the mint bowl at the front desk twice, not because anyone's taken a mint but because rearranging them gives her something to do with her hands. Tuesdays are like this. Mondays it's closed, officially, for reasons nobody remembers deciding. Wednesdays the school groups come through, thirty kids at a time smelling like wet wool and impatience. Saturdays there's the odd tourist who got lost looking for the lake. But Tuesday belongs to no one.

The museum has three rooms. One for the fishing industry, which used to be the whole reason the town existed, back when there were forty boats instead of six. One for the war years, mostly ration books and a soldier's uniform that doesn't fit any mannequin quite right, so it hangs a little loose in the shoulders like it's shrugging. And one upstairs that changes every few months, currently local textiles, before that it was regional geology, which drew exactly one visitor the whole run, a retired teacher who stayed for two hours and left without saying a word to anyone.

Marit does the rounds anyway. She checks the humidity gauge in the textile room, which has read the same number for six weeks. She dusts the ship models even though nothing settles on them on a Tuesday that wasn't already there on Monday. She sits at the desk and does the crossword from the regional paper, the one that comes out on Mondays and is already three days behind the national one, which she also has, but the regional one has better clues.

Sometimes Henrik from the hardware store next door comes in around eleven, not to look at anything, just to stand in the doorway and complain about the council or ask if she's heard about the ferry schedule changing again. He never goes past the entryway. She's not sure he's seen the fishing room since they were both in school, when it was still new and there was a ribbon-cutting and the mayor at the time gave a speech that ran too long.

The museum loses money. Everyone knows this, it comes up at the budget meeting every autumn, someone always suggests cutting Tuesday hours entirely, or Monday and Tuesday both, and someone else always says it's about presence, about the town having somewhere that says here is what we were, even if the saying happens to an empty room.

By four the light through the west window hits the ration books just right, yellowing them further, a process that's been going on for eighty years and isn't stopping for anyone's schedule. Marit turns off the upstairs lights first, then the fishing room, then the war room last, because something about leaving the uniform in the dark alone feels like it should come at the end, not the middle. She locks up at five. Nobody was there to see her do it, same as nobody was there for any of it, and tomorrow there will be children.
