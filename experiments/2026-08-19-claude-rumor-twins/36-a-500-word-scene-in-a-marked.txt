Claude responded: The rehearsal room smelled of dust and rosin.

The rehearsal room smelled of dust and rosin. Fluorescent tubes buzzed overhead, one of them flickering in a slow, irregular rhythm nobody had bothered to fix. Against the far wall stood the piano, an upright Yamaha with a cracked veneer, missing its front panel so the hammers and dampers sat exposed like ribs.

Mattias pressed a key. E-flat, or what used to be E-flat. The note came out flat and papery, more thud than tone, and underneath it something rattled.

"That's the one," he said. "Every time."

Sara didn't look up from her violin case. "Then don't play that one."

"It's in the piece."

"Then transpose it."

He pressed it again anyway, as if the second attempt might produce a different result. It didn't. He lifted the fallboard cover fully and peered inside, and Sara finally crossed the room to look too, resin dust still on her fingers.

"There," Mattias said, pointing. A felt hammer for the note had come loose from its shank, hanging by a thread of old glue, tilted at an angle that meant it caught two neighboring strings instead of one.

"You could push it back."

"I could push it back and it would fall off again in ten minutes."

"Ten minutes is longer than we have," Sara said. She checked her watch. Nineteen minutes until the room was booked for the wind quintet.

Jonas came in from the corridor with two coffees, saw them both leaning into the piano's guts, and stopped in the doorway. "Are we performing surgery now?"

"E-flat's broken," Mattias said, not turning around.

"E-flat's been broken since March."

"Since March?"

"I told Birgitta in March."

Sara straightened up. "So nothing's happened since March."

"Someone put in a request. I saw the form on the board." Jonas set the coffees down on the closed lid of a second, older piano pushed into the corner, one that hadn't been played in years and was used mostly as a shelf for scores and a metronome with a dead battery.

Mattias tried the note a third time, softer, as though gentleness might coax it into behaving. The same dull knock, the same sympathetic rattle from the strings beside it.

"We'll just skip it in rehearsal," Sara said. "Play the line an octave up. Mark it in pencil."

"And the performance?"

"The performance is in five weeks. Somebody will fix it, or we'll fix it, or we'll find another room."

"Or it stays broken and we play around a hole for five weeks and then discover on the night that the hole is still there."

Nobody answered that. Jonas picked up his coffee, blew across the top of it. The fluorescent tube kept flickering, indifferent, on its own schedule. Outside in the corridor a door opened and closed, footsteps passed, someone's phone rang twice and stopped.

Sara opened her case and began tightening her bow, methodical, not hurried. "Bar forty-one," she said. "Let's start there and see how far we get before the hour's up."

Mattias closed the fallboard, not all the way, so the broken hammer wouldn't catch, and sat down anyway.
