Dear Marit,

I'm writing this at the kitchen table, which is the only piece of furniture currently assembled. Everything else is still in the position your brother-in-law's van left it — the couch wedged diagonally across the living room because the doorway turned out to be four centimeters narrower than we measured, the mattress leaning against the wall like it's sulking. I keep meaning to move things and then I sit down instead.

I wanted to write before the thank-you turns into the kind of thing I say once at a dinner and never again. You carried the bookshelves. Both of them, and you know as well as I do that the taller one has that hidden weight to it, the way the wood is denser at the base than it looks. You didn't say anything about it going up the stairs, just adjusted your grip at the second landing and kept walking. I noticed. I don't think I said anything either, which is its own kind of rudeness, but I noticed.

The new place is smaller by about eleven square meters, which sounds like nothing until you try to fit a life into it. I found the box with the mismatched forks yesterday — the ones from your parents' cabin, actually, that you gave me two Christmases ago because you said your mother kept buying new sets and never throwing out the old ones. They're a strange bronze color that doesn't match anything I own. I put them in the drawer anyway.

There's a window in the kitchen that faces a courtyard instead of the street, which I didn't expect to matter but does. No traffic sound. Just someone below practicing scales on what I think is a clarinet, badly, for about twenty minutes each evening around six. I don't mind it. It gives the day a kind of hinge.

I owe you a proper dinner, not the pizza we ate off the floor because there were no chairs yet. I'm thinking sometime after I've located the box with the good pan — I know it's here somewhere, I labeled it, but the labeling system fell apart around box forty and after that everything just says "kitchen" in increasingly impatient handwriting.

Thank you for the boxes, obviously, but also for not asking me halfway up three flights of stairs whether this was really the right decision, moving again so soon after the last time. You just kept carrying things. I've been turning that over — the specific kindness of someone who helps without narrating the help, who doesn't make you account for your choices while your arms are full.

The clarinet's started up again. I should go find the pan.

With more gratitude than I've managed to say out loud,
E.
