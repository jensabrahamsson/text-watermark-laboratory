At five the ovens go quiet. Mikaela pulls the final tray of rye and sets it on the rack by the window, where the glass has fogged from the difference between outside cold and the heat still coming off the stone. She doesn't look at it. She's already moving toward the register, toward the trays of yesterday's cardamom buns that need to be marked down, a red sticker for each one, pressed on with her thumb.

The smell has changed since morning. Then it was yeast and butter, sharp and rising. Now it's flatter, sweeter, mixed in with the smell of the mop bucket someone filled early so it would be ready. Under the counter the last of the cinnamon buns sit in a shallow tray, six of them, their glaze gone tacky rather than wet. A woman comes in for two, and Mikaela uses the tongs even though she doesn't need to, because the tongs are part of closing too, part of not touching things with bare hands this late in the day when her hands feel dry and used up.

The light outside is the color it gets in November at four thirty, which is to say it isn't really light anymore, it's the memory of light, blue-gray and thin. Someone has switched on the string bulbs above the pastry case, and they make small yellow dots on the glass, on the last of the seeded rolls, on the case's chrome trim where fingerprints from the day's rush haven't been wiped yet.

The espresso machine gets its last shots pulled for a man in a wool coat who wants something to drink on his walk home. The hiss is sharp in the emptying room. Then the machine gets purged, backflushed, and it goes silent the way large appliances go silent, not gradually but all at once, mid-breath.

Ester counts the drawer in the back, coins in small stacks that click against each other, notes folded and rubber-banded. She does this every night and every night it takes exactly as long as it takes, no faster for wanting to leave. The radio, which has played the same three stations rotating since morning, is turned down but not off. A song about a river keeps looping in the background of the count.

Outside, someone tries the door out of habit, finds it locked, looks up at the sign, walks on. Inside, the last unsold loaves go into paper bags for the shelter two streets over, a task nobody assigned but somebody always does. The flour on the floor near the ovens has been swept twice today and will need it once more before anyone leaves.

At six the lights along the counter go off in sequence, case first, then the espresso bar, then the ceiling strip above the tables. The string bulbs stay lit a minute longer, because someone forgot, or because someone likes the last minute better with them on.
