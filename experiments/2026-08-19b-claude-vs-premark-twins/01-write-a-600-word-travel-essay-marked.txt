The ferry cuts its engine a hundred meters out, and the silence that follows feels like a held breath. You drift the rest of the way in, the water slapping against the hull in a slower rhythm now, and the town ahead is doing what small harbour towns do at this hour: turning on its lights one at a time, as if someone is walking down the main street flipping switches by hand.

The light itself has gone the colour of weak tea. It sits low over the water and makes long shadows out of the fishing boats moored along the quay, their masts leaning slightly with the current, ropes creaking against the wood. Gulls complain somewhere above, fewer now than an hour ago, settling onto rooftops for the night. A dog barks twice from a garden you can't see and then stops.

The gangway thuds down and you walk off onto stone that's been worn smooth and a little slick by decades of boots and rain. Your bag is heavier than it was in the morning, or feels that way, the strap cutting into your shoulder as you look for a place to put it down. The town smells of diesel and salt and, underneath that, something frying — onions, maybe fish, coming from an open kitchen door somewhere behind the row of houses that face the harbour.

Nobody is in a hurry here. A man in a wool sweater is coiling rope by his boat with the unbothered patience of someone who has done it every evening of his working life. Two women stand talking outside a shuttered shop, their voices carrying flat across the water, and you catch a few words in a language you don't speak, though the tone needs no translation — this is just the end of a day, nothing more.

You find your way up a narrow street that climbs from the harbour, cobbles giving way to cracked asphalt, and the houses lean in close, painted in colours that have faded to something softer than they once were — ochre, a blue gone almost grey, a red like old brick dust. Windows are lit now, curtains not yet drawn, and in one you see a woman setting a table, in another the blue flicker of a television, in a third nothing but a lamp and an empty chair.

The guesthouse, when you find it, has a bell that doesn't work, so you knock, and you wait longer than you expect to before someone comes. The woman who opens the door doesn't smile exactly, but she doesn't not smile either. She hands you a key attached to a wooden fob with a number burned into it and says the room is up the stairs, second on the left, and that breakfast is at eight, not before.

The room smells of clean linen and something like lavender that's been in a drawer too long. The window looks back down toward the harbour, and you stand there for a moment with your bag still on your shoulder, watching the last of the light go out of the sky, the boats now just dark shapes against darker water, a single lamp burning at the end of the pier for no one in particular.

You unpack nothing. You just stand there a while longer, listening to the town settle into its evening the way it must every evening, whether or not anyone from outside happens to be watching.
