Claude responded: Dear Elin,

Dear Elin,

The market was slow today. I don't know if it was the heat or the football match everyone seemed to be watching instead, but by noon I'd sold maybe a third of what I usually do by then. The Andersson stall next to mine did no better — old Bengt spent most of the morning leaning on his crates of turnips, complaining about his knees and asking me if I remembered how it used to be, back when the square was so full you could barely push a pram through it.

I do remember. But I didn't say much. There's no use in agreeing with a man who wants to be agreed with about how everything's worse now.

I brought too many strawberries. You know how I get in June — I always think this will be the year everyone wants strawberries by the kilo, and then I stand there at four o'clock with three flats going soft in the sun. I gave a punnet to the little boy who always comes with his grandmother, the one with the gap in his teeth. He said thank you very seriously, like I'd given him something important, and for a moment that felt like the best sale of the day even though no money changed hands.

I thought about you a lot, actually. There was a woman around your age going stall to stall with a baby strapped to her chest, and she stopped and asked me the same question you used to ask when you were small — why the yellow tomatoes cost more than the red ones. I gave her the same answer I gave you. She didn't believe me either.

I keep thinking I should get out of this stall business, some day when my back finally gives up completely, but then I have days like this — slow, hot, a bit sad in that particular way slow market days are sad — and something in me still doesn't want to let it go. Maybe it's stubbornness. Maybe it's that the stall is the only place left where I talk to more than four people in a day.

Your father would have hated a day like today, all that standing around with nothing to do. He always said the worst thing about the market wasn't the early mornings, it was the waiting. I think I've made peace with the waiting. It gives me time to think, which is either a blessing or a curse depending on what I'm thinking about.

I packed up around five, gave the last of the strawberries to Bengt for his wife, and walked home slower than usual. The square looked strange and empty behind me, the way it always does once the awnings come down.

Call me on Sunday if you have a moment. I want to hear how the new job is going, and whether you've eaten anything other than toast this week.

With love, and a bag of very ripe strawberries I couldn't sell,

Mamma
