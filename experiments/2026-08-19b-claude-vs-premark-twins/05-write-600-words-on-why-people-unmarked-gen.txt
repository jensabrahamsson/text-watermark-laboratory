Thought for 5s

People keep dying houseplants for reasons that have almost nothing to do with plants.

Start with the obvious: buying a plant is buying a future version of yourself. The version who waters on schedule, who notices the drooping leaf before it's too late, who has a light-filled kitchen where things thrive. That person is aspirational, and plants are cheap enough that failing to become them doesn't cost much. A dead fern is a small, forgivable failure — cheaper than a gym membership you didn't use, less humiliating than admitting you never read the books on your shelf.

Then there's the fact that plants ask for almost nothing, which paradoxically makes them easy to forget. A pet cries or scratches at the door. A plant just quietly declines. There's no feedback loop urgent enough to interrupt a busy week, so by the time the leaves go yellow, the damage already has momentum. People don't kill plants through malice or even carelessness exactly — they kill them through the absence of any signal sharp enough to notice.

But I think the deeper thing is that a plant on a windowsill is doing a kind of psychological work independent of whether it lives. It's a stand-in for care itself — evidence, even just to yourself, that you're the kind of person who tries to keep something alive. Buying a plant is an act of optimism about your own attentiveness, and that optimism is worth something even when the follow-through isn't there. You get the feeling of nurturing without necessarily being any good at it, the same way people buy cookbooks they never cook from because the buying is itself a small vote for the life they want.

There's also something about houseplants specifically being low-stakes stand-ins for bigger anxieties about domesticity and control. An apartment can feel sterile, transient, unclaimed — a plant, even a struggling one, makes a space look inhabited. It says someone lives here who thinks about tomorrow. So people replace plants the way they replace resolutions: not because the underlying failure gets fixed, but because the impulse behind buying it was never really about horticulture. It was about wanting the apartment, and the self, to look a certain way.

And I'd guess there's a quieter reason too — plants let people practice a kind of care that doesnn't talk back, doesn't have expectations, doesn't require the vulnerability of a relationship with another person or even a pet. You can fail a plant repeatedly without it ever being a referendum on your character in the way failing a friend would be. It's a low-risk arena for people who want to feel responsible for something but aren't sure they're ready for the weight of something that notices.

None of this is really about botany. Most dying houseplants aren't victims of ignorance — plenty of serial plant-killers know exactly what pothos needs, could recite the watering schedule from memory. They just live in a gap between knowing and doing that has nothing to do with information and everything to do with attention, mood, the hundred other things pulling at a given Tuesday. The plant becomes a small, ongoing referendum on whether you're currently the kind of person who follows through — and most people, most of the time, are inconsistent about that in ways that have nothing to do with plants at all.

So the dead fern on the windowsill isn't really a failure of gardening. It's a pretty accurate little monument to how people actually live — buying the future they want in small, cheap gestures, and then getting distracted by the present, the way everyone does, over and over, with plants and diets and unread books and good intentions generally.
