The Split Trunk

The apple tree had grown for thirty years exactly on the property line between the Berglund and Ek houses, its trunk splitting the boundary the way a knife splits a peach, unevenly and without apology. Nobody had planted it there on purpose. It had simply arrived, a seedling from some bird's mistake, and by the time anyone noticed it mattered, it was too big to move.

"You've taken twelve," Ingrid Berglund said, standing at her fence with a bucket already half full. "I counted."

Torbjörn Ek didn't look up from his own bucket. "The branch leans into my yard. What falls in my yard is mine."

"The branch leans because you cut back the one on your side three years ago. You made it lean."

"I trimmed a hazard. There's a difference."

Ingrid set her bucket down harder than she needed to. Sixty-one years old and she still felt her face go hot the way it had in schoolyards decades ago. "My grandmother planted the roots of that seed, in a manner of speaking. She used to feed the birds that dropped it there."

"Your grandmother didn't own this property. Nobody owned this property in 1961."

"That's not the point."

"Then what is the point, Ingrid?"

She didn't answer right away. Above them the tree shifted in a small wind, and a few leaves came down between the two yards, landing without preference on either side.

"The point," she said finally, "is that you take the good ones before they're ripe. You go out at six in the morning."

"You go out at six in the morning."

"Because you go out at five-thirty."

He almost smiled at that, she could tell, the corner of his mouth twitching before he caught it and turned back into a man defending a fence line. "I'll split it," he said. "Half to you, half to me. Every week."

"You said that in July. I got four apples in July."

"That's because you don't wake up."

"I have a hip."

"You've had a hip for six years and you still beat me to the low branches."

For a moment neither of them said anything, and the argument seemed to run out of road the way arguments sometimes do, not resolved so much as tired. Ingrid picked her bucket back up. Torbjörn examined an apple in his palm, turning it over, checking the far side for the brown soft spot that meant it had fallen too early.

"This one's yours," he said, and held it out over the fence. Not the whole bucket. Just the one, slightly bruised, the kind neither of them would have picked first.

She took it without comment and put it in her own bucket among the others.

"Six o'clock tomorrow," he said. "I'll knock."

"Five-thirty," she said. "Don't lie to yourself."

He shrugged, conceding nothing, and went back to his side of the tree, where the low branches he'd trimmed three years ago had already begun, in the stubborn way of things, to grow back toward the light on her side of the line.
