The platform smells of wet wool and diesel before the train even arrives. Rain has been falling since four in the morning, steady and unhurried, the kind that doesn't so much fall as hang in the air and settle on everything — hair, coat shoulders, the black asphalt gone slick and mirror-dark under the sodium lights that haven't switched off yet at half past seven.

People stand under the narrow overhang of the shelter, packed closer than they'd like, each one holding an umbrella they can't fully open without hitting a stranger. Water drips off the ribs of collapsed umbrellas onto shoes. Someone's trouser cuffs are already soaked through, dark to the ankle. A woman checks her phone, screen fogged faintly from her own breath in the cold. Nobody speaks. The quiet isn't unfriendly, just the ordinary quiet of people who've done this exact walk to this exact platform hundreds of times and have nothing new to say about it.

The train comes in low and grey, headlights smeared by the rain into soft cones. Doors hiss open and there's a brief scramble — not rushed, just efficient, the choreography of people who know where the doors will stop before the train does. Inside, the air is warmer, thick with the smell of damp fabric drying against skin, coffee from paper cups, someone's aftershave. Windows are fully fogged along the bottom third, condensation beading and running in thin trails when the train picks up speed. A few passengers wipe a porthole clear with a sleeve to check the station name, then let it fog over again.

The seats have gone a shade darker where wet coats have sat. A backpack drips a small dark ring onto the floor. Somebody's boots squeak on the rubber matting between carriages. Outside, the city slides past in grey blocks — apartment buildings with lit kitchen windows, a tram crossing at a distance, trees stripped early by autumn and now just black branches holding rain rather than leaves. Streetlights are still on, their orange light doubled in the puddles collecting in every uneven patch of pavement.

At the next stop, the doors open to a gust that brings the smell of the outside in — cold, mineral, faintly of exhaust — before it's pushed back by the warmth of the carriage. New passengers shake umbrellas before folding them, apologize quietly when water sprays a neighbor's sleeve. A child presses a finger to the fogged glass and draws a line, watching it bead and run. Nobody minds. An older man reads a folded newspaper, the paper gone slightly soft and translucent at the corners from the damp on his hands.

The announcement for the central station comes through the speakers, slightly distorted, followed by the same message in English. People start gathering bags, umbrellas, checking phones for the time. The doors open onto the platform and the rain is heavier now, audible even over the train's idling hum, drumming on the glass roof of the station in a steady percussion. Everyone moves at once, umbrellas blooming open in a ragged wave, shoes splashing through puddles that have started pooling at the base of the stairs. The escalator up to the street is crowded, wet umbrellas dripping onto the steps, the air thick with the smell of rain-soaked concrete rising from below. At street level, the city continues exactly as it was — grey, wet, indifferent, already Tuesday, already late morning by the time the sky has any intention of lightening.
