The road drops down toward the water without much warning, one minute pine trees crowding the windows and the next a wide grey sweep of sea opening up below, and the harbour town sits right at the bottom of it like something that slid there and settled. I arrive at that hour when the light goes soft and orange but hasn't yet turned blue, when the shadows of the boat masts stretch out long and thin across the concrete of the quay. There's a particular smell that comes with small harbours at this time of day — diesel and salt and something fried, chips maybe, drifting from a shuttered kiosk that will open again in an hour or two once the evening crowd wanders down.

I park the car somewhere I probably shouldn't and just walk. The town doesn't announce itself the way cities do, with signs and noise and people moving with purpose. Here everything happens slowly, at the speed of water lapping against hulls. A man in a wool sweater coils a rope on the deck of a fishing boat, not looking up as I pass. Somewhere a dog barks twice and then gives up. The houses climb the hill behind the harbour in tiers, painted the colors that these places always seem to choose — faded red, a chalky yellow, a blue that has been rained on so many times it's turned almost grey. Windows are lit now, one by one, small yellow squares against the darkening hillside, and I can see the outlines of rooms, a lamp, a shelf of books, someone's arm reaching to close a curtain.

Down at the water the boats knock gently against the pilings, that hollow wooden sound that seems to belong only to harbours, nowhere else. A woman is selling the last of the day's catch from a cooler set on the back of a truck, and she calls out the price to no one in particular, more habit than hope at this hour. I buy nothing but I stand and watch the exchange when an old man stops, counts coins from a cloth purse, and walks off with a paper parcel under his arm, already unwrapping the corner to check what he's got.

The light keeps failing, slowly, the way it does near water, where the sky seems to hold onto its color longer than it should. The hills across the bay turn from green to a deep flat blue, then to almost black, and the first stars come out before I've even noticed the sun is gone. Somewhere behind me a door opens and closes and a burst of voices and warmth escapes into the cool air, then is shut in again. I follow the sound of it eventually, find a place with three tables and a chalkboard menu, order whatever the woman behind the counter suggests, and sit near the window where I can still see the harbour, now just a scatter of lights on black water, the boats reduced to shapes.

There's a particular kind of tiredness that comes from a day of travel ending in a place like this, not exhaustion exactly but a loosening, as if the body finally understands it can stop moving. I eat slowly. Outside, someone laughs, a rope creaks, a gull complains once and settles. The town has closed its hand around the day and is holding it there, quietly, the way these places always do, long after the rest of the world has let go.
