The till drawer sticks on the way out. It always has. I put my hip against the counter and pull, and the drawer gives with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and there's forty kronor and some centimes from a customer who paid in euros last week because she didn't have anything else.

I count it anyway. Force of habit. Then I zero out the register, print the tape, fold it into the binder where three years of tapes live now, none of them read again after the first week.

The mannequin in the window has been wearing the same green coat for eleven days. I should change it. I look at her a moment — no head, one hand missing at the wrist where the joint broke last spring — and decide the coat can stay one more night. Nobody's coming in now. It's twenty past six and the street outside has that flat evening light that makes everything look like it's already closed.

I start with the rails nearest the door, because that's where people paw through things and leave them crooked. A wool sweater, size L, gets rehung on its hanger the right way round. Someone's tried on a denim jacket and left it inside out on the floor of the fitting room, sleeve still holding the shape of an arm. I turn it back the right way, smooth the shoulders, hang it. There's a coffee ring on the changing room bench. I don't clean it tonight. I'll clean it tomorrow, or the day after, or it'll just become part of the bench, like the ring before it.

The boxes came in this morning — a house clearance, a woman's whole wardrobe from someone who'd died, her daughter said, apologizing for the smell of a closet that had been shut a long time. I sorted maybe a third of it before opening. Wool coats, good ones, from back when coats were made to outlast a person. I hung four of them already. The rest sit in the box by the stockroom door, waiting, and I think about them while I sweep — not sentimentally, just as a fact of tomorrow's work, the way you think about dishes still in the sink.

The broom catches on a button near the till, small and white, off something I'll never identify now. I sweep it into the pan with the dust and a receipt somebody dropped and a single earring, gold-colored, cheap.

Lights off in the fitting rooms first, then the back, then the floor lamps by the books-and-records corner nobody asked for but that does better than the shoes. The street light through the window is enough to see the register drawer sitting open the way it's supposed to sit overnight, empty, so nobody breaks the lock for nothing.

I lock the door from outside, test it twice, the way I always do, though I couldn't say what I think might happen if I only tested it once. Down the street the pizza place is still lit. I don't go in. I just go home, tired the ordinary way, the way you'd expect.
