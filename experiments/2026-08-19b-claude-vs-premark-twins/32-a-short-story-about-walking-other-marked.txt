Thought for 1s

The dogs come in a rotation Marta knows by smell before she sees the leashes. Tuesday is the greyhound, thin as a coat hanger, who walks like he's apologizing for something. Wednesday is the two terriers who hate each other in a way that looks like love if you don't know better. Friday is the old lab, Otis, who she takes last because he takes the longest and she doesn't mind anymore, not after two years of this.

The park is the same each time and different each time. Same gravel path around the pond, same iron benches bolted down so nobody steals them, same stand of birches losing bark in long white curls. But the light changes, and the people change, and sometimes there's a saxophone player near the north entrance and sometimes there isn't, and she's stopped trying to predict which.

She started walking dogs after she left the accounting job, not because she couldn't find another one but because she didn't try very hard to. Her sister said this was a phase. It has been a phase for two years and four months. She keeps a spreadsheet of clients, rates, notes on which dog pulls on the leash and which one needs the harness clipped in front, and the spreadsheet is more organized than anything she made at the accounting job, which she thinks about sometimes without drawing any conclusion from it.

The greyhound's owner is a man who works from home and pays her through an app without ever coming to the door. She's met him twice in two years, both times by accident, both times he seemed startled to find a person attached to the name on his phone. The terriers belong to a couple who are always mid-argument when she arrives, the kind of argument conducted entirely in tone, no actual words exchanged, and she takes the dogs before the tone can resolve into something worse.

Otis belongs to a woman named Ingrid who is eighty-one and broke her hip in March and has not, as far as Marta can tell, fully accepted that this means she can't walk him herself anymore. Ingrid stands in the doorway when Marta comes to collect him and gives instructions about the route as though Marta hasn't walked this exact loop three hundred times. Left at the pond, not right. He doesn't like the right side, too many geese. Marta says of course, same as always, and takes the leash, and Ingrid watches from the doorway until they're out of sight, which takes longer than it used to because Marta has learned to walk slow enough that Ingrid can watch for as long as she wants to watch.

Today it's Otis, and the light is doing the thing where it comes low through the birches and turns the whole path gold for about twenty minutes before it doesn't anymore. Otis stops to smell the same tree he always smells. Marta waits. Somewhere behind them a dog she doesn't know is barking at something she can't see. Otis doesn't react to it. Neither does she.
