The tree stood exactly on the property line, or close enough that no one had ever bothered to measure it properly. Bengt had lived on the north side for thirty-one years. Karin had moved into the south house four years ago, after her divorce, with a moving van and a cat and a folder of documents she kept in a drawer in the kitchen.

The apples that year were heavy and early. By the second week of August they were dropping onto both lawns, thudding into the grass, splitting where they hit the flagstones on Karin's side.

"You could rake your half," Bengt said, over the fence, in the tone of a man stating something obvious.

"It's not my half. It's not anyone's half. It's a tree." Karin had a rake in her hand already, though she wasn't using it.

"The trunk's on my side."

"The trunk is on the line. I looked it up. There's a law about trees on boundaries."

"There's no law that says you can pick all the good ones off the branches that hang over your garden and leave the rot for me."

"I haven't picked anything. I don't even like apples like this, they're too sour for eating, you have to cook them."

"Then cook them."

"I don't want thirty kilos of apples, Bengt."

He crouched down and picked one up, turned it over, showed her the brown bruise on its underside as if it were evidence in a trial. "See, this is what happens. They just sit there."

"So rake them."

"Why should I rake the ones that fall on my side from branches that grew out over your garden because nobody's pruned this thing in a decade?"

"You could prune it."

"It's not my—" He stopped. Neither of them finished the sentence, because finishing it required saying whose tree it actually was, and neither of them knew, and the not knowing had become, over four years, a kind of ongoing low-grade fact of life, like the noise from the road or the neighbour's dog two houses down.

Karin went inside and came back out with a tape measure, the metal kind that snaps back and stings your fingers if you're not careful. She measured from the fence post to the trunk. Forty-two centimetres onto Bengt's side, she announced.

"That doesn't account for the survey markers being off," Bengt said. "The whole fence was put up wrong in the eighties, everyone on this street knows that."

"Then whose fault is that?"

"Not mine."

The cat came out and sat on Karin's side of the fence, in the shade, licking one paw, uninterested in either of them or the apples or the tape measure now coiled loosely in Karin's hand.

Bengt picked up three more apples, set them in a neat row on the flat top of the fence post, like a small offering or a small insult, it was hard to say which. Then he went inside and came back with his own rake and began working his side of the lawn in long even strokes, saying nothing else, while Karin stood watching him for a moment before going back to her half, which was not a half, of the raking that neither of them had agreed to call fair.
