Ice fishing, as I understand it, is the sport of relocating the discomforts of winter to a more remote and less forgiving location, then calling it recreation.

I went once. A friend invited me with the kind of enthusiasm that should have been a warning sign — the enthusiasm of someone who has forgotten, or never fully processed, how cold it actually is. He talked about it the way people talk about saunas or cold plunges, like suffering was the point and I'd thank him later. I did not thank him later.

We drove out before sunrise, which felt unnecessary given that the fish, presumably, were not going anywhere. The lake was a flat white nothing, and walking onto it required a small act of faith I wasn't sure I had. Somewhere under my boots was water, and above the water was ice, and the ice was, I was assured, "plenty thick." Plenty is not a measurement.

He drilled a hole with an auger, which was the single most impressive part of the day — a genuinely satisfying machine, chewing down through ice in a spray of white shavings. I remember thinking: this is the good part. This is the part where something is happening. Everything after the hole was drilled was, in a sense, waiting.

We sat on overturned buckets beside a hole roughly the size of a dinner plate, and dropped a line into it, and waited for a fish to make a decision. I had pictured a heated shanty, or at least a wind block. There was a low fold-out tent, technically, but the cold came in sideways anyway, at ankle and collar height, in the gap between glove and sleeve, in the two centimeters of exposed neck I hadn't accounted for. I have never been more aware of my own extremities as discrete, separately-suffering body parts.

My friend caught something — a small perch, I think, though by then my judgment on all matters had narrowed to "cold" and "less cold." He held it up like a trophy. I nodded with the enthusiasm of a man whose face had stopped fully cooperating with him an hour earlier. I caught nothing, unless you count a profound, structural understanding of my own thermal limits.

What surprised me most wasn't the cold itself but how much of ice fishing is just sitting inside it, doing nothing, on purpose. No casting, no reeling, no motion to generate even the illusion of warmth. Just stillness, and a hole, and the faint hope that something beneath you has decided today is the day.

We left before noon. I remember the car heater as one of the more transcendent experiences of my adult life. My friend talked the whole drive back about how we should go again sometime, next winter maybe, and I made a sound that could be interpreted as agreement, the way you agree to things you have no intention of doing, purely to end the conversation and get closer to the heater vents.
