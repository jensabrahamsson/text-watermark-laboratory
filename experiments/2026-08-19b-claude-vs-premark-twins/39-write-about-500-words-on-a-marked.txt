The ferry was supposed to leave at ten past three, and by half past three the sign at the dock still said DELAYED, which everyone had already worked out for themselves from the way the water looked. It wasn't rough, exactly. It was the kind of wind that came in gusts you could see coming, a darker patch moving across the sound, and then the little boats tied up along the pier knocked against their fenders and settled again.

There were maybe nine people waiting. A woman with two dogs on short leads, both of them lying flat against the concrete like they'd done this before. A man in a hi-vis jacket who kept checking his phone and then putting it away without having done anything with it. Two teenagers sharing earbuds, one each. An older couple who had brought their own thermos and were passing the cup back and forth without saying much.

The ferry itself sat maybe eighty meters out, engine idling, not committing to the crossing. You could see the captain in the wheelhouse, a shape more than a person, and every so often the boat would turn a little into the wind and then let itself drift back, like it was testing something. Someone said it was about the gangway, that they could handle the crossing fine but didn't want to try loading cars in this and have the ramp swing.

A man from the terminal came out in an orange coat and said maybe twenty more minutes, we're watching it, and nobody asked him anything because there wasn't anything to ask. The wind was the wind. He'd know when it eased before any of them would.

The dogs got up eventually and the woman walked them in a slow circle around the parking area, and one of the teenagers went to buy something from the machine by the ticket office and came back with two bags of chips, gave one to the other without asking. The older man topped up his wife's coffee and she said something and he laughed, short, and looked back out at the water.

Somewhere around the forty-minute mark the boat straightened out and started moving in, slower than usual, angling itself against the wind rather than with it. You could hear the change in the engine before you could see the change in the boat. The gangway crew came out onto the pier in their vests, hands in pockets until they weren't.

Loading took longer than normal because they were doing the cars one at a time instead of waving them on in a line, and the ramp made a different sound going up than it usually did, a kind of complaint. But it went. People filed on, found seats near the windows out of habit rather than need, and the dogs lay down again on the floor of the cabin like the concrete pier had just moved indoors.

The crossing itself was nothing. Twenty minutes of grey water and a horizon that tilted a little more than it should have. Nobody mentioned the wait once they were moving.
