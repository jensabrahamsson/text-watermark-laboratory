The ferry cuts its engine a kilometer out, and the silence that follows is its own kind of arrival. You feel the boat settle into a slower rhythm, riding the last of its own wake, and the harbour town ahead resolves itself out of the blue-grey haze the way a photograph does in a darkroom tray, one detail at a time. First the church spire, black against a sky the color of a bruise healing. Then the cluster of rooftops, red and ochre, huddled close as if for warmth. Then, lower down, the lights.

They come on one by one, or so it seems, though of course they don't; it's only that your eye finds them in sequence, a window here, a lamppost there, until the whole waterfront is strung with small yellow fires and the dusk around them deepens by comparison. There's a particular quality to arriving somewhere at this hour, neither day nor night, when the place hasn't yet decided what kind of evening it will have. The gulls are still working the harbour, wheeling and complaining over some scrap near the fish market stalls, but their voices carry differently now, flattened by the cooling air.

The boat noses in alongside a stone quay slick with the day's rain, and a man in an orange coat catches the rope without much interest, as though he has caught ten thousand ropes before and expects to catch ten thousand more. Nobody hurries. This seems to be the town's first lesson, delivered before you've even set foot on its ground: here, things happen at the pace they happen. You disembark down a ramp that clangs and gives slightly underfoot, and the smell reaches you before anything else does — diesel and brine and something frying, onions maybe, from a window above a shuttered chandlery.

The streets climbing back from the water are narrow enough that two people must turn sideways to pass, and they're paved with stones worn smooth and faintly silver by centuries of exactly this kind of foot traffic, travelers arriving in the last light, looking for a bed and a meal and something to explain, even briefly, why they came. A cat regards you from a doorstep with total indifference. Somewhere a shutter is being latched for the night, the sound of it wood against wood, unhurried.

What strikes you, walking up from the harbour, is how much of the town seems built for this hour specifically, for dusk rather than noon. The cafés have strung their outdoor lights already, small bulbs looping between buildings, though it isn't dark enough yet to need them; they burn a little pale and apologetic against the remaining daylight, waiting for their moment. An old man sits outside one of them with a glass of something amber, watching the harbour the way you might watch a fire, not for information but for company. He nods as you pass. You nod back, and it feels like the first real exchange of the trip, wordless and complete.

By the time you find your room, the window looking down over the water, the last boats are coming in under lights of their own, small green and red eyes moving across the dark surface. The harbour town has finished its transformation; day is gone, and what's left is intimate, self-contained, a little theatrical in the way small places are when the light goes soft and forgiving. You sit for a while without doing anything at all, which turns out to be exactly the right way to arrive somewhere: slowly, and without insisting on understanding it yet.
