The first time, I killed it with kindness. That's the only way I can describe it. I fed the starter like it was a pet I was anxious about, twice a day, precise little spoonfuls of flour and water, and I think the poor thing never got hungry enough to build any character. It bubbled politely for a week, the way a stranger laughs at your joke out of courtesy, and then one morning it just sat there, flat and faintly sour-smelling in a way that wasn't the good sour, and I knew. I baked with it anyway, out of stubbornness, and got something that was less bread than a dense beige brick with the structural integrity of wet sand. My wife was kind about it. She said "rustic." I said nothing and ate two slices with an unreasonable amount of butter, mostly to prove a point to myself that I no longer remember the content of.

The second failure was the opposite mistake, which I found almost funny in retrospect, the way you find your own patterns funny once they've cost you something twice. I overcorrected. I neglected the starter for four days because I'd read somewhere that discomfort builds resilience, in starters as in people, which is the kind of thing that sounds wise until you actually try it and get a jar of what I can only describe as regret. It smelled like nail polish remover. I fed it back to health, or thought I did, and made the dough too wet because some voice in my head insisted wet dough was the mark of a serious baker, and when I went to shape it, it simply spread across the counter like it had given up on the idea of being a loaf at all. It baked into something that looked, structurally, like a large flat mistake. My daughter, who is at the age where honesty is not yet tempered by strategy, asked if it was supposed to look like that.

What I've come to think, having failed at this twice in fairly opposite directions, is that sourdough punishes both anxiety and neglect equally, which feels almost moral, like it's designed to catch you wherever your particular flaw happens to sit. Too much attention and you smother whatever wildness the yeast needs to do its work. Too little and it curdles into something resentful. There's no safe middle you can find by formula, because the starter is a living thing responding to a kitchen that changes temperature by the hour, and no amount of my usual instinct for systems and control seems to help here. I keep thinking about it anyway, the way you think about a person you've misjudged twice — wondering what you missed, whether the third attempt will finally be the one where you stop trying to manage it and just pay attention to what it's actually doing instead of what you assume it should be doing. I haven't tried again yet. The jar is still in the fridge, dormant, faintly judgmental, waiting to see if I've learned anything.
