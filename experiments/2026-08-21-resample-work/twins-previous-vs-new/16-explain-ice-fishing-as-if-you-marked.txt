Ice fishing, as I understand it, is the practice of sitting very still on a frozen lake so that the cold has time to find every seam in your clothing. I went once, invited by a colleague who described it as "peaceful" in the tone people use when they mean something else. We drove out before sunrise, which felt less like an outdoor pursuit and more like a rendezvous with a body of water that had recently decided to become solid.

The lake itself was unglamorous. No postcard blue, just a flat grey-white expanse that could have been mistaken for a very large parking lot if not for the auger someone produced from the truck bed with real ceremony, as though it were a violin case. The drilling took longer than I expected and produced a hole roughly the size of a dinner plate, which struck me as an absurd aperture through which to expect anything meaningful to happen. My colleague assured me that fish would find this hole significant. The fish, as it turned out, kept their opinions to themselves for the next four hours.

What I remember most is the specific geography of cold. It starts in the feet, which is expected, but then does something unfair: it moves upward through the knees and settles in the lower back, where it sits like an uninvited guest who has found the good chair. My hands, wrapped around a rod that occasionally twitched with what I was told was "current" rather than "fish," went from cold to numb to a third state I hadn't previously encountered, somewhere between numb and simply absent. I remember thinking that the tent — we had a tent, a small nylon thing that flapped in a wind I couldn't feel but could certainly hear — was less a shelter than a suggestion.

There was a thermos. The thermos became, within the first hour, the emotional center of the entire outing. Coffee that would have been unremarkable anywhere else achieved something close to religious significance out there. I have never before or since experienced gratitude of that particular intensity for a beverage.

We caught one fish. Not me — my colleague, who reeled it up with the weary satisfaction of a man confirming a hypothesis rather than discovering a miracle. It was small, silvery, and looked, I thought, almost as unenthusiastic about the whole affair as I was. He held it up for a photo I don't believe either of us ever looked at again, then let it go back down through the dinner-plate hole into whatever business fish have under ice.

We packed up not long after. My feet did not feel entirely mine again until sometime after lunch, in a car with the heat turned up to a setting I would normally consider hostile. I don't think I understood, until that day, that "peaceful" and "uncomfortable" could occupy the exact same four hours without any contradiction at all. I haven't gone back. I think about it fondly anyway, which is its own kind of strange arithmetic.
