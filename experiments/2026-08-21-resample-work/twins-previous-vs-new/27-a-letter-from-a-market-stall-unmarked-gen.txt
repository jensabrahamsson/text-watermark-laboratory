Dear Elin,

Slow today. I mean properly slow — by eleven I'd sold two jars of the plum jam and a bunch of dahlias to a woman who then came back twenty minutes later to ask if I'd take less for them. I said no. She bought them anyway, which tells you something about how these negotiations usually go, though I couldn't say what.

The rain held off, at least. It sat up there all morning deciding, the sky that flat grey colour like the underside of a mushroom, and I kept looking at it between customers the way you'd watch a dog that might or might not bite. Bengt two stalls down had his usual crowd for the smoked fish, there's always a queue for Bengt, and I stood there rearranging the same twelve jars of jam into different patterns just to have something to do with my hands. Pyramid. Then a row. Then the pyramid again.

Your father came by at half twelve with coffee in the thermos, the good one, and a rundstycke he'd already eaten half of in the car, he said, because he was hungry and I wasn't there to stop him. He sat on the upturned crate for a while and told me about the gutter on the Andersson house, which apparently is his problem now though I don't fully understand why. I didn't ask. Some things you let him have.

I did sell the rest of the courgettes eventually, all at once, to a man buying for what he said was a restaurant though he had the look of someone buying for himself and being ashamed of how many courgettes one person could want. Seven kronor a kilo, I didn't even negotiate, I just wanted the crate empty so I'd have less to carry back.

By three the light was doing that thing it does in September where it isn't dark but it's given up trying to be anything else, and I started packing down early. Nobody minds this time of year. The other stallholders were doing the same, that quiet rustle of tarps and crates all down the row, and Karin waved from hers, held up a bag of something, chanterelles I think, and mimed a question I didn't have the energy to answer so I just gave her a thumbs up and she seemed satisfied with that.

I keep thinking I should stop doing Saturdays in autumn, that it isn't worth the four-thirty start for two jars of jam and a strange woman's dahlia negotiations. But then I think of you asking about it, whenever you call, always the same question — how was the market — and I don't know what I'd tell you if the answer was nothing, if there wasn't a stall to have been slow at all.

Bring the boys next weekend if the weather's decent. There's a doll's-house chair somewhere in father's workshop I've been meaning to give Freja, small enough she could lose it under the sofa within the hour, but she'd like it while it lasts.

Love,
Mor
