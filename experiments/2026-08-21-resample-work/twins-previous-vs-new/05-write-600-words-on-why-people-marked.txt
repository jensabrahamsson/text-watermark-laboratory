There's a particular kind of grief that comes from a dead pothos on a windowsill, and it's out of proportion to the plant's actual value, which is to say a few dollars and a pot of dirt. And yet people keep buying them. They keep bringing home the same fern that killed its three predecessors, setting it in the same north-facing corner that gets forty minutes of weak light a day, and watering it on a schedule dictated by guilt rather than the plant's actual needs. This isn't stupidity. It's something else.

Part of it is that a houseplant is one of the last living things you're allowed to fail at privately. You can kill a plant and no one has to know. There's no vet bill, no shelter to call, no small creature looking at you with confusion when you get it wrong. The stakes are low enough that you can keep trying, keep believing that this time you'll be the kind of person who remembers to check the soil, who notices the yellowing leaf before it's a metaphor for something larger. A plant lets you rehearse care without the terror of consequence. Which is maybe why it stings more than it should when they die anyway — you'd built a version of yourself around the low-stakes attempt, and the plant just went and proved that version wrong.

There's also something almost tender about the specific delusion of it. People don't buy a plant because they've done the research on its light requirements. They buy it because it looked good in the shop, because someone at a dinner party had one, because a corner of the apartment felt unfinished in a way that furniture couldn't fix. The plant is less a living organism at that moment and more a piece of the self they're trying to build — the self who has a slower morning routine, who notices things, who isn't just surviving their own week. Buying the plant is aspirational. Keeping it alive is a separate and much harder problem that gets solved, if at all, only after the aspiration has already been satisfied by the purchase itself.

And then there's the matter of what a dying plant actually asks of you, which is attention on a timescale most people have stopped practicing. A plant doesn't announce its distress the way a phone notification does. It just gradually, quietly, changes color. You have to already be looking to notice. In a life organized around things that ping and buzz and demand immediate response, a plant's silence reads as an absence of urgency, and so it gets deprioritized right up until the leaves are unmistakably brown, at which point it's too late and the whole thing becomes proof of something the person already suspected about themselves — that they're bad at slow attention, at the unglamorous maintenance of things that don't complain.

But I think the deeper reason people keep doing this, keep replacing the corpse with an identical plant from the same shop, is that the alternative is worse. The alternative is an apartment with nothing green in it at all, a windowsill that has given up. There's a stubbornness in continuing to try that isn't really about the plants. It's a refusal to let one measure of incompetence become a permanent fact about who you are. So the pothos dies, and three weeks later there's a new one in the same pot, and it isn't really optimism, exactly. It's closer to a small, recurring act of defiance against the idea that you are only ever going to be the kind of person things die around.
