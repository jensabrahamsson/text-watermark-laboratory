Dear Elin,

Your mother tells me you have started sailing on Wednesdays, small boats out of the marina by the school, and that you don't much care for it when the fog comes in. I want to tell you some things about fog, since I spent forty-one years on the water and most of what I know about it isn't in any book.

The first thing is that fog is not weather, not really. Weather has a mood to it, storms build and pass, rain has a rhythm. Fog just arrives and sits there like a piece of furniture nobody ordered. You can be running full visibility one minute, and then it comes down off the water itself, not from the sky, and you're in a room with no walls.

I remember a crossing in October, must have been 1987 or thereabouts, taking the afternoon run from Öckerö with maybe sixty passengers, and the fog came in so fast that the bow of the boat disappeared before the wheelhouse did. You could hear people talking on the foredeck but you couldn't see them. I had radar, of course, we weren't animals, but radar tells you where things are, not what they are. A shape on the screen could be a rock, a buoy, another boat, a whale. You have to know your water so well that the instrument becomes a second confirmation rather than a first guess.

What people don't understand about fog, until they've sat in it for hours, is how much it changes sound. Everything gets closer and farther at the same time. A foghorn from two kilometers away sounds like it's coming from inside your own skull, and a boat passing thirty meters off your beam can sound like it's already gone. I taught myself to trust my ears more than my eyes on those days, which is a strange thing to have to teach an old sailor's body.

Your grandmother used to wait for me on the dock at Hönö when I ran the afternoon route, and on the fog days she would already know, before the horn sounded, before anyone radioed ahead, that I was close. She said she could feel it. I never asked her to explain that and she never offered to.

There is a particular kind of patience fog requires. You cannot fight it and you cannot hurry through it. The ferry that runs at eighteen knots in clear water runs at six in a real fog, sometimes four, and every captain who has tried to make up the time has a story about it, and not all of those stories end the way you'd want. I throttled back more times than I can count and took the complaints from tourists who didn't understand why we were crawling past islands they couldn't see anyway.

I don't have a neat lesson to leave you with about the fog days, Elin. I know you'd like one, you've always liked things that resolve. But some of it just sits with you the way it sat on the water — you go through it slower, you listen harder, and eventually you come out the other side with the shore looking exactly the way you remembered it, except now you know it was there the whole time.

Sail carefully on Wednesdays. Trust your ears.

Morfar
