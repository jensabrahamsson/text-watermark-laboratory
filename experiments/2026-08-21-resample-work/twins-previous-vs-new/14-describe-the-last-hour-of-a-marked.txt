The last hour smells different from the rest of the day. Not of fresh bread anymore — that ended around three — but of flour dust settling, of butter cooling into the cracks of the counter, of coffee left too long on the burner going bitter and thick. Elsa turns down the oven though it stays warm for hours after, a low animal heat you feel through your socks if you stand too close to the vents.

The shelves have gone spare. What's left: a heel of rye, three cardamom buns gone slightly grey at the edges, a tray of broken meringues nobody will pay full price for. She moves them into the discount basket by the register without ceremony. A man comes in for the rye, weighs it in his hand like he's checking it's real, pays in coins.

Outside the window the light is doing that early-evening thing particular to October in Lerum, thin and sideways, catching the flour still hanging in the air near the ceiling fan. It looks almost like snow if you don't think about it too hard. The street is quieter now. A bus passes with three people in it. Someone's dog waits tied to the bike rack, patient, watching the door.

Elsa wipes the counter in long strokes, and where the cloth passes the wood goes dark then lightens again as it dries. She's done this enough years that her hands know the counter's dips and burns without her eyes checking. Under the counter, the radio plays something with no words, an accordion maybe, turned low enough that it disappears under the sound of the cloth and the occasional bell above the door.

A regular comes in near closing, the same woman most days, always at 5:40, always for a cinnamon bun she eats standing at the counter rather than taking home. Today there are none left. She takes the last cardamom bun instead, unbothered, and eats half of it before she's even out the door, brushing sugar off her coat with the back of her glove.

The register gets counted. Coins first, sorted into their tubes, then the notes smoothed flat and stacked. Elsa writes the total in a notebook that's mostly full of crossed-out numbers from other days. The chalkboard menu gets wiped, though she leaves tomorrow's soup written faintly underneath, not quite erased, so she doesn't have to write it twice.

Chairs go up onto tables, legs pointing at the ceiling like something overturned. The mop bucket fills with water gone grey almost immediately. She works from the back toward the door, so she's never trapped in her own cleaning.

At the very end she stands for a moment with her coat half on, one arm in one arm out, looking at the room the way you might look at someone sleeping. Then the lights, then the lock, the small double-check of the handle that she does without thinking, and the street takes the sound of her footsteps and doesn't give anything back.
