The alarm had come in at 11:40, a smoke detector at the Lindqvist farm on Åsvägen, and by the time they got the truck out there it was clear the smoke was from a neighbor burning brush, upwind, nothing more. They idled past the farmhouse once, lights still turning, and then Torbjörn radioed it in as a false alarm and turned the truck around.

Back at the station it was Torbjörn and Elin left to close up, the others peeling off to their cars in the gravel lot, headlights swinging across the corrugated door before they pulled out one by one. Elin coiled the last length of hose while Torbjörn checked the water level gauge, tapping it once with two fingers the way he always did, though neither of them knew what the tap was supposed to tell him.

"Lindqvist's going to owe us a coffee," Elin said.

"He'll say it was the neighbor's fault."

"It was the neighbor's fault."

Torbjörn grunted and hung the last coat on its hook, the row of yellow turnout gear swaying slightly under the fluorescent light. The station smelled the way it always did at this hour, diesel and rubber and the machine oil they used on the ladder hinges. Somebody had left the radio on low in the back office, a Swedish pop station playing something from the nineties, and Elin went to switch it off.

She checked the logbook was filled in — time of call, time of return, unit dispatched, outcome — and slid it back into its drawer under the counter. Torbjörn was already at the main switch panel, running through the sequence he ran through every time: compressor off, block heater on for the engine since the nights were getting cold now, exterior lights set to the dusk-till-dawn timer.

"You want the door or the gate?" he asked.

"Door."

He went out to the gravel lot to swing the chain-link gate shut and loop the padlock through, while Elin pulled the bay door down on its tracks, the metal clattering into place with a sound that always seemed too loud for how empty the lot was. She turned the key in the side lock and tested the handle twice, out of habit more than doubt.

Outside, the sky had cleared, and there was a thin line of pink still along the ridge to the west though it was near midnight. Torbjörn's car started with a cough. Elin stood a moment by her own car, keys in hand, looking back at the station — a squat brick building with the town's crest painted crookedly above the door, someone's amateur brushwork from years ago, before either of them had joined.

"Same time Thursday," Torbjörn called through his open window.

"Unless something burns first."

"Nothing's burning tonight."

He pulled out, gravel crunching under the tires, and she watched his taillights take the turn onto the main road before she got into her own car. The dashboard clock read 11:58. She sat for a moment with the engine running, hands on the wheel, not yet ready to drive, though there was nothing left to wait for.
