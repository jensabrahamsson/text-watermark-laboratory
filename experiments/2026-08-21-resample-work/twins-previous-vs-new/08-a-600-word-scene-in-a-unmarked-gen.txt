Claude responded: The shop smelled of dust and radiator heat.

The shop smelled of dust and radiator heat. Elin had already flipped the sign to CLOSED but left the door unlocked, because the man in the grey coat was still working his way along the poetry shelf, one finger dragging spine to spine, and she didn't have the nerve to interrupt him.

"Ten minutes," she called, not unkindly, and went back to counting the till.

The coins made their small sounds. Outside, the street lamp buzzed and flickered against the wet glass, throwing a smear of orange across the window display — a stack of Penguin classics arranged in a pyramid that had taken her twenty minutes and that nobody, in three weeks, had touched.

"Do you have any Tranströmer that isn't the Bly translation?" the man asked, without turning around.

"There's a Robin Fulton somewhere. Try the blue shelf, second from the end."

He moved without hurry. She liked that about him, though she'd only spoken to him twice before this. He handled the books like they weighed something.

She found the note in the register — a five-pound note folded into a paper crane, left there by a child three days ago, some kind of joke or offering, she still didn't know which — and set it aside again, the way she always did, not ready to spend it and not ready to throw it out either.

"Got it," the man said. He held up a thin paperback, cover gone soft and grey at the corners. "How much?"

"Two fifty. Might be three, let me check the pencil mark."

He brought it to the counter. Up close he smelled of cold air and cigarettes, though she'd never seen him smoke. His hands were bare despite the weather.

"Three pounds," she said, reading the faint number inside the cover. "Sorry."

"That's fine." He didn't reach for his wallet yet. He was looking past her, at the shelf behind the till where she kept the books not for sale — the ones with inscriptions, or missing pages, or names crossed out and written over in another hand. "You never sell those?"

"No."

"Why keep them, then?"

"Because someone else already decided they mattered." She shrugged. "Feels wrong to undecide that for them."

He almost smiled. Paid with exact change, three coins set down one at a time like he was placing them on a board. She put the paperback in a paper bag though he hadn't asked for one, folded the top twice, the way her father used to.

"You're here most evenings," he said.

"Someone has to be."

"Not an answer."

"No," she agreed, "it isn't."

He took the bag and didn't push further, which she appreciated more than she let herself show. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at the pyramid of Penguins in the window, tilted now from where he must have brushed it earlier.

"You should let people take those apart," he said. "The pyramid. Nobody buys a wall."

"Maybe I like the wall."

"Maybe." He pulled the door open. The bell above it gave its one note, thin and a little flat, the way it had sounded for as long as she'd worked there. Cold air came in ahead of him and then he was gone, his shape passing the window and breaking up in the rain-streaked light.

Elin straightened the top book on the pyramid without taking the rest down. Then she turned off the lamp over the poetry shelf, left the one behind the till burning a while longer, and went back to counting what was left in the drawer.
