The Line

The washing line ran the length of the courtyard, strung between two iron hooks bolted into brick that had gone soft and orange with age. It belonged to no one and everyone. Mrs. Halvorsen had used it since 1994, when she and her husband moved into the ground-floor flat, and she still thought of it as hers, though she never said this to anyone, not even to her husband before he died.

The Kowalski family took the west end now. Three children meant washing every second day, and their clothes went up in a particular order — smallest first, then school uniforms, then the father's shirts, ironed and pinned by the shoulders so they wouldn't crease. Mrs. Halvorsen pinned her things by the hem. She thought the Kowalski method wasted pegs.

In August, a new tenant moved into the flat above the Kowalskis. A young man, maybe thirty, who worked nights at something involving a laptop bag and headphones around his neck at all hours. He appeared at the line on a Tuesday morning with a single black bin bag of laundry and no idea that the eastern third belonged, by unspoken agreement going back years, to whoever had lived there before the Petrossians moved to Malmö.

He pinned up two towels, a bedsheet, and seven identical grey t-shirts. Mrs. Halvorsen watched from her kitchen window and said nothing.

By Thursday the sheet was still there. He worked nights; he slept through the mornings when the sun would have dried it. Mrs. Kowalski, hanging her own sheets, had to reach around it, and one corner came loose and dragged along the cobblestones, picking up a long grey smear.

"Somebody should say something," Mrs. Kowalski said to Mrs. Halvorsen over the low fence that didn't really divide anything.

"Somebody should," Mrs. Halvorsen agreed, and continued pinning.

Neither of them said anything. The sheet stayed up another day, then vanished sometime in the night, taken in without either woman witnessing it. The young man began leaving a note instead, folded and weighted under a stone on the low wall: back by six, feel free to move if needed. The handwriting was small and slanted backward.

Nobody moved his things. They worked around him, the way water works around a stone in a streambed, without deciding to.

In September he started coming down earlier, before his shift, to collect his washing while it was still damp and finish drying it on a rack indoors. He said good morning to Mrs. Halvorsen the third time this happened, and she said good morning back, and asked whether the machine on the second floor still ate coins without dispensing the extra rinse cycle it promised. He said he didn't know, he used the launderette on Brogatan.

"Waste of money," she said.

He didn't argue with her. He just shrugged, in a way that suggested he'd already spent the money and there was no undoing it now.

The line stayed where it was, sagging slightly more each year under its own weight and the weight of what was hung on it. Nobody re-strung it. Nobody suggested a schedule, or a rotation, or a sign-up sheet taped to the door of the laundry room, though someone, at some point, must have thought of it.
