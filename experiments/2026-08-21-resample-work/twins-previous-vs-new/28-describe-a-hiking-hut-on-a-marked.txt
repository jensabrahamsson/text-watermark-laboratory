The fog had been sitting over the ridge since noon, and by three it had settled into the valley like something that had decided to stay. The hut appeared without warning — first a shape, grey on grey, then a roofline, then the actual building, low and turf-topped, its timber darkened by decades of rain. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin unwilling line, bent sideways before it had gone a metre, swallowed almost immediately.

Inside, the air was different — dry, close, thick with woodsmoke and wet wool. A kettle ticked on the iron stove, not yet boiling. Someone had left a pair of boots by the door, laces still crusted with mud, and the smell of them mixed with pine resin from the logs stacked beside the hearth. Condensation ran down the inside of the single window in irregular lines, blurring the world outside into shapes that might have been trees or might have been nothing.

The floorboards had a particular give to them near the table, a soft creak that came from the same spot every time, worn in by years of boots crossing to the bench. The table itself bore the marks of previous visitors — initials cut shallow into the wood, a coffee ring gone permanent, a long scratch that might have been a knife test or might have been an accident with a ski edge. A logbook sat under a stone paperweight, its pages swollen slightly from damp, filled with handwriting in three or four languages, dates going back further than seemed plausible for a book so thin.

Outside, sound behaved strangely. Footsteps on the gravel path arrived without warning, as if the person walking had only just been invented, and voices carried oddly — a laugh from what should have been fifty metres away sounded as though it came from just past the wall. A rope, strung between two posts to mark the trail, had gone slack and dark with moisture, and drops fell from it at uneven intervals, striking the stone step below in a rhythm no one could have predicted.

The cold came in waves rather than steadily — a draft finding the gap under the door, then retreating, then finding it again. Whoever had lit the stove had not yet managed to push the warmth past the first few metres of the room, so the corners stayed cool, the benches along the far wall still holding the chill of the morning. A candle, unlit, sat in a jar on the windowsill, wick slightly bent, waiting for evening, which today might arrive without any visible change in the light — the fog simply deepening from white to grey to something closer to black.

Someone had written a note on a torn page and pinned it to the doorframe: gone to fetch water, back soon. The ink had smudged where a damp finger had touched it. Beyond the window, the shapes that might have been trees held still, betraying nothing, and the smoke went on bending sideways into the white, thinning until it was only a smell, and then not even that.
