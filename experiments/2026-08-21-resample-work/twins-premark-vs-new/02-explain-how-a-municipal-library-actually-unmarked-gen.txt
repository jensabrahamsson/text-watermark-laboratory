Six-forty in the morning and the library is dark except for the staff room, where someone's already running the coffee machine and complaining about the boiler. Before doors open at nine or ten, there's a chunk of invisible work: book returns from the overnight chute get sorted, the self-checkout machines get restarted because they crashed again overnight, and someone walks the floor checking for anything left behind — a laptop charger, a kid's shoe, once a prosthetic leg, apparently, according to a librarian who's worked there twenty years.

The public thinks a library is books and quiet. The staff experience is closer to running a small, underfunded community center that happens to have books in it. On a typical day you've got: an elderly man who comes in every morning to read the newspaper because he can't afford his own subscription and treats it as a social visit; a home-schooling parent who needs printer help; someone using the bathroom to wash up because the library is one of the few free indoor public spaces in town; a school class arriving twenty minutes early for a booked tour, throwing off the whole morning schedule; and at least one person asking where the bathroom is within the first ten minutes.

Behind the desk, staff are doing triage constantly — reference questions mixed with de facto social work. Librarians end up being informal experts on housing forms, unemployment portals, and how to use a smartphone, because nobody else in town will sit with someone for twenty minutes helping them set up an email account. This isn't in most job descriptions, but it eats a real chunk of the day.

Collections work happens in the gaps. New books get processed — barcoded, covered in that plastic film, entered into the catalog system, which is often some clunky vendor software from the 1990s in spirit even if it's technically "modern." Weeding — pulling out damaged, outdated, or never-borrowed books — is constant and a little brutal; someone has to decide a beloved but unread 2003 book on fax machines finally goes to the discard cart. Discarded books go to a book sale, a Little Free Library, or recycling, and there's always faint guilt about it.

Programming is its own machine: toddler storytime needs a room booked, a plan, and someone energetic enough to sing the same five songs without dying inside. Then there's the teen after-school crowd, who are loud, present, occasionally destructive with the furniture, and mostly just need somewhere to exist that isn't home or a shopping mall. Evening programs — a genealogy talk, a local author reading — draw six to fifteen people, and half the staff's afternoon is chairs, projector setup, and hoping the Wi-Fi holds.

Then there's the friction nobody puts in the newsletter: patrons who are intoxicated or in crisis, disputes over computer time limits, someone shouting because their hold didn't come in, theft of security tags off DVDs, a person sleeping who staff have to wake gently and repeatedly because the policy says you can't just kick someone out for sleeping. Many libraries have become de facto daytime shelters, and staff — who trained as librarians, not social workers or security guards — are doing all three jobs on a librarian's paycheck.

Closing is its own ritual: shelving the day's returns (there's always a cart nobody got to), locking study rooms, counting the till if there's a cafe or fine payments, and doing a final sweep so nobody's locked in overnight — which has happened, more than once, at more than one library.

It runs on a mix of real professional expertise, chronic understaffing, community goodwill, and improvisation. The brochure image — hush, shelves, orderly rows — is real for maybe forty minutes a day. The rest of it is closer to a nonprofit crisis center with a card catalog.
