The bell above the door had stopped ringing an hour ago, and now the shop held that particular quiet of late afternoon turning to evening — dust motes suspended in the last slant of light, the radiator ticking somewhere behind Poetry & Drama.

Mira came out from behind the till with the closing sign in her hand and found the old man still in the same aisle he'd claimed since three o'clock, kneeling now in front of the bottom shelf like a man at prayer.

"We're closing in ten minutes," she said, not unkindly.

"Yes," he said, not moving.

She waited. He turned a book over in his hands — a slim clothbound thing, the spine gone soft and pale, no jacket. He held it the way people hold things they're deciding whether to keep.

"Anything I can help you find?" she asked.

"I found it." He said it quietly, almost to himself. "I wasn't looking for it. That's the trouble with these places. You come in for one thing and the shop has other ideas."

Mira smiled and crouched a little, curious despite the clock. "What is it?"

He held it up. The gilt lettering had worn to almost nothing, but she could make out a name, a year — 1962.

"My wife had this," he said. "Not this copy. Her copy. She read it to me once, badly, on a train to Dover, because she said the man next to us was pretending not to listen and she wanted to give him something to pretend not to listen to." He laughed, short and dry. "She's been gone three years."

Mira didn't say anything. She'd learned, working the till in a shop like this, that silence was usually the correct answer.

"I gave her copy away," he continued. "After. To a niece who never read it, I'm quite sure, but who needed something of hers, and this was small enough to fit in a bag." He turned the book over again. "I didn't think I'd see it again. Not this one, obviously — this is somebody else's ghost, not mine. But close enough."

"Do you want it?"

"I don't know what I'd do with it. Put it on a shelf. Not read it — I know it too well to read it, if that makes sense. Some books you don't read anymore, you just keep as evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

He looked up at her properly for the first time, and there was something almost amused in his face, under the tiredness. "That it happened. That there was a train, and a man pretending not to listen, and a woman who thought that was funny." He set the book gently back on the shelf, spine out, exactly where he'd found it. "No. Someone else can have the evidence. I've got my own."

He stood, knees cracking audibly, and Mira pretended not to notice that either.

"Sorry to have kept you," he said.

"You didn't. Not really."

At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back once at the shelf, at the shop, at the dust turning gold in the last of the light. "Strange business, isn't it. All these books waiting for the one person who'll understand why they matter. And most of them just wait forever."

"Not forever," Mira said. "Just until closing."

He laughed properly this time, and the bell rang as he left, and she locked the door behind him and did not, in the end, move the book from where he'd left it.
