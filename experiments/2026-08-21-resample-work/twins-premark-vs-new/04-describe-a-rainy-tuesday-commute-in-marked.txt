The platform smells of wet wool before anything else. That, and diesel from the buses idling on the street above, seeping down through the stairwell in thin grey threads. It's 7:14, and the fluorescent light over track two flickers on a two-second delay, so that for a moment everyone standing there looks slightly stunned, caught between one version of the light and the next.

Rain doesn't fall in this city so much as hang. It's the kind that beads on wool coats without soaking through, that turns hair not wet but heavy, so people keep touching theirs, checking. A woman in a red anorak wrings out the hem of her scarf without seeming to notice she's doing it. Everyone's shoes make a different sound on the tile — the hard click of leather soles, the wet suck of rubber boots, the near-silence of trainers gone soft at the seams.

The train, when it comes, exhales before the doors open — that pneumatic sigh, then the doors sliding apart to release a warmth that smells faintly of other people's coffee and the ozone tang of wet electronics. Inside, the windows have already fogged from breath and body heat, and someone has drawn a small, embarrassed heart in the condensation near the door, which no one acknowledges.

Newspapers left on seats have gone soft and translucent at the corners. A man reads one anyway, carefully, the way you'd handle something already dying. The overhead announcement comes in two languages, the second version always a beat slower, as if translating itself reluctantly. Umbrellas drip in a loose, arrhythmic percussion against the floor — not quite a pattern, but not quite random either, the way rain sounds against a tin roof from a distance.

At the second stop, more people get on than can comfortably fit, and there's the particular negotiation of bodies in wet clothing trying not to touch — shoulders angled, bags held close, a general common effort at minimal contact that somehow makes everyone more aware of each other, not less. Someone's umbrella, still open, catches another passenger's sleeve, and there's a soft exchanged apology, mutual, neither party really at fault.

Outside, the city slides by grey-on-grey: apartment blocks with balconies stripped bare for autumn, a crane motionless against low cloud, cyclists leaning into the wind with that particular hunch that says they've done this every day for years and have made peace with it. A cyclist stopped at a crossing has water running off the brim of his helmet in a steady line, like a small eave.

The train slows for the tunnel and the windows go black, throwing back the carriage's own fluorescent interior — tired faces, damp shoulders, a child pressing a finger to the glass and drawing a line through her own reflected breath-fog. Then daylight again, flatter now, filtered through cloud that shows no seam, no gap, just an even white-grey ceiling stretching to wherever the buildings stop.

By the final stop the rain hasn't worsened or eased — it simply continues, patient, indifferent, the way it will likely continue past noon and into the evening commute too. People gather their bags, their damp newspapers, their umbrellas, and start moving toward the doors before the train has fully stopped, that small anticipatory lurch forward that happens every morning regardless of weather. The doors open onto the smell of wet pavement and someone's cigarette, and the crowd disperses into the rain without hesitation, umbrellas blooming open in near unison, black and grey and one incongruous yellow, already merging into the grey of the street beyond.
