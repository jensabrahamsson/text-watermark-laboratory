Dear Anna,

Your letter arrived on a grey Tuesday, and I want you to know it did exactly what good letters do — it stopped my whole day for a moment and made me smile at the kitchen table like a fool. I had to read the part about your first year of teaching twice, partly because I was moved and partly because I couldn't quite believe that the girl who used to hide novels inside her geography textbook is now the one standing at the front of the room, trying to get thirty restless children to care about rivers and rainfall.

You asked whether I remember you from those days, and of course I do — though perhaps not in the way you'd expect. I don't remember you as the top of the class, though you often were. I remember you as the one who asked why. Not to be difficult, though I'm sure it looked that way to me some mornings, but because you genuinely wanted the seams of things to show. You wanted to know why a sentence was built the way it was, why a war started when it did, why the answer in the back of the book was right and not some other, equally reasonable-sounding thing. That kind of hunger is rare, and it doesn't always announce itself as talent. Sometimes it just looks like a child who won't let a lesson end.

I'm glad to hear the job is hard. I'd be more worried if you told me it was easy. The hard parts — the child who won't speak, the parent who won't listen, the curriculum that asks you to cover in a week what deserves a month — those are the parts that will actually teach you how to teach, if you let them. Don't rush to smooth them over. Sit with the difficulty a little. You'll find, a few years in, that the moments you learned the most from are rarely the days that went well.

You wrote that you sometimes think of my classroom when you're planning your own lessons, and I'll admit that undid me a little. Teaching is a strange profession in that you rarely get to see what becomes of the seeds you plant. Most days you simply have to trust that something is taking root somewhere, in someone, and get on with the next lesson. To hear, all these years later, that something did take root — that it grew into you standing at your own blackboard, asking your own restless children to wonder — well. That is as close to a report card as this old teacher is ever likely to get, and I'll take it gladly.

Keep asking why, Anna. Teach your pupils to ask it too, even when it makes your lessons run long and your lesson plans go sideways. It served you well once. I have every confidence it will serve you — and them — well again.

With great fondness and no small amount of pride,

Margaret
