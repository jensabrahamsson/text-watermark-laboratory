Claude responded: He didn't call it teaching.

He didn't call it teaching. He called it "watching me do this once."

That's how I learned to true a wheel — not from a stand, not from spoke tension meters, but from Bengt, three houses down, who waved me over one Saturday because he needed someone to hold the bike upside down while he cursed at it. "Hold the fork. Not there. There." That was the whole introduction.

Bicycle repair, according to Bengt, is mostly about not overthinking things you can feel with your hands. A wheel is true when it stops wobbling — you don't need to know why a spoke is loose, you need to spin the wheel, watch where it kisses the brake pad, and give that spoke a quarter turn. "Righty tightens, obviously," he said, in a tone that suggested I'd insulted him by not already knowing this. He never explained the physics of opposing spoke tension pulling the rim straight. He just showed me the wobble, handed me the spoke key, and walked off to answer his phone, leaving me to figure out that turning the wrong spoke makes it worse before it makes it better.

Brakes were the same. He yanked the pads out, held them an inch from my face and said "see how it's worn at an angle, toe it in a bit," then put them back before I'd fully registered what "toe it in" meant. I learned later, on my own, with my own bike screeching down a hill, that it means angling the front of the pad to touch the rim first, so it doesn't judder. He never said "so it doesn't judder." He assumed I'd find that out the hard way, and I did.

Chains, he treated with something close to contempt. "It's not delicate. Stop being delicate with it." Degreaser, an old toothbrush, don't baby it, oil it after, wipe off the excess or it'll just collect grit and grind your drivetrain into dust. That last part he only mentioned after I'd ruined a cassette by not wiping it off the first time. Impatience, it turns out, teaches you to ask fewer questions and watch more carefully, because you only get the demonstration once.

Punctures were where his impatience actually made me faster than any manual would have. No careful diagrams of tire levers, no step-by-step. Just: lever under, lever under, pop, feel around inside for the thing that did it, new tube, pump, done — thirty seconds of talking for what other people stretch into a paragraph with pictures. I still hear his voice when I do it. "You're overthinking the last bit, it just clips on."

What I actually learned wasn't bicycle repair. It was that most mechanical problems have a small, stupid, physical cause, and if you slow down and actually look — really look, the way he never let me look for more than four seconds before moving my hand himself — you'll usually find it. He gave me no theory. He gave me a bike, upside down, and just enough irritation to make sure I paid attention the first time, because there wasn't going to be a second one.
