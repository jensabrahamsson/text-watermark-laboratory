The basement of Härlanda Church smelled the same every Thursday: dust, coffee gone bitter in the urn, and the faint mildew of hymnals stored too long in cardboard boxes. Eleven chessboards were arranged on folding tables, their surfaces scarred with decades of coffee rings, and above them a single fluorescent tube buzzed with a frequency only Rutger seemed to notice.

"It's flickering again," he said, not looking up from his position. He was playing white against Ingemar, a retired ferry mechanic who moved his pieces with the unhurried confidence of a man who had once operated much larger and more consequential machines.

"It's been flickering since Erikson was chairman," Ingemar said. "You get used to it."

Erikson had been dead eleven years. The club had not replaced the tube, out of what Marta called respect and what her husband called laziness, though he never said this aloud, because Marta kept the minutes and controlled the coffee rota.

Seven members tonight. Low for August, when half the town was still at their summer cottages, pretending the archipelago would keep them young. Astrid sat alone at board four, waiting for an opponent, arranging and rearranging her pawns into a shape that meant nothing, a habit she'd had since her husband stopped coming. He had not died. He had simply found Thursdays inconvenient after the divorce, and the club, with the diplomatic cowardice of small institutions, had never asked either of them to leave, so they had taken turns instead, alternating weeks like custody.

Pastor Lindqvist came down at eight, as he always did, not to play but to make sure the outer door was locked and to accept, without comment, the fifty kronor the club left in the collection box upstairs for use of the space. He nodded at Astrid. He had married her, seventeen years ago, in the sanctuary directly above where she now sat.

"Anyone winning?" he asked, the same question every week, and every week nobody answered him directly, because winning was not really the point, though nobody could have said what the point was instead. Rutger might have said it was the buzzing tube, the coffee, the particular silence of eleven people concentrating in a room that had been built for something else entirely — confession, maybe, or the kind of communal thinking that happens in pews. Ingemar might have said it was Thursday itself, a shape in the week he could hold onto.

At board four, a young man named Petter finally sat down across from Astrid. He was new, had wandered in three weeks ago after seeing a notice on the community board upstairs, between a lost-cat flyer and an advertisement for a men's grief group that met on Tuesdays. He did not yet know about the divorce, or the ferry, or Erikson, or why the tube still flickered. He only knew that Astrid played the Caro-Kann and drank her coffee black.

"White or black?" he asked.

She turned a pawn between two fingers, one white, one black, and held out both closed fists.

Outside, above them, the church bell did not ring, because it was not yet nine, and would not ring again until Sunday, when a different set of people would fill the room upstairs, unaware of what had been decided, board by board, in the basement below.
