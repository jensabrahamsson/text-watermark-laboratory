The fluorescent light above the vending machine flickered on a two-second delay, so the room seemed to breathe. Ingrid counted the flickers instead of the minutes, which was the same thing, more or less, except it gave her hands something to do that wasn't picking at the paper cup.

Across from her, a man in a reflective vest sat with his elbows on his knees, staring at a spot on the linoleum that no one else could see. He had introduced himself an hour ago — Pelle, from Kungälv, his father, chest pains, nothing dramatic, just an ambulance and a night that wouldn't end — and since then they had settled into the specific intimacy of strangers who share a bad hour.

"You'd think they'd fix that light," Pelle said.

"You'd think a lot of things."

He almost smiled. Behind the desk, a nurse named Åsa moved through her rounds with the unhurried economy of someone who had worked enough 2 a.m.s to know that speed helped nobody. She checked a monitor, adjusted a blanket on a sleeping boy two chairs down, said something low to his mother that made the mother's shoulders drop half an inch.

"Is it always like this?" Ingrid asked when Åsa passed near enough.

"Like what?"

"Quiet. Slow."

"It comes in waves. Right now we're between waves." Åsa said it the way you'd describe weather, not because she didn't care but because caring, at 2 a.m., had to be rationed like everything else.

The doors hissed open and a paramedic wheeled someone through, fast, the kind of fast that made everyone look up and then look away, ashamed of their own curiosity. Pelle's eyes followed the gurney until it disappeared down the corridor.

"That's not my dad," he said, mostly to himself.

"No."

"I keep thinking it will be."

Ingrid didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer for it, not one that fit in a waiting room chair at two in the morning. Instead she offered him half of a chocolate bar she'd bought from the flickering machine, and he took it without ceremony, the way you accept small kindnesses when you're too tired to be polite about refusing them.

"My mother used to say hospitals are where time forgets to move," Pelle said.

"Mine used to say nothing. She wasn't the saying type."

"Is she—"

"Ten years now."

He nodded, not with pity, just acknowledgment, and they sat with that for a while, two people who had run out of small talk and found something better in its place: a kind of companionable silence, the sort that didn't need filling.

A door opened somewhere down the hall. Åsa's head turned toward the sound before either of them registered it. She was already walking before the doctor called the name — Andersson? — and Pelle was on his feet, chocolate wrapper crushed in his fist, the fluorescent light flickering once more above the machine as if to mark the moment.
