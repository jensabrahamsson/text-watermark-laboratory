The lights in Corridor B go off in sections, and Assar works from the far end toward the exit, so the last thing he sees each night is the whole length of it dark except for the strip under the office door. He starts with the gym floor because the marks come up easier before they've set — heel scuffs, an arc of something that could be juice, a streak from a chair leg dragged instead of lifted. He runs the machine in long parallel passes, the same rhythm every time, and by the third pass he's stopped thinking about it.

Room 4A smells like clementine peel and pencil shavings. Someone has left a drawing taped half off a desk — a horse, mostly, with too many legs corrected and left visible, not erased. He doesn't touch it. He empties the bin, which today holds an apple core, a broken ruler, three balled-up worksheets, and a single glove, navy blue, child-sized, no pair. He puts the glove on the shelf by the door where lost things go, on top of two others already waiting there since Tuesday.

The toilets take the longest. Paper towels missed the bin and dried stiff on the floor near the sinks; he has to scrape at one with the edge of his shoe before the mop will lift it. Someone has written PELLE ÄR DUM in pencil on the stall door, the letters uneven, pressed hard enough to be visible from outside too. He doesn't sand it out tonight — that's a weekend job, needs the fine paper — he just notes it on the pad he keeps in his breast pocket, third item down.

In the staff room the coffee machine is still on, the hot plate empty but glowing faintly orange, and he switches it off at the wall the way he's told the teachers a dozen times not to need him to. A stack of ungraded papers sits by the sink with a mug on top of them, ring already dried into the top sheet. He moves the mug, doesn't move the papers.

At 6:40 the caretaker's radio, low volume, gives the regional traffic report for roads he doesn't drive. He listens to it out of habit while he checks the fire doors, one by one, the push bars stiff on the two at the east end that need oil, also on the pad. The gym floor has dried by now, and in the overhead lights it looks wet anyway, a trick of the wax.

Outside, the bike racks are empty except for one bike with a flat back tire, chained, left probably on purpose, someone's parent coming for it tomorrow or not. He checks the gate, checks that the gate checks, and locks it. Behind him the building holds four hundred desks, two hundred coat hooks, one glove on a shelf, and no children, and it is exactly this — the specific, itemized absence of them — that he clocks out against, not any feeling about it, just the number on the sheet: 18:52.
