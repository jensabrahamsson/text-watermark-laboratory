The campsite shop sold firelighters, postcards of a lake that was a twenty-minute walk, and bread that was always yesterday's unless you arrived at eight. I arrived at eight and still got yesterday's, which the woman behind the counter announced as a fact, not an apology. The path to the pitches was gravel that announced every visitor. My tent had taken on the smell of the last person's wet socks. I unzipped everything and let the morning do what it could.

A man two pitches over was arguing quietly with a stove. His children had already gone to the shower block with towels over their shoulders like capes. I walked to the lake because the postcard had made it look nearer. It was a proper lake, dark and uninterested, with a sign about not swimming that everyone ignored in summer and obeyed today. A dog entered the water and came out believing it had won. I sat on a bench dedicated to someone who had loved this view, which is a sentence I have never known how to test.

Back at the tent the stove man had succeeded and was boiling something in a pan too small. He nodded as if we had a shift together. I made tea on a kettle that whistled in a key I disliked and drank it from a mug that said a seaside town I had not visited. In the afternoon it rained in a straight line. I lay in the tent and read the same page twice. When the rain stopped the gravel started again: people returning from the shop with bread that would be tomorrow's yesterday. I thought about leaving a day early and then put the thought in the porch with the shoes, where damp decisions usually go.
