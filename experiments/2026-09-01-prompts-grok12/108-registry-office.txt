The registry office had a carpet that muffled important shoes and a noticeboard of ceremonies with times written in a hand that did not hurry. I was not getting married. I was a witness for a colleague who had asked in the kitchen as if it were a lift home. The waiting room held a low table of flowers that were real and a stack of leaflets about changing a name. A child in a waistcoat kicked the table leg in a rhythm I started to count and then stopped, because counting felt like taking a job.

We were called by a number, not a name, which made the whole thing briefly like a deli. Inside, the room was smaller than the photographs on the website. The registrar had a voice trained to carry without echoing. I signed where the little sticky arrow said sign and used a pen that was heavier than any pen I own. The colleague cried in a tidy way. The other witness had a cough he swallowed for the length of the vows. Someone in the back row photographed the moment with a phone held too high, which is how you know it happened.

Afterwards we stood on the steps and rice was not thrown, only confetti that the wind took sideways into a hedge. I had a slice of cake from a tin in the car park. The colleague said thank you as if I had lifted a sofa. I said it had been nothing, which was a lie in the useful direction. Walking back to the office I still had the heavy pen's balance in my fingers. The afternoon at my desk was ordinary and therefore strange. I filled the kettle and did not mention the number, the arrow, or the hedge. Some days you hold a pen that is not yours and then you put it down, and the rest of the building continues as if ink were not a kind of promise.
