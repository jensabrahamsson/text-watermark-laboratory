The charity shop smelled of ironed cotton and the inside of old novels. A rail of coats had been sorted by colour so thoroughly that walking past them felt like weather. I had come for a picture frame and stayed because the volunteer at the till was telling another volunteer about a kettle that had sold in ten minutes, as if speed were a kind of blessing. The books were in a room at the back with a step down. Someone had written genres on cards in a hand that believed in order.

I found a travel guide to a city I have never visited and whose currency no longer exists in that form. The maps were still confident. I sat on the step and read about a tram that probably does not run. A child in the main room asked whether the toys had names and was told they could have new ones. I put the guide back because I did not need a tram, then took it again because the maps had been so sure of themselves.

At the till the volunteer wrapped the book in a paper bag from a grocer that had closed. She asked if I wanted the stamp on the loyalty card and I said yes, though I had no card. She found a blank one in a drawer of elastic bands. I paid with coins that made a sound like rain on the glass dish. Outside a bus sprayed the kerb and the coats in the window did not move. I walked home with a city in a bag and a card with one stamp, which is a ridiculous inventory and also the whole point of that kind of shop: you leave carrying a life that was briefly someone else's, and the kettle that sold in ten minutes remains a story you were only standing next to.
