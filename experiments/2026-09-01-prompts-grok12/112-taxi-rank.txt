The taxi rank outside the football ground was a line of engines that idled as if they were thinking. Drivers read phones. One man stood beside his door and smoked with the concentration of a break that had been negotiated. I had left before the whistle because the crowd in the stand had started to feel like weather. The rank had a sign about match-day fares and nothing about fares to my street. I waited until a driver looked at me instead of the phone, which took the length of a cigarette I was not smoking.

Inside, the car smelled of mint and upholstery cleaner. The meter jumped as if surprised to be employed. I gave an address and then a landmark because the address had never helped anyone. We talked about the score as if it were weather. He knew the landmark. The streets at that hour are mostly shutters and the backs of signs. A group in scarves argued about whose idea the season had been. We passed them without comment, which felt like professionalism.

At my street he could not turn around, so I got out on the wrong side and paid through the window. He printed a receipt I did not need and I took it anyway, because refusing a receipt after a match is a kind of argument. The mint smell stayed in my coat. I climbed the stairs and put the receipt under a magnet, a proof of a journey that had been only a failure of staying. In the kitchen I ran the tap until the water was cold. The rank would still be thinking. The whistle would already have gone. That is a closed loop, and also how you get home when the stand has become weather.
