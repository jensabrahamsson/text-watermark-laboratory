The bowling alley still used paper score sheets if you asked, though the screens above the lanes did the arithmetic whether you wanted it or not. I asked. The man at the counter had a name badge from a different chain and did not comment. He handed me shoes that had been many people's shoes and a short pencil. The carpet in the approach was a pattern designed so spilled cola would not show, which meant it showed everything else: dust, glitter from a birthday, a single sequin that had given up.

I bowled badly in a way that felt practiced. The ball returned with a thud I had forgotten. Two lanes over a league team in matching shirts reset the pins with professional boredom. A speaker played a song from a year when I thought I would come here more often. I wrote numbers that did not add up and then let the screen win. The pencil rolled off the table twice. I put it in my pocket so it would stop making a decision.

At the cafe hatch the tea came in a styrofoam cup with a lid that did not fit. I stood and watched a child celebrate a spare as if it were a public holiday. The air smelled of wax and fryer oil. When I returned the shoes the man stamped a card I had not asked for and said the next game was cheaper before six. I thanked him and left with the pencil still in my pocket. Outside it was still afternoon. The alley had been darker than the street by a whole season. I sat in the car and added my own score, which was worse, and felt oddly finished, as if the pins had been the only thing on the list.
