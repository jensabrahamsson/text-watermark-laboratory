The fire station kitchen was ordinary in a way that felt like the point. A long table, a notice about milk, a toaster that launched bread with too much enthusiasm. I was there on an open evening that had been advertised on a lamppost with a torn corner. Children tried on helmets that made them into different animals. I drank tea from a mug that named a charity run and listened to a firefighter explain the difference between two kinds of alarm with the patience of someone who has explained it at every open evening since the toaster was new.

We were shown the engine. The cab smelled of rubber and something clean. I did not climb in; a boy ahead of me was already giving a speech to nobody. In the yard a hose had been coiled with a neatness I associated with ships. Someone asked a practical question about chimney fires and received a practical answer. I asked nothing and felt that this, too, was allowed. The night air made the kitchen window yellow.

At the end they gave out stickers and a leaflet I folded into a square. I walked home past the lamppost with the torn corner, which still claimed the evening was happening. The mug's charity run would be in a month I would not enter. I put the sticker on the inside of a kitchen cupboard, where it would surprise me later and mean almost nothing, which is the correct scale for a Tuesday that included an engine and a toaster. Some public rooms exist so that the rest of us can see that the alarms have a difference, and that milk has a notice, and that patience can be a job.
