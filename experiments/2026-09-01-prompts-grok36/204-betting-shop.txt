The betting shop kept its light at a constant afternoon even when the street was evening. I went in because the rain had become personal and because I have always been able to stand near other people's hope without catching it. Screens showed horses I could not tell apart and numbers that changed faster than anyone could be said to understand. A man at the high shelf wrote on a slip with the care of a will. The carpet was patterned to hide everything.

I did not bet. I stood near the door and read a poster about responsible gambling written by someone who had never stood near this door. The cashier knew three customers by a version of their names. When a race ended there was a sound that was not quite a groan and not quite nothing. Someone crumpled a slip and then smoothed it again, as if paper had feelings. I watched a woman check her phone and the screen and the phone, a triangle of attention that left no room for the street.

The rain had not stopped. I bought a coffee from the machine because leaving empty-handed felt like walking out of a church during the hymn. It tasted of the machine's previous life. I drank it under the awning beside a man who was having a cigarette as a form of accounting. He nodded at me as if we had both lost. I had lost nothing except twenty minutes and a dry coat. Walking away I passed the window and saw myself among the screens, a person who had gone in for weather and come out with a story he would not tell, because there is nothing to tell except that the light in there never becomes evening, which is a fact and not a moral.
