The warehouse on the ring road ran a night shift I had agreed to cover for a friend who had a better reason to be elsewhere. I was given a hi-vis and a scanner that beeped in a language of locations. Aisles went on longer than the building seemed to allow. The heating was a rumour. I picked items into a crate: a kettle, three identical boxes of something labelled only with a code, a child's tent in a bag. The tent felt like a message from another life.

Break was a room of vending machines and a table with a newspaper from yesterday. I sat with people who knew each other's business and did not ask mine, which I appreciated. Someone microwaved fish and the room became a negotiation. I ate a sandwich I had made at five in the afternoon, which is a kind of time travel. Back on the floor the scanner knew me now, or I knew it. Beep, walk, beep. There is a hypnosis to it that I did not dislike.

At the end of the shift the sky was considering morning. I signed out and returned the hi-vis, which had never become mine. My friend texted thanks. I texted a thumb, which is a poor sentence. The bus into town had two other night people and a driver who did not speak. I watched the warehouse shrink into a box among boxes. At the flat I slept in my clothes for an hour and woke smelling of cardboard. I will not cover again unless asked, and I will be asked, and I will say yes, because the scanner was simple and the tent in the bag is still, somewhere, a crate heading towards a child I will not meet.
