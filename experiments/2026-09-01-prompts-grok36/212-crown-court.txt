The crown court steps were wider than they needed to be, as if the building wished to slow you down before the metal and the lists. I was not on trial. I had been asked to attend as a character witness for a man I had worked with years ago, a sentence that still sits in my mouth like a stone. Security took my belt and my coins and gave them back in a tray. The corridor had benches polished by other waiting.

I sat opposite a vending machine that sold nothing I wanted. Lawyers moved past with folders that looked heavier than paper. My name was not on the screen. I read it anyway. When I was called it was into a room that was smaller than television and more ordinary, which I found both a relief and a disappointment. I swore on a book I do not live by. The questions were simpler than my fear of them. I told the truth as I remembered it, which is not the same as the truth.

Afterwards I stood on the steps again and put my belt back on in public, which felt like the wrong kind of ceremony. The man I had spoken for nodded from a doorway and did not come over. I understood. I walked to the station the long way, past a park where office workers were eating at benches as if nothing in the stone building applied to sandwiches. My coins were still in a small plastic tray in my pocket; I had forgotten to empty it. I sat on a low wall and sorted them, a private accounting after a public one. The train home was delayed. I did not mind. Delay seemed, for once, correctly sized.
