The night shelter took names at a table under a strip light that made everyone look tired in the same way. I was there as a volunteer for the soup, not because I am good, but because a friend had asked and I am worse at refusing friends than at getting up at five. The hallway smelled of wet coats and disinfectant. A man signed the book with a signature that was a long line. Someone behind him joked with the precision of a person who has used humour as a coat for a long time.

I stood at the urn and poured. Bread came in a bag from a supermarket that was closing its day. I buttered it badly. A woman asked for tea with two sugars and then changed her mind and then changed it back, and I did all three versions in order without comment, which seemed to be the skill. In the dining room the television was on a channel nobody had chosen. Volunteers spoke to each other in the kitchen in the low voices of people who do not want to be overheard being useful.

At half past I washed cups. The water was too hot and then too cold. A resident came in for a glass and we stood aside for each other in a doorway too narrow for dignity. When the shift ended the street was that particular dark which is not night yet and not morning. My friend locked the door with a key on a lanyard. We did not recap the evening; we recapped the urn. Walking home I passed people who would not be sleeping in that hallway, including myself. The smell of the bread stayed on my sleeve until I washed it, which I did as soon as I got in, and then felt obscurely disloyal.
