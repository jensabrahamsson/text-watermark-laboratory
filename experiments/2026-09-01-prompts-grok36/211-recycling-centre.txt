The recycling centre was a Saturday of engines and good intentions. I queued on a ramp behind a car whose boot was a Tetris of cardboard. A man in a high-vis jacket pointed with the authority of a person who has seen every kind of waste. I had brought glass, a broken chair, and a bag of clothes I was pretending were still clothes. The glass went into a skip with a noise like a small accident. The chair was assessed and sent to wood.

The clothes were a problem. The textile bin was full. I stood holding the bag like a person who had not planned an ending. Another attendant said I could try the charity shop in town, which I already knew, which is why I had come here. I put the bag back in the car. A child in the next vehicle was eating crisps and watching the skips as if they were a fair. Wind moved a piece of plastic across the concrete in a way that felt pointed.

I drove home with the clothes still on the back seat, passing the charity shop I had not wanted to enter because entering it with a bag is a conversation. At the flat I left the bag in the hall. It will move again next Saturday, or the one after. The chair is gone. That is a kind of progress I can count. I washed my hands of dust that might have been anyone's attic. Through the window a neighbour was putting out a sofa as if the pavement were a centre of its own. I did not offer help. The bag in the hall waited with the patience of cotton.
