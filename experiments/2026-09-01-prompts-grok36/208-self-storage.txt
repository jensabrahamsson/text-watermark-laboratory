The self-storage site was a compound of metal doors in a field that had given up being a field. I had hired the smallest unit because the flat had run out of corners. The office sold padlocks, tape, and a kind of reassurance. A man with a clipboard walked me to unit 114 as if it were a hotel room. The corridor lights came on in sections and went off behind us, so we travelled in a moving present tense.

The unit was a box with a smell of other people's cardboard. I stacked what I had brought: a lamp that flickered, a suitcase of clothes I might fit again, papers I was not ready to throw away and not ready to read. The lamp looked foolish in that company. I stood in the doorway and felt the particular shame of paying rent for objects that had already lost an argument. The man with the clipboard waited at a polite distance, checking his phone.

I locked the door and immediately wanted to open it to check I had locked it, which I did, twice. Driving out I passed other units with their doors up: a motorbike, a crib, a stack of framed prints facing the wall. A woman was crying into the open boot of a car without performing it for anyone. I did not stop, which is the etiquette of such places. On the dual carriageway the sky was a low lid. At the flat the extra corner did not feel extra. I made a list of what I had stored and then lost the list, which completed the exercise.
