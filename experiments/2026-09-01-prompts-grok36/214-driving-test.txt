The driving test centre was a brick rectangle with a car park of other people's nerves. I had not come to take a test. I had come to sit in the passenger seat while my nephew took his, which the examiner allowed with a look that said this was irregular and also already happening. We waited in a room of plastic chairs and a video about roundabouts. A plant in the corner was doing its best in fluorescent light. My nephew tapped his keys in a rhythm I decided not to mention.

The examiner introduced himself by a first name that did not match his lanyard. I sat in the back and became furniture. We left the car park as if the painted arrows were law. I watched the town I knew become a set of manoeuvres. A van indicated late. A pedestrian stepped out as if the highway code were a rumour. My nephew did not swear, which I counted as a pass of a different kind. The examiner wrote without looking at the page, a skill.

When we returned nobody spoke until the handbrake. The result was a piece of paper and a sentence I will not paraphrase because it belonged to him. I waited outside by a fence while he rang his mother. A woman came out crying in the other direction; her instructor offered a mint. I offered nothing. On the drive home, which I did, my nephew was quiet in the way of people who have been measured. I kept to the speed limit with a new and useless piety. We stopped for petrol and he paid, which was not the test, and felt like one.
