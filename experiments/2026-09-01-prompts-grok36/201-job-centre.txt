The job centre opened its doors with a sound like a supermarket and none of the smell. I took a ticket from a machine that printed a number already behind the one on the wall. Plastic chairs faced a screen of surnames that arrived and vanished without explanation. A child dragged a toy car along the skirting board and nobody asked him to stop. The carpet had a path worn from the ticket machine to the water cooler, which was empty.

When my number appeared it was spoken as well as shown, as if the room could not be trusted with either channel alone. I sat opposite a woman who had a lanyard and a kindness that was on a timer. She asked what I had been doing and I told her the true version, which sounded smaller than it had in the kitchen. She typed with two fingers and the computer made a noise of disagreement. We waited. Behind her a poster promised a future in logistics. I nodded at it as if it had asked a question.

I left with a printout still warm and a next appointment I would keep because the alternative was another ticket. Outside a man was smoking under the no-smoking sign with the concentration of work. The bus shelter had a timetable from a previous operator. I folded the printout so the logo would not show through my coat. Rain started as a fine argument. I walked to the stop that actually existed and stood with people who were also carrying paper they did not want to crease. The number on the wall would have moved on without me. That was the comfort and the insult.
