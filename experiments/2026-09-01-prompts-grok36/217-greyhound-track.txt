The greyhound track on a Thursday was a stadium with more seats than people and a wind that came in from the river as if it had a ticket. I went because a cousin had a dog running, or rather a share in a dog, which is a kind of ownership that requires explaining in pubs. The programme was a thin booklet of names that sounded like boasts. I stood by the rail. The track was a pale oval under lights that made the grass look like a different crop.

When the traps opened the sound was a collective intake. The dogs were a blur with a mechanical hare they would never catch, which seemed an honest metaphor and then a cruel one. My cousin's animal came fourth. He swore without heat and bought me a pie from a hatch that had been serving the same pie since a better era. We ate standing up. A man beside us tore his ticket with ceremony. The next race was already being called. I did not bet. I watched the hare and thought about work, which is a failure of attention I often commit at events.

The last race emptied the stands in a slow leak of coats. We waited by the kennels because my cousin wanted to say well done to a dog that did not need it. The animal was a long muscle being walked in a circle. I did not stroke it; I was not sure of the etiquette. Driving home the stadium receded into a glow and then into nothing. My cousin talked about next week. I listened as if I would come. I might. The pie sat in me like a decision. At the flat I put the programme in the bin and then took it out again, which is how souvenirs begin.
