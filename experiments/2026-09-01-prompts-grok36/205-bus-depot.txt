The bus depot at the edge of town was a shed of a building that had outgrown itself with parked vehicles. I had come to collect a wallet a driver had found and phoned about, which is a sentence that still surprises me. The office window was the height of a standing adult's chest. I spoke to a woman through a hole in the glass and she spoke to a radio and the radio spoke to someone who was eating. Diesel hung in the air like a weather.

While I waited I watched buses reverse with the slow confidence of animals that know the yard. A cleaner pushed a bin on wheels between them as if threading a needle. The woman at the window offered me a seat that was a plastic chair with a rip repaired by tape. I sat. A timetable on the wall was for a service that had been withdrawn; nobody had taken it down, which I respected. The radio crackled a joke I did not catch and several people laughed in different rooms.

The wallet arrived in a clear bag as if it were evidence. It was mine. The cash was still in it, which made me feel briefly famous. I signed a book whose previous page was a pair of gloves. Thanking them took more words than the recovery deserved. Outside a bus pulled out for a route I sometimes take and I almost boarded it out of gratitude. I walked instead, holding the wallet in my hand like a prize until that felt foolish, then putting it away. The depot receded into a smell and a roof. I have been more careful with pockets since, which is not the same as being a careful person.
