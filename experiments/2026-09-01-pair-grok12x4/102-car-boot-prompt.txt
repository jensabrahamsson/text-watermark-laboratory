Sunday at the car boot is a field that has agreed, for a few hours, to be a market. Grass flattened into aisles. A woman sold crockery from a blanket and wrapped each plate in last week's free paper. A man asked whether the toaster still worked and was told, honestly, that it had been in a cupboard since the wedding. I walked with a coin purse and a rule about not buying chairs. The rule failed at a pair of kitchen stools whose vinyl had cracked into a map of somewhere warmer.

The tea van had a queue that did not move like a queue. People stood and talked about the weather as if it were a committee item. A child ran between tables with a plastic sword and nobody's parent claimed the running, only the sword. I bought a paperback missing its first twelve pages and told myself the story would explain itself. The seller had a radio on low and a flask that steamed when the lid came off, which made the whole field smell briefly of instant coffee and wet wool.

By eleven the serious buyers had gone and the rest of us were rearranging other people's lives into our own boots. I paid for the stools with a note too large and waited while the woman found coins in a tobacco tin. She said they had been her neighbour's. I said I needed somewhere to put the kettle. Neither sentence required the other. Driving home the stools rattled and I kept one hand on them at the roundabouts, which is how you learn what you have actually purchased: not furniture, a noise, and a story about a cupboard since a wedding I will never attend.
