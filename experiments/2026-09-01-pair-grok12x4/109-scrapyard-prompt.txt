The scrapyard office was a Portakabin with a kettle and a window that looked onto stacked cars like a cliff. I had come for a wing mirror for a car I should have stopped repairing. The man inside had oil in the creases of his hands and a computer from a year when screens were deeper. He asked the make without looking up, then looked up anyway, as if the second look were courtesy. A dog slept under the desk on a blanket that had once been a seat.

We walked the aisle between the stacks. The ground was a paste of rust and rain. He found three mirrors that were almost right and one that was right if I did not mind a scratch that looked like a river. I did not mind. He named a price that included the walk. I paid in notes because the card machine in the cabin had a handwritten sign about Tuesdays. It was Thursday. He laughed at that, once, and the dog did not wake.

Back in the cabin he wrapped the mirror in an old free newspaper whose headline I had already forgotten by the car. The kettle clicked off and he did not make tea; the click was just the room's clock. I drove out past a crane that was not moving and a pile of exhausts that looked like a nest. At the first lights I checked the mirror against the one I still had, which was worse. Fitting it would be a weekend job I would postpone. For now it sat on the passenger seat in newsprint, a river-scratch of glass, evidence that the cliff of cars would still be there when I finally admitted the rest of the vehicle was a hobby with an engine.
