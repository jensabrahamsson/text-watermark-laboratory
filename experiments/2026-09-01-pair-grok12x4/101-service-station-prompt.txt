The motorway services at two in the morning are a different building from the one you pass in daylight with a full tank and a plan. The fluorescent strips over the food court have a green edge. A floor buffer drones in the far aisle and nobody looks at the person driving it. The coffee machine offers eight buttons and produces the same drink from three of them. I sat at a bolted table with a plastic stirrer and watched a man in a hi-vis jacket eat chips with the careful manners of someone who has been told off for eating in a nicer place.

Outside the pumps hissed and a lorry radio talked about fog in a county I would not reach tonight. The shop sold phone chargers, boiled sweets, and a magazine whose cover celebrity I did not recognise. The cashier had a plaster on one finger and counted change onto the counter instead of into my hand. I thanked her anyway. The toilets had a handwritten note about the dryer, which was true: it roared and did nothing.

I went back to the car because the alternative was to keep sitting under the green light until the sky remembered itself. The car park was almost empty. A fox crossed between the disabled bays without hurry. I started the engine and did not pull out for a full minute, which is a habit I cannot explain except that leaving a place at that hour feels like interrupting someone. Then I joined the slip road and the services folded back into a logo on a sign, as if the chips and the plaster and the fox had been a station I imagined between junctions.
