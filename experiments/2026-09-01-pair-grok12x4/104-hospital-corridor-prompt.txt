The hospital corridor did not echo in the way television had promised. It absorbed sound: trolley wheels, a printer, someone laughing once behind a curtain and then not. I sat in a plastic chair whose arms were too close together and read the same poster about hand gel three times. The clock jumped a minute as if it had been thinking. A volunteer with a trolley of biscuits asked if I wanted tea and I said not yet, which meant I wanted it very much and did not know how long I would be allowed to stay.

When my name was called it was the wrong surname first, then mine, as a correction. I followed a nurse whose shoes were silent. The bay had a window onto a brick wall and a tree that had grown in the gap anyway. Someone in the next bed was watching a cookery programme without sound. I answered questions I had answered at the desk. The blood pressure cuff complained. A student stood at the foot of the bed and was introduced by a title that made us both nod too hard.

I was sent back to the chairs with a sticker on my wrist and no new information. The volunteer had moved on. I bought tea from a machine that needed coins I had to borrow from the woman beside me, who was waiting for a different name and did not ask mine. We watched the corridor as if it were a platform. When I was finally told I could go, the tree in the gap was still there and the clock had jumped again. Outside the air was ordinary and therefore enormous. I sat on the low wall by the bus stop and finished the tea, which had never been hot, and waited for a bus whose number I knew, which felt like the first true fact of the afternoon.
