The chip shop after nine had the lights on in the back and the front window steamed into a map of nowhere. I joined a queue of people who had already decided what they wanted and were only waiting for the oil to agree. The radio was local and too loud, then not loud enough when a song nobody liked came on. A woman in a coat too warm for the room ordered for four people who were not present. The man at the fryer repeated the order without writing it down, which I took as a kind of faith.

When it was my turn I asked for the smaller portion and received the larger one, because the scoop had a single mood. Salt and vinegar were my own responsibility at a metal counter that had been wiped in a circle, not a line. I ate the first chips standing in the doorway so they would still be too hot. A bus passed and its lights ran across the steam. Someone behind me laughed at a joke I did not hear and then apologised to the room in general.

I walked home along the road that pretends to be a high street after dark. The parcel of paper cooled in a useful way. By the time I reached the flat the chips were ordinary, which is their second life. I put them on a plate to pretend I had cooked. The kitchen light made them look paler. I ate them with a fork because it was late enough to bother. Tomorrow the shop would be a shut shutter and a smell on the pavement. Tonight it had been a queue, a scoop, and a faith in an unwritten order, which is as close as I come to a plan.
