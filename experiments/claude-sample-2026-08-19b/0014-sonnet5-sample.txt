The flour dust has settled by now, a fine gray film on the black mats behind the counter. Marta wipes down the glass case with a cloth that smells faintly of vinegar, working around the last four croissants, the heel of a rye loaf, two slices of almond cake going slightly waxy under the lights. The overhead fan ticks as it cools. Outside, the light has gone the color of weak tea, and the street lamps haven't caught yet.

The ovens are off but still warm enough that the back room holds a low, bready heat, the kind that clings to your collar on the walk home. A tray of egg wash sits forgotten on the steel table, its surface skinned over. Someone should have wrapped it an hour ago. The radio, tuned low to a station nobody remembers choosing, plays something with too many strings.

A man comes in at ten to six, shakes rain off his jacket, doesn't apologize for tracking water across the floor. He points at the rye without speaking much, pays with coins he counts out one at a time onto the little tray by the register. Marta counts them back into the drawer without comment. The bell above the door settles into stillness after he leaves.

In the back, Johan scrapes the last dough scraps from the bench into a bowl for tomorrow's starter feed, though half of it will go to the bin regardless. He rinses proofing baskets under water that never quite gets hot enough this late in the day, stacks them upside down to dry, their spiral ridges leaving faint lines in the residual flour. His forearms are dusted white to the elbow. He doesn't bother brushing it off.

The pastry case light hums, one bulb dimmer than the others, something to mention to the electrician who never comes. A fly investigates the crack under the case and is ignored. Marta writes the day's numbers on a scrap of receipt paper, adds wrong, erases with a licked thumb, adds again. Outside the window, a bus passes with three passengers, none of them looking in.

At 6:20 a woman in scrubs buys the last of the almond cake, eats a corner of it standing at the counter before she's even out the door, crumbs on her lower lip that she doesn't notice. She says the cake is good, not to anyone in particular. Marta nods, already reaching for the broom.

By 6:40 the chairs are up on two of the three tables, legs jutting toward the ceiling like something overturned in a hurry, though nothing here has hurried in an hour. The last croissant goes into a paper bag for Johan's dog, who waits every evening by the back gate regardless of whether there's anything for her.

The lock turns at 6:58, two minutes early, an allowance nobody questions. The key ring jingles once against the glass. Down the street, the tram brakes with a long metal sigh, and the smell of the bakery — yeast, browned butter, the ghost of cinnamon from the morning batch — stays behind the closed door, undiluted, belonging to no one until tomorrow.
