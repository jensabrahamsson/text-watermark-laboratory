The library opens at ten, but someone's been inside since eight-thirty. The returns bin outside the door fills up overnight — people drop books off on their way to work, on their way to nowhere, sometimes just a plastic bag of paperbacks left leaning against the wall because the slot was full. The first job is always the same: empty the bin, check for anything wet or moldy or chewed, sort into rough piles by section, and start the trolley run before the doors open and the day gets complicated.

Staffing is thin. A branch library serving thirty thousand people might have four or five actual employees on any given shift, plus a couple of volunteers who shelve or run the children's reading hour. One person is almost always on the desk, which means answering questions that range from "where's the bathroom" to "can you help me apply for unemployment benefits" to "my printer job didn't come out, can you fix it" to genuine reference questions nobody's asked in months. The desk person is also watching the room, because libraries are one of the few indoor public spaces that don't require you to buy anything or prove you belong there, so they get everyone — students doing homework, retirees reading newspapers, people sleeping because they have nowhere else to be, parents parking toddlers in the picture-book corner for forty minutes of quiet.

Behind the desk, cataloguing and processing happens in fits and starts. New books arrive in boxes, get stamped, covered in plastic film, tagged with RFID or barcode, entered into the system — a task that sounds administrative and is actually fairly meditative, done between interruptions. Weeding is constant and a little brutal: books that haven't circulated in three years get pulled, no matter how good they are, because shelf space is finite and the collection has to keep breathing. Nobody enjoys pulling a beautiful, unread poetry collection to make room for the fifteenth copy of whatever's trending. It happens anyway.

The technology never quite works the way it's supposed to. The self-checkout machine jams. The public computers need their sessions reset every twenty minutes. The printer eats paper on a schedule only it understands. IT support might be centralized at a different building entirely, so a broken scanner can mean days of workarounds — writing due dates on paper slips, doing checkouts by hand, apologizing to the same people twice.

Programming is where the actual mission shows up, unglamorously. Someone spends two hours prepping a craft activity for six children, only four show up, and that's fine, that's normal, nobody complains. A local historian gives a talk to an audience of nine people and it's still worth doing. There's a language café on Tuesdays that's become the real center of gravity for a cluster of new immigrants — more useful to them than half the formal integration services in town, and it exists because one librarian decided to start it and kept doing it.

Money is the undertone to everything. The acquisitions budget doesn't stretch as far as the list of requested titles. Opening hours get quietly trimmed during budget years. A part-time position goes unfilled after someone retires, and the remaining staff absorb the gap without much conversation about it, because the alternative is closing early, and nobody wants to be the one explaining that to the regulars.

By closing time the returns bin is already filling again. The last task is walking the floor — waking the sleeping man gently, telling the teenagers it's time, turning off the reading lamps one by one down the row. The building goes quiet in a specific way, still smelling faintly of dust and old paper and the coffee someone wasn't supposed to bring in. Tomorrow the bin outside will already have three bags in it before anyone unlocks the door.
