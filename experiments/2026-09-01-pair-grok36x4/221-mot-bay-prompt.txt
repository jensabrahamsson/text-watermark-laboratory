The MOT bay was a garage with a waiting room the size of a thought. I handed over the keys and a form I had filled in wrongly and then correctly. The mechanic looked at the car as if it had already confessed. I sat on a chair with a split vinyl seat and read a poster about tyres until I could have given a talk. Through the glass the car was up on a lift, exposed, a little embarrassing, like seeing a colleague in a hospital gown.

A radio played a station that specialised in songs I knew the choruses of. Another customer arrived with a van and a story about a knocking. I made space on the remaining chair. We did not become friends. We watched our vehicles being judged. When my name was called it was to hear a list of items that were fine and one that was not, a bulb, which felt both trivial and like a moral. I waited while they found a bulb. The other customer offered me a mint. I took it.

The car came down. I paid at a counter that also sold ice scrapers although it was not winter. The certificate was a piece of paper that made the car legal for another year, a kind of pardon. Driving out I was careful at the first junction in a way that would not last. The mint was still in my mouth. At home I put the certificate in the glovebox with the previous ones, a small archive of being allowed to continue. The bulb was someone else's problem now, or rather it had been made not to be mine. I sat in the parked car for a minute listening to the tick of the engine, a sound I have decided means nothing, and then went inside.
