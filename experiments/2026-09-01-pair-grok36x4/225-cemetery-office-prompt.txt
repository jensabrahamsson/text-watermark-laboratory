The cemetery office was a small brick building with a map of plots on the wall like a polite city. I had come to ask about a stone that needed resetting after the winter. A woman behind the desk had a book of names and a kettle. She found my family in a handwriting I did not recognise as official. We looked at the map together. The plot was a square among squares. She offered to show me rather than describe the path, which I accepted because descriptions of cemeteries always sound like other cemeteries.

We walked between rows that were more organised than grief. A robin used a cross as a perch without commentary. The stone was leaning in the way she had said, a few degrees towards a neighbour. She talked about ground and frost and a waiting list. I nodded. I did not feel what I had expected to feel, which was a relief and a small shame. A man in the distance was trimming something with a machine that made the place briefly ordinary. We waited for it to pass as if it were a funeral.

Back in the office I paid a deposit on a form that had a carbon copy. She gave me the copy as if it were a receipt for a different kind of shopping. I folded it into my coat. Outside the gates a bus went by with advertisements for a sofa sale. I walked to the stop and did not look back, not out of hardness but because looking back at a cemetery is a performance I do not always want to give the street. At home I put the form in a drawer with other papers that are not bills and not letters. The stone will be reset in a season I was given as a range. I can live with a range.
