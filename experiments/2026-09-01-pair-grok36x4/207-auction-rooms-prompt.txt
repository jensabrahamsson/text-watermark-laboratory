The auction rooms were a former chapel with the pews gone and the habit of quiet remaining. Lots were arranged as if a house had been emptied in a hurry and then labelled. I had come to look, which is what people say when they are afraid of raising a hand by accident. A teapot with a cracked lid had a number. A box of spoons had a number. A painting of a cow had a number and an optimism I did not share.

The auctioneer spoke in a rhythm that made ordinary objects sound urgent. I stood at the back with my hands in my pockets as a policy. A couple bid on a wardrobe they had not measured. A dealer in a coat bought things without looking at them, which I found both impressive and sad. When the teapot came up nobody wanted it until the price became an insult, and then two people did. I did not move. The cow painting went to someone on the telephone, a ghost with money.

Afterwards they served tea in Styrofoam as if the chapel still owed hospitality. I walked among the unsold lots and felt the particular melancholy of furniture that had failed twice. The woman at the desk asked if I had a paddle and I said no, I had only come to look. She smiled the smile of someone who has heard that sentence become a purchase. I left empty-handed and was glad, then less glad on the bus when I thought of the teapot. At home I already have a teapot. That is not always the point. I made tea in it and sat with the feeling of having been in a room where other people's lives were being priced by the sentence.
