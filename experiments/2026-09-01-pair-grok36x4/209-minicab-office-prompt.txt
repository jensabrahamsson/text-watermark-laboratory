The minicab office was a room behind a shutter with a counter, a radio, and a kettle that had boiled too many times. I went in because the app had failed in a part of town the map still called a high street. A man behind the counter asked where I wanted to go and wrote it on a pad as if ink were more trustworthy than satellites. Drivers sat along the wall in a row of jackets. The television was horse racing with the sound down.

I waited on a chair that had been designed for a shorter person. The radio named streets I knew in a voice that made them sound like destinations in another country. A driver stood up, was given my piece of paper, and looked at me with the assessment of someone calculating rain and traffic together. We went out to a car that had a pine tree hanging from the mirror and a crack in the windscreen like a river. He did not talk at first, which I preferred.

When he did talk it was about a bypass that had been promised for years. I agreed, because agreeing is the fare you pay besides money. The meter was a number I watched too closely. We took a route that avoided something I could not see. At my door I paid in cash because the machine was a rumour. He wrote me a receipt that would not survive my pocket. The car pulled away before I had found my keys. Inside I put the receipt on the table and felt the particular relief of having been delivered by a person rather than a pin on a screen, which is an old-fashioned feeling and, tonight, accurate.
