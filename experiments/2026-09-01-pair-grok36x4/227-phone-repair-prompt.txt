The phone repair shop was a counter, a row of screens, and a man who could open a device without looking cruel. My screen had become a web of accidents. I described the drop as if it were unique. He had heard it. He named a price and a time that was later today, which felt like medicine. I left the phone in a tray with a numbered stub, suddenly a person in 1998, and walked into the high street with nothing in my pocket but keys and the stub.

An hour without a phone is a long corridor. I went into a bookshop and read jackets. I stood in a cafe and watched the door as if someone might need me in a way that required a device. Nobody did. A clock above the till became interesting. I bought a newspaper I would not finish. The repair shop's window showed a waterfall of phone cases in colours that assumed a future of drops.

When I returned the screen was a lake again. He showed me the inside briefly, a courtesy. I paid and immediately used the phone to check that it was a phone. Messages had accumulated like dust. None of them were emergencies, which I both wanted and did not. Outside I put it in my pocket and then took it out again, a habit the crack had not taught me to break. I walked home the long way to use up the hour's leftover attention. At the flat I put the stub in the bin. The glass is bright and I am already slightly afraid of it. That is not the shop's fault. It is the drop, waiting somewhere in the future like a relative who visits without warning.
