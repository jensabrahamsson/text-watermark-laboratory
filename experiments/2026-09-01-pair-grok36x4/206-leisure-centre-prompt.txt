The leisure centre smelled of chlorine even in the corridor where no water was visible. I had booked the gym because the swimming slots were full of people who had made better decisions earlier. The receptionist gave me a band for my wrist and a look that said I was late for my own appointment with myself. Changing rooms were a geometry of lockers that did not quite close. I put my shoes in one and did not trust it.

The gym was a room of televisions showing the news without sound and people moving as if they were being paid in minutes. I got on a machine whose instructions assumed a previous life. After ten minutes I was simply hot. A man beside me ran as if he were late for a train inside the belt. I watched the car park through high glass: rain, a parent, a child with a float under one arm. The float seemed a more honest object than anything in the room.

I lasted forty minutes by the clock that was trying to be encouraging. In the shower the water came in two temperatures and neither was mine. I dressed with the haste of someone who has remembered an errand. At the desk I was asked if I wanted to join as a member. I said I would think about it, which is how I leave most buildings. Outside the air was cold enough to feel like an achievement. The child with the float had gone in. I walked home along the dual carriageway's pavement, which exists as an afterthought, and the chlorine stayed in my hair until the evening, a souvenir I had not intended to buy.
