The seaside arcade in March was a cave of lights that refused the season. I had come to the town for a funeral and had an hour to kill before the train, which is a sentence I did not say to the attendant who changed notes into coins. The carpet was a pattern designed to hide the sea. Machines sang to nobody. A crane grabbed at teddies with a delicacy that was a kind of mockery. I put in a coin because standing empty-handed felt like a criticism of the room.

I won nothing, which aligned with the day. A child in a coat too big for her was being lifted to reach the buttons by a grandmother who had the patience of tides. I stood aside. Through the open front the promenade was grey and honest. A man was walking a greyhound. I thought of other tracks and other Thursdays and then stopped, because today was not for stacking. I bought a cup of tea from a hatch that also sold chips and ate nothing.

The train was late, as if it had heard about the funeral and did not want to rush me. I sat on a bench facing the water with the leftover coins, which were enough for one more machine and not enough for a gesture. I put them in a charity box shaped like a well, another kind of crane. The arcade lights went on making their case to the afternoon. Walking to the platform I smelled vinegar and electricity. In the carriage I was a person returning from a family event with salt on my shoes. I looked at my phone and did not look at the messages yet. The sea slid away behind a cutting and then it was just fields, which felt like a kindness.
