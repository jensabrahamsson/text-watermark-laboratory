The citizens advice office sat above a shop, up stairs that made the visit already an effort. I had an appointment about a letter from a company that used English as a weapon. The waiting room had posters about rights in several languages and a toy box that had seen duty. I filled in a form I had already filled in online, a ritual of paper. A volunteer called my name as if it were a good name.

The adviser had a cubicle, two screens, and the calm of someone who has read worse letters. We went through mine line by line. She highlighted a sentence I had feared and made it ordinary by naming a regulation. I felt briefly like a person with a case rather than a person with a stomach ache. She typed a reply I would not have dared and printed it for me to sign. I signed. Through the window a bus advertised a loan of the sort that produces letters.

I left with a copy and a next step. On the stairs I passed a man coming up with a folder and the face I had worn. I did not smile; it would have been a claim. In the street I stood in a doorway to put the papers in my bag as if the wind were interested. I bought nothing. Walking home I rehearsed the regulation under my breath, a spell. At the flat I put the copy next to the original letter, a pair. I have not posted the reply yet. I will, tomorrow, when the park-and-ride form also goes, a day of paper leaving the house. Advice is a kind of company. I am trying to remember that when the letter was still only a threat in an envelope.
