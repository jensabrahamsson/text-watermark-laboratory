The community centre advertised a jumble sale, a blood pressure clinic, and a class whose name I did not understand. I had come for none of those. I had come because a notice on the door said the hall could be hired and I wanted to look at a room before deciding I could not afford it. The foyer smelled of floor polish and instant coffee. A woman at a hatch asked if I was here for the clinic and I said no in a voice that made it sound like a character flaw.

The hall had a stage six inches high and a piano with a sign asking people not to put cups on it. Stacked chairs leaned in a way that suggested previous leaning. I walked the length of the room and heard my own shoes too clearly. Through a high window I saw the car park and a man tying a dog to a bicycle stand. The woman from the hatch appeared with keys and a speech about the kettle. She showed me the kitchen, which was a sink and a rule about milk. I nodded as if I had a committee.

I did not hire the hall. I bought a raffle ticket instead because the book was open on the hatch and refusing it would have required a sentence. The prize was a hamper. I have never won a hamper and do not know where I would put one. Outside the dog had been collected. I stood under the canopy until the rain decided something. A class arrived with mats under their arms and the faces of people who had paid in advance. I walked home along the road that still thinks it is a village. The raffle ticket lived in my wallet for a week, then in a drawer, then nowhere I can name, which is how most of my plans end.
