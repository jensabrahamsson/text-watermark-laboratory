The working men's club had a sign that still used an apostrophe with confidence. I was a guest of an uncle who has been a member since the carpet was a different pattern. The door was a code of knocks I failed and then did not need because he was there. Inside: a bar, a snooker table not in use, a function room with its lights off, and a smell of beer that had become architecture. He signed me in as if I were a parcel.

We sat with men who knew his stories and allowed them. I drank what he drank. A quiz machine in the corner blinked with a patience I admired. Someone was organising a trip to the coast in a book that looked like accounts. I was asked what I did and I said a version that fitted the room. They nodded. A woman behind the bar called last orders in a voice that had called them for years and would call them again. My uncle bought a bag of crisps and opened it for the table, a liturgy.

Walking back to his house he talked about members who had died and members who had moved and the club that remained. I listened. The street was the kind that still has a phone box. At his gate I said I had had a good night, which was true in the way of rooms that do not ask you to become someone else, only to sit. He said come again. I might, on a night when being a guest is the right size of belonging. In the taxi home the apostrophe on the sign stayed in my head, a small correctness. I ate a biscuit in the kitchen at midnight and did not turn on the main light, as if I were still in a club that preferred its corners.
