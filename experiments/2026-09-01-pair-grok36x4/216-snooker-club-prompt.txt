The snooker club was downstairs from a row of shut shops, a basement of green cloth and low lamps that made everyone look like they were concentrating even when they were not. I had been invited by a man from work who said it was just a frame. It was never just a frame. Membership required a form and a look. I paid at a hatch where a woman counted coins into a tin and called people love without affection or insult.

I broke poorly. The balls arranged themselves into a problem I could not solve. My colleague played with the economy of someone who had been coming here since a different decade. Around us men spoke in numbers. A television showed a match in another city. I drank a lager that had been poured too long ago. Between shots I looked at the carpet, which had known many years of chalk and patience. A young man at the next table was being taught by his father in a language of taps on the wood.

We played two frames and I lost both in ways that felt educational only to him. I put the cue back on the rack as if returning a borrowed tool. Up the stairs the street was brighter than I was ready for. He talked about a league. I said I would think about it, my usual passport stamp. At home I found chalk on my sleeve and left it there until morning, a small blue fact. I have not gone back, which does not mean I disliked it. It means the basement asked for a regularity I do not currently have, and I am trying, this year, to notice when I say yes to rooms I will not inhabit.
