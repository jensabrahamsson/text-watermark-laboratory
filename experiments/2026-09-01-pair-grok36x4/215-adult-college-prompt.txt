The adult college occupied a building that had been a school and had not quite forgiven the change. I enrolled in evening Spanish because the form was on a table at an open night and I am susceptible to forms. The classroom still had posters of photosynthesis. Our teacher was called Rosa and had the patience of a person who has heard every English vowel. We were twelve: a plumber, two retirees, a woman who took notes like minutes, and me.

I sat at the back because the front felt like claiming a future. We repeated phrases about hotels I would not visit. The plumber was better than he pretended. During the break the vending machine ate a pound and returned nothing, a lesson in another language. I stood with a paper cup of water and listened to someone describe a daughter in Madrid. The corridor smelled of floor polish and bags of chips from the shop next door.

By week four I could ask for a room and not understand the answer. I did not mind. The point had become Tuesday. Walking home I practised under my breath and a cyclist looked at me as if I were a problem. At the flat I wrote verbs in a notebook I had bought for this and would, I knew, eventually use for shopping lists. Rosa said we were doing well. I believed her in the room and not on the bus. That gap is where most of my education lives. I kept going until the term ended, then let the notebook close, which is also a kind of fluency.
