The civic offices were a building that wanted to be glass and had been forced to be practical. I had an appointment about a bin that the collection had missed three times, a grievance so small it had become a principle. Security gave me a badge on a lanyard I wore as if I worked there. The waiting area had a screen of ticket numbers and a plant that was trying. I sat beside a woman with a folder of papers held like a child.

My number was a letter and two digits. The officer had a cubicle and a computer that knew my address better than I do. We looked at a map of my street. She apologised in the official way, which is not nothing. She issued a credit I would never notice on a bill. I thanked her more than the bin deserved. Through the glass I could see the car park and a flag at half something I did not know about. She asked if there was anything else. There was, and there wasn't.

I handed back the lanyard. The woman with the folder was still waiting. Outside I walked past the bins of the civic offices, which were being emptied with a competence my street lacked. I did not take a photograph; I am not that person today. On the bus I sat with the printout of the credit as if it were a verdict. At the flat the bin was still full. Thursday would come. I have learned that civic time and kitchen time are not the same clock. I made tea and put the printout on the fridge with a magnet from a place I have not visited, which is how most of my victories are displayed.
