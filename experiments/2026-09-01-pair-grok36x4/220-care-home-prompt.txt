The care home lounge faced a garden that was mostly lawn and a bird table the birds used when they felt like it. I was visiting my aunt, who sometimes knew me and sometimes knew a version of me from a different year. The corridor smelled of laundry and lunch. A member of staff called me by a name that was nearly right. I did not correct her. In the lounge the television was a cooking programme, always a cooking programme, as if appetite were the last public fact.

My aunt held my hand with a strength that did not match the rest of the afternoon. We looked at the garden. She asked about a dog we have not had in twenty years and I answered as if it were waiting in the car. A man in the next chair was asleep with a newspaper open at the racing. Tea arrived on a trolley and was poured with a competence that made me feel amateur. I blew on my cup although it was not hot. The bird table stayed empty.

When I left I signed a book whose previous entries were the same names in different inks. The receptionist said she was having a good day, meaning my aunt, and I said thank you as if goodness were a service. In the car I sat until the radio came on by itself because I had hit something. I did not cry; I am saving that for a less organised hour. Driving home I passed a school emptying, children with the future still attached. I thought of the cooking programme and of the dog that does not exist. At the flat I rang my sister and we did the report we always do, short and practical, which is how we love each other when the lounge is too far to describe.
