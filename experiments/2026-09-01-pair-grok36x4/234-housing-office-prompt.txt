The housing office was a hall of tickets and glass that had replaced a counter you could lean on. I was not applying. I was accompanying a neighbour whose English is better than the forms and worse than the fear. We took a number. The screen jumped. A security guard walked a route that was a job. My neighbour held her papers in a bag inside a bag. I held the tickets. We did not talk much. The room talked enough, in several languages and one kind of waiting.

When her number showed we went to a window where a man asked for documents as if they were a test she might fail for folding. She did not fail. I said two sentences when a word stuck, then stopped, because this was not my window. He typed. The printer behind him became the most important machine in the building. A letter came out that she read twice. I read it over her shoulder without pretending not to. It was a delay dressed as a process.

We left into a car park that was also a wind. She thanked me in the way of people who would have managed and are still glad of a body in the chair beside them. I walked her to the bus and did not get on. Standing there I felt the uselessness of being only a ticket-holder. At home I made tea I did not want. I will go with her again if the next letter asks for another window. Some help is just not letting a person sit in a hall of glass alone. I put nothing on the fridge. This one is not my victory to magnet.
