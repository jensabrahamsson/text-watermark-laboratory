The harbor freezes at the edges first, a thin skin along the concrete where the tide doesn't reach. By seven it's still dark and the boats knock against their fenders, an irregular sound, wood and rubber and rope, nobody bothering to fix the rhythm of it. Frost on the winches. Frost on the coiled net by the shed door, stiff as wire.

Lars comes down at quarter past, boots first, then the rest of him, and stands a while looking at the water like it might have changed overnight. It hasn't. Grey, moving slow, a skin of ice breaking up near the pilings where his boat rubbed against them all night. He unties the tarp and it comes away crackling, ice flaking off in sheets that fall and don't shatter, just sit there on the dock like broken glass that isn't glass.

The fish house opens at eight though nobody's sure why eight, since nothing's been caught yet to bring there. Ingrid runs the scale and the ledger both, sits with a space heater at her feet and a coffee that goes cold before she drinks half of it. She keeps the door propped with a brick because the latch freezes shut otherwise, and twice a winter someone gets locked in overnight and has to kick the window.

Three boats go out most days in January, four if the cod are running and the ice hasn't come in past the breakwater. The rest sit. Owners come down anyway, out of habit or to check the lines, tighten something that doesn't need tightening. There's a particular quiet to the harbor when the boats are in, different from summer's quiet — summer's is empty, this one is full of things not happening.

The village itself pulls back from the water this time of year. Curtains stay drawn longer. Smoke goes straight up from the chimneys when there's no wind, sideways and thin when there is. The one shop cuts its hours, closes at four, and the woman who runs it says it's not worth the electricity to stay open for nobody. She's not wrong. You can walk the main street at five and see maybe two lit windows and hear a television through one of them, muffled, some program with canned laughter that sounds wrong against the sound of the sea two streets over.

The men who still fish talk about the ice like it's a person with moods. Coming in early this year. Stayed offshore all December, this is the payback. They say it without complaint, the way you'd note a change in weather that had already happened and couldn't be helped. Nobody expects January to be generous. It sets the terms and the terms don't move.

By four the light's already going. The gulls that follow the boats in have less to follow, so they sit in a row on the breakwater instead, facing into the wind, not moving, waiting on nothing in particular. Lars ties the tarp back down. His hands are the color of the harbor.
