Claude responded: Dear Elin,

Dear Elin,

Saturday was slow. I mean properly slow — the kind where you count the people who walk past without stopping and lose track somewhere in the forties. The market opened at eight and by ten I'd sold two jars of the plum chutney and a bag of the small waxy potatoes to a man who asked if they were organic and then argued with me about the price anyway. I told him no, they're not organic, they're just small, and he bought them regardless. People like that.

The weather didn't help. Not rain, which people expect and dress for, but that flat grey cold that gets into your hands through the gloves. I had the paraffin heater under the table again this year, the one your father fixed with the wire coat hanger, and it did nothing except make a smell. By noon I couldn't feel my fingers properly and dropped a jar of the honey, the good spring honey, right on the cobbles. It didn't break, which felt like it should have counted for something, but it rolled under Berit's table and she made a whole thing of retrieving it, her knees cracking the way they do now, and I felt bad for the rest of the afternoon.

Berit's stall was next to mine again — she's moved three times this year, always ends up beside me, we joke it's fate though I think it's actually that nobody else wants to be near the cheese because of the smell in the heat. Not that there was any heat Saturday. She gave me a wedge of the aged one, the one with the rind like tree bark, and I ate it with my fingers standing up because there was nowhere to sit that wasn't wet.

I kept thinking about moving the stall closer to the church end, where the tourists come off the bus, but then I think about the walk with the crates and I don't do it. Maybe next year. I say that every year.

Your uncle came by around one, didn't buy anything, just stood there telling me about his knee and a program he watched about Portugal. I love him but forty minutes of my life I won't get back. He did carry the empty crates to the car after, so I suppose that's the trade.

I sold out of the apple cake by two, which was the one good thing — a woman bought four slices for what she said was a birthday, though she didn't say whose, and I didn't ask. The rest sat there. The chutney, mostly, and a tray of eggs I'll need to use myself now, and I don't even like eggs the way your grandmother did, boiled soft with salt, I've never had the patience.

Packed up at four, earlier than usual, because there was no point staying. The lights were already on in the flower shop across the square, that orange kind that makes everything look older than it is.

Tell Mattias the rabbit food arrived, the big bag, it's by the door.

Love,
Mamma
