The call had come in at 11:40, a smoke detector at the Lindqvist place, and by midnight it was clear the smoke was from a pan of bacon and nothing else. Now it was ten past one and Erik was coiling the last hose while Maja swept grit off the apparatus bay floor with the push broom that had lost half its bristles on one side.

"You'd think they'd learn to open a window," Erik said.

"You'd think." Maja didn't look up from the floor. She pushed the broom in long strokes, herding a small drift of sand and dead leaves toward the drain in the corner.

Erik hung the hose on its rack, checked that the coupling was seated, then went around behind the truck to check the water level gauge, though he already knew it would read full because they hadn't used any water. He checked it anyway. It was a habit from Nils, who'd run this station for eleven years before his hip gave out, and who had told Erik once that you check the gauge every time, false alarm or five-alarm, because the day you skip it is the day the tank's actually low.

"Lights," Maja said, and Erik reached up and flipped the switch bank by the door, killing the overheads one row at a time until only the single bulb over the sign-in desk stayed lit.

She finished sweeping and stood the broom in its corner, bristles up. The station smelled the way it always did this late — diesel and old coffee and the particular mustiness of the turnout gear hanging on its pegs, damp even when it wasn't wet, because the building never quite dried out in October.

"You want to log it or should I," Erik said.

"I'll do it. You did the last one."

She sat at the desk and pulled the logbook toward her, clicked the pen, wrote the time and the address and false alarm — cooking, no injuries in handwriting that slanted hard to the right. Erik watched her write it, not because he needed to but because there wasn't anything else to look at, and the quiet of the bay after a call always took him a minute to settle into.

Maja closed the book and stood, pushed the chair in. Erik was already at the panel, keying in the code for the alarm system, the four beeps sounding too loud in the empty bay. He waited for the confirmation tone, then pulled the personnel door shut behind them and tried the handle twice, the way he always did, though it had never once failed to lock.

Outside the air had gone properly cold, the kind of cold that meant frost by morning. Their two cars sat alone in the gravel lot, headlights off, windows already fogging at the edges. Maja zipped her jacket to the chin.

"Same time Thursday," she said, meaning the drill, not meaning anything else.

"Same time Thursday," Erik said, and got in his car.
