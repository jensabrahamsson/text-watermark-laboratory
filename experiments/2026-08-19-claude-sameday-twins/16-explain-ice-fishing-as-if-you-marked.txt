We went in January, my brother-in-law's idea, and I said yes before I understood what I was agreeing to. He had an auger, a sled full of gear, two collapsible stools, and a thermos he wouldn't let me touch. The lake was called Stora Lövsjön or something like that, I never actually confirmed the name, and it looked less like a lake and more like a parking lot someone had forgotten to plow.

The walk out took twenty minutes. He said it would take ten. My boots were wrong — too much fashion, not enough wool — and by the time we reached the spot my toes had already started that ache that isn't quite pain yet but announces itself as a coming pain, patient about it.

He drilled the hole with the auger, which was louder than I expected, a a churning metal sound that seemed to embarrass itself in all that silence. Ice came up in a long ribbon, curling out of the hole like a wood shaving. He said this meant the ice was good, thick, nothing to worry about. I nodded like I understood what bad ice sounded like.

Then we sat. This is the part nobody prepares you for. You picture the auger, the fish, maybe a dramatic pull on the line, but mostly what happens is you sit on a stool eight centimeters off a frozen lake and you wait, and the cold works up through the stool legs and into you with no urgency at all, like it has an appointment later and is simply passing the time until then.

He had two lines going. I had one, and it sat untouched for most of the two hours, an orange bobber doing nothing against grey ice, grey sky, a grey unbroken line of spruce on the far shore. He talked about work, or maybe his father, I don't remember, because I was busy performing the internal calculus everyone eventually does in cold weather: how bad is this really, could I ask to leave, how long has it actually been versus how long it feels like.

At one point he caught something — a perch, small, indignant, flipping against the ice before he got it into the bucket. I remember being almost offended that a fish had chosen to exist down there, warmer than us, apparently, while we sat up top losing feeling in our hands for the privilege of catching it.

We ate sandwiches that had gone slightly stiff from the cold. The thermos coffee had cooled to lukewarm by the time he finally passed it over. I drank it anyway.

Eventually he said we could head back, not because we were finished but because he could see I wasn't really in it anymore, and I was grateful he didn't make me say so. The walk back felt shorter, the way return walks always do, and my feet didn't really warm up until we were in the car with the heat blasting and he was already talking about doing this again sometime, maybe with better boots for me, and I said sure, sure, not meaning it, watching the frost melt off the windshield in slow uneven patches.
