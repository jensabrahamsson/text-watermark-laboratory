Dear Elin,

I am writing this from a station I cannot pronounce, somewhere north of where I meant to be. The conductor said the name twice and both times it sounded like something breaking.

I fell asleep somewhere after Hallsberg. I remember the trolley coming through with coffee that smelled burnt, and a man across the aisle eating a sandwich very slowly, unwrapping the foil like it was something he'd been asked to be careful with. Then nothing. Then a hand on my shoulder and a light that wasn't the reading light.

The stop I wanted has a name I've said a thousand times without thinking about it, and now I can't remember if I dreamed the announcement or actually heard it and let it pass. There's a version of the last hour where I woke up, saw the platform, and chose to stay seated. I don't think that's what happened but I can't rule it out. The body does strange arithmetic at four in the morning.

The station here has one light working, over the ticket machine, which doesn't take cards. There's a bench bolted to the platform and a timetable behind glass so scratched I had to read it with my phone flashlight, tilting it at an angle until the numbers came clear. Next train south is in two hours and eleven minutes. I've decided not to check again until an hour has passed, because checking makes it longer.

It's cold in the particular way that isn't dangerous, just insistent. I can see my breath if I look for it. There's a dog somewhere, not barking exactly, more like commenting.

I keep thinking about the sandwich man. I wonder if he got where he was going, if he's already home, taking his shoes off in some hallway I'll never see. It's strange how completely people disappear once the train doors close. He knew, I think, exactly where he was. That seemed like a skill for a while there, watching him fold the foil into a small square and put it in his coat pocket instead of the bin.

I'm not upset about this, or not only upset. There's something almost pleasant about being definitively in the wrong place with definitively nothing to do about it until the 5:47. My phone has fourteen percent battery so I'm going to stop writing to you soon and just sit with the one light and the bench and whatever the dog is saying.

Tell Mikael I'll be late. Tell him it's not a metaphor, I really did fall asleep on a train, though I understand if he doesn't believe that from me specifically.

I'll call when I'm back in range of something.

J.
