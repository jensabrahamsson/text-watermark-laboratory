The rehearsal room smelled of instant coffee and radiator dust. Twenty-two chairs, arranged in a loose semicircle, and seventeen people in them by seven-thirty, which Ingrid noted out loud because Ingrid always noted things out loud.

"We'll start with the Brahms," said Torbjörn, the conductor, a retired music teacher who still wore a tie to rehearsals though nobody asked him to. He raised his hands. The altos came in a beat early. He stopped them.

"From the top."

They tried again. This time the tenors, all three of them, entered on the wrong syllable, turning "Wie lieblich" into something that sounded like a sneeze. Torbjörn set down his baton on the music stand, picked it back up, and said nothing for a moment.

"Let's just speak the words first."

They spoke the words. It went fine. It always went fine when they spoke the words.

Sing again, and the sopranos drifted flat by the fourth bar, the way water finds a slope without meaning to. Margit, in the front row, kept glancing at her phone, which was face-up on her lap, screen dimmed but not dark. Her daughter was in the hospital with a kidney stone, not serious, but not nothing either, and Margit had told two people this and would tell no one else, and so she watched the phone and sang half a beat behind everyone.

"Sopranos, listen to the piano," Torbjörn said, though the piano was being played by Elsa, who had a head cold and kept swallowing at the ends of phrases, adding tiny clicks to the Brahms that were not in Brahms.

They started a third time. A phone rang — not Margit's, someone else's, buried in a coat on the back row — and by the time it was silenced, half the choir had stopped singing to see whose it was, and the other half kept going, so for four bars there were two choirs, one a measure behind the other, both wrong.

Torbjörn sat down. This was unusual. He never sat down.

"Ten minutes," he said, "then we try the Bach instead."

During the break, Ingrid told someone the Brahms was too hard for this group and always had been. Karl-Erik, eighty-one, who had sung this piece annually for eleven years without ever getting the entrance right, defended it. Their voices overlapped, the way the sopranos had overlapped with the pianist's cough, the way everything in the room seemed to overlap without meeting.

At ten past eight they reconvened. Torbjörn raised his hands again. Someone's chair scraped. Elsa found her place in the score. Margit turned her phone face-down, finally, and looked up.

"From the top," Torbjörn said, for the fourth time, and this time, for reasons no one could have named, the entrance came in together — ragged, a little sharp, Elsa still clicking faintly through her cold — but together, four bars of it, before the second sopranos lost the thread and the whole thing came apart again, the way it always did, the way it would again next Tuesday.
