Old Sten from two houses down didn't so much teach me bike repair as tolerate my presence while he fixed his own. He'd already have the wheel off before I finished saying good morning.

First thing he showed me was the puncture, because that's the only thing that actually happens to you on a Tuesday. He didn't explain how tires work. He just yanked the tire off the rim with two flat spoons — not levers, spoons, because that's what was in his shed — and said "you don't need special tools, you need the right angle." Pump the tube up a bit, dunk it in the bucket he kept by the door for exactly this, watch for bubbles. Find the hole. Mark it with the pencil stub that lived in his shirt pocket, not because you'll remember where it was, but because you won't and this is the only way you'll find it again in the next ten seconds.

He hated the glue-and-patch kit instructions. "It doesn't say wait five minutes because five minutes is magic. It says that because the glue has to stop being wet. Touch it. If it's tacky, wait longer." That was the whole lesson on adhesives.

Putting the tire back on, he'd do the last bit with his thumbs and get annoyed if I reached for a lever. "If you need the lever to get it back on, you used the lever to get it off wrong." I still don't fully agree with him on this but I hear his voice every time I do it.

Brakes he covered in about ninety seconds, standing in his driveway holding the bike upside down by the seat and handlebars, wheels spinning in the air. Squeeze the lever. If it goes to the bar, cable's stretched or the pads are shot. Look at the pads — worn at an angle means the wheel's not true, not that the pads are bad. Barrel adjuster, quarter turn, test again. He never once said "torque" or "tension." He said "too loose" and "too tight" and expected you to feel the difference, which, eventually, you do.

The chain he judged with his hand, not a tool. Pinch it, lift it off the big ring. If it comes up more than you'd like, it's stretched, replace it before it wears the cassette too, because cassettes cost more than chains and he resented spending money on things that could've been prevented.

He never once said the word "maintenance." He said "you're going to want to check this before winter" or "do this now while it's already apart," which is the same thing but sounds less like homework.

The one rule he actually stated out loud, more than once, annoyed each time he had to repeat it: everything on a bike is either turning, gripping, or sealing something out. If it's not working, it's usually because one of those three stopped happening, and your job is just to figure out which one before you start taking things apart looking for it.
