The ferry dropped us a little after seven, late enough that the ticket booth had already pulled its shutter halfway down. Salt sat on the railings and on the back of my tongue. A man in a yellow coat hosed fish crates into the gutter; the water ran pink for a few metres and then just looked like water again. Nobody rushed. That was the first thing I noticed. People stood in doorways with plastic cups and watched the last of the light go out of the harbour as if it were a scheduled show.

The hotel sign buzzed on one letter only. The rest of the name was a dark rectangle against the brick. Inside, the lobby smelled of boiled vegetables and floor polish. The key was attached to a wooden block too large for any pocket, which I took as a local joke about running off without paying. From the window I could see the masts and a closed ice-cream hatch with a handwritten note about tomorrow.

I walked anyway. Fried onions from a van that was packing up. A radio in Spanish or Portuguese from an open flat. Two teenagers sat on the sea wall and did not look up when I passed. I had a return ticket for Sunday and no good reason to use it. The town did not promise anything. It just kept being there after each corner, which is how staying longer usually starts.
