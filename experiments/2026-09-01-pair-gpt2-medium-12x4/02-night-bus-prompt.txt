The night bus is a different vehicle from the one that runs at noon, even when the number on the front is the same. At eleven the seats have a sour fabric smell and the driver knows every regular by the way they tap the card. A woman in a supermarket uniform counts coins into a zip bag. A student sleeps with a violin case between their knees. Nobody talks except to say thanks when they get off, and even that is half a word.

I take this route when the trains have finished and I have stayed too long at someone else's table. The windows show the same shops in a different order: pharmacy, shuttered bakery, the gym that leaves its lights on. Rain makes the glass useless. You watch your own face instead, older than you feel in the kitchen you just left.

At the depot loop the driver opens both doors and eats something from a foil tray. Two minutes. Then we go back the other way with fewer people. I always think I will walk the last stretch and I never do. The stop nearest my street has no shelter. You stand under the tree anyway, because that is what the others do, and the bus appears exactly when you have decided it will not.
