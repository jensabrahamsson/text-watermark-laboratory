Claude responded: The vending machine hummed louder than the television.

The vending machine hummed louder than the television. Someone had turned the sound off but left the captions running, a cooking show, chef's mouth moving around words that scrolled past too fast to read. Elin sat under it anyway, watching the numbers count down on a can of orange soda she hadn't decided to buy.

Her father was somewhere behind the double doors, on the other side of a sign that said STAFF ONLY in red letters gone pink with age. She had counted the ceiling tiles twice. Fifty-eight, both times, though she suspected she'd miscounted the same square once and corrected it wrong the second time.

A man came in holding his hand wrapped in a dish towel, blood coming through in a shape like a continent. He gave his name to the woman at the desk, who typed without looking up, and then he sat three chairs down from Elin and closed his eyes like he was tired rather than hurt.

"You waiting on someone," he said. Not really a question.

"My dad."

"Long time?"

"Two hours. Maybe more." She looked at her phone. "Two hours nineteen."

He nodded at that, at the specificity of it, like it made sense to him. The towel had gone from red to a darker red, and he didn't seem to notice or didn't care to.

"They took my brother back there once," he said. "Motorcycle. He came out fine. Walked out on his own two legs complaining about the bill."

"That's good."

"It is good." He said it like he was reminding himself. Then he was quiet, and the captions kept scrolling — and now we'll fold in the — and neither of them read the rest.

A nurse came through the doors, not for Elin, for an older woman across the room who stood up so fast her purse fell and spilled a small avalanche of receipts and hard candy across the floor. Nobody helped her pick it up. She did it herself, on her knees, gathering it into her lap before she followed the nurse.

"You want half of this," the man said, holding out nothing, then remembering. "Sorry. Thought I had gum."

"It's fine."

"You want me to go find someone. Ask what's taking so long."

"No," Elin said. "They said they'd come get me."

He accepted this. The dish towel was fully dark now, and he shifted it in his lap like a sleeping animal he didn't want to wake.

The automatic doors at the entrance opened for no one, just the wind, and closed again. Somewhere down the hall a cart with a bad wheel squeaked past, twice, going and coming back. Elin picked the soda can up off the machine tray, turned it once in her hand, and set it back down without opening it.

"Fifty-eight," she said, to no one.

"What's that."

"Nothing. Ceiling tiles."

He looked up, counted three of them, and gave up.
