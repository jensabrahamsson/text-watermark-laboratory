The shutter goes up at seven-ten, ten minutes late because the chain caught on the bracket again, and Torbjörn has to shake it twice before it rattles free. Inside it smells like cold cement and machine oil, the particular smell of a room that's been locked overnight with a space heater dying in the corner. He doesn't turn the lights on all at once — first the register, then the back wall, then the aisle with the fasteners, because the fluorescent tubes flicker for a good minute before they settle and he's learned not to watch them do it.

The delivery from the wholesaler is already on the step, three boxes and a crate of paint thinner cans stacked crooked, and one of the boxes has a torn corner where something — screws, probably — has been leaking a thin brown trail across the cardboard and onto the mat. He drags them in with his foot, not bothering to lift, and leaves them by the counter to deal with after coffee.

The coffee machine is from 2003 and takes four minutes to heat, during which he goes through yesterday's receipts, most of them small — a box of wood screws, a tube of silicone, a padlock. One receipt for a hundred and forty kronor of picture hooks that he remembers ringing up for the woman with the dog, the one who always apologizes for the dog even though nobody's asked her to.

By eight the radio is on, tuned to whatever it was tuned to last, some talk program about ferry schedules, and he doesn't change it. He starts unpacking the fastener box, sorting by hand into the little wooden drawers that have been there since before he owned the place — M4, M5, M6, the labels half rubbed off so he goes by memory as much as by the faded ink. Somewhere in the second drawer down there's a scattering of wrong-sized washers that shouldn't be there and never seem to leave.

The first customer is a contractor, in and out in ninety seconds, doesn't say hello, just says "the usual caulk, the good one" and drops exact change on the counter without looking up from his phone. The second is an older man who wants advice on a dripping tap and stays for twenty-two minutes, most of which is not about the tap.

Around nine the boy who does deliveries on Tuesdays and Fridays comes in for his list, and Torbjörn writes it on the back of an old invoice because the good notepad is somewhere under the crate of thinner. Outside, someone's parked half on the curb and is unloading lumber from a trailer, and a horn goes off two streets over for reasons nobody will ever know.

By half past nine the shop still smells like oil and the heater's finally caught up, and the drawer of washers is still wrong, and the receipt for the picture hooks is still sitting under a coffee ring on the counter because he forgot to file it, the way he forgets most days, and will again tomorrow.
