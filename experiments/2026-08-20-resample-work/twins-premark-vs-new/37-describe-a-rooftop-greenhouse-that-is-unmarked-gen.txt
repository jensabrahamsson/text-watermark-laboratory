The greenhouse sits on top of the parking garage like something that was promised more than it received. Eight years ago someone drew this up on a napkin—urban agriculture, community resilience, a rooftop Eden feeding the block below—and for maybe two seasons it almost worked. Now half the panels have gone opaque with algae bloom, a green-white film that turns the afternoon light into something underwater and sickly. You can still see, if you squint, where the original grid of aluminum struts was supposed to hold everything true, but three of them have buckled under snow load and were never replaced, just wired to their neighbors with mismatched gauge, a temporary fix now older than some of the plants.

Inside, the air is wrong before you notice why. Too still. The exhaust fans at the far end turn lazily when they turn at all, one blade clipped short from some old collision, throwing the whole assembly slightly off-balance so it ticks against its housing every third rotation. The irrigation lines run along the beds like exposed nerves, and at least a third of the emitters have calcified shut, leaving pale rings of mineral crust on the soil where water used to arrive and now doesn't. Somebody has gone through with a marker and labeled certain beds DO NOT WATER MANUALLY in handwriting that's already faded to gray, and beside it, in a different hand, someone else has written why not.

The plants tell the real story better than the infrastructure does. There are tomato vines that have given up on producing and just sprawl now, thick and woody and strange, reaching for structural support that isn't there, bowing the trellis wire into slack catenaries. A stand of peppers has gone glossy and small, stunted in a way that suggests root rot rather than neglect, which is worse, because it means the failure is underground and invisible and has been happening for a long time before anyone noticed. Only the mint has thrived, which is its own kind of joke—it has escaped its bed entirely and colonized the gravel path, and nobody has had the will to pull it.

There's a corkboard by the entrance still pinned with a watering schedule, a volunteer sign-up sheet, a laminated sheet of companion-planting diagrams. The sign-up sheet has four names on it, three of which have lines through them. The board itself is coming unglued from the wall, curling forward at one corner like it's trying to look at its own shadow.

What gets you, standing in the doorway, is how much of the original intention is still legible underneath the decay—the raised beds still in their geometric rows, the drip system still shaped like a plan, the little hand-painted signs for herbs long since dead. It isn't a ruin exactly. It's more like a sentence that trails off, that you can still tell what it meant to say.
