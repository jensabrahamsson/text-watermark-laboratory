The basement of St. Anselm's smelled like coffee grounds and the inside of an old piano. Fluorescent tubes buzzed above six folding tables, and on Tuesday nights, from seven to ten, the Lerum Chess Club took over the space between the Sunday school shelves and the stacked communion tables.

Halvar arrived first, as always, and unfolded the tables in the same order he had for eleven years — the two good tables by the window, the wobbly one pushed against the wall for beginners. He set out the clocks, checked their batteries with a small screwdriver he kept in his coat pocket, and made a pot of coffee that nobody would finish.

Ingrid came next, carrying her set in a velvet bag her husband had sewn before he died. Her pieces were heavier than regulation, worn smooth at the knights' ears. She played the Caro-Kann against everyone, without exception, and had done so since 1994.

The younger players trickled in around quarter past — a boy named Emil who was fourteen and rated higher than anyone else in the room, a couple from the housing office who played only each other, and a man everyone called the Norwegian though he'd lived in Lerum longer than most of them.

Tonight Halvar paired Emil against Ingrid, which he did every few months, partly to watch and partly because Ingrid asked for it. Emil played the Sicilian. Ingrid, predictably, met it with her Caro-Kann transposition tricks, and within twelve moves the position had gone somewhere neither of them had prepared for.

The pastor's assistant, a woman named Sara who used the basement for a knitting circle on Thursdays, came down at eight to ask if anyone had seen a missing box of hymnals. Nobody had. She stayed a few minutes anyway, watching Emil's hands hover over the board without touching anything, and then went back upstairs.

Around nine, the radiator started ticking the way it did when the heat came on, and the Norwegian complained, as he did every week, that the ticking broke his concentration. Nobody moved to fix it. It had never been fixed.

Emil won on move thirty-one, a rook endgame that Ingrid resigned two moves before mate because she said she could see it coming and didn't want to sit through the formality. She shook his hand, reset the pieces into their velvet bag in the order she always used, and went to refill her coffee though she hadn't drunk any of the first cup.

By ten, Halvar collected the clocks and wiped down the tables with a rag that lived under the sink upstairs. The couple from the housing office left holding hands, still arguing about a move from an hour earlier. The Norwegian lingered by the door, buttoning his coat slowly, watching Emil pack his own set — a cheap plastic one, the kind sold in the toy aisle — into a backpack next to a math textbook.

Halvar turned off the lights in sections, the way you're supposed to, and locked the outer door. Upstairs, the sanctuary was dark except for the small red light above the organ. Outside, it had started to rain, the kind that wouldn't stop until morning.
