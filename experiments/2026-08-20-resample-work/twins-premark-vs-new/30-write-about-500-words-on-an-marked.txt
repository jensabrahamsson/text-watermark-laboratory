The pool sits behind a chain-link fence that someone locks every October and unlocks every May, and in between it just holds whatever weather does to it. Right now that's about four centimeters of grey ice with leaves frozen into it like things preserved in aspic, and a skin of snow on top that nobody has walked across because there's no reason to.

You can see it from the road if you know to look — a rectangle of white cut into the brown grass, the lane-rope stanchions still bolted along the sides even though the ropes themselves get taken down for the season. They look a bit like the abandoned staffs of a fence long dismantled. The lifeguard chair is still up too, wrapped in a blue tarp that's come loose at one corner and slaps against the frame when the wind picks up. That sound carries a long way when there's nothing else going on.

In summer this place is loud enough that you can hear it from two streets over — the flat slap of kids doing cannonballs, a whistle, someone's radio. Now the loudest thing is the tarp, and under that, if you stand at the fence for a while, you start to notice smaller sounds: the pump house ticking as it cools, a branch somewhere dropping its load of snow onto another branch. The changing rooms are dark. There's a padlock on the door with rust already bleeding down from the shackle, which seems fast for a lock that's maybe four months old.

Somebody's left a kickboard on the pool deck, half buried now, one corner of it poking up through the snow like it's trying to remind you what the deck is for. It'll probably still be there in May, a little sun-bleached, and someone on staff will pick it up without thinking about it, the way you pick up a thing that's obviously supposed to be somewhere else.

The water under the ice hasn't been drained — you can tell because the ice has that particular flatness, no wrinkles, the kind you get over standing water rather than a wet floor. It means in a thaw the whole thing could crack unevenly, and there's usually a sign about that, THIN ICE in orange letters, though right now it's crusted over with snow so you'd have to already know what it says.

There's something almost funny about a pool in winter — a piece of infrastructure built entirely around a season, sitting there fully intact and doing nothing, like a theatre with the seats still bolted in but the doors chained. Not sad exactly. Just off-duty. The kind of thing you'd forget was even shaped like a pool if you didn't already know, if you hadn't swum forty lengths of it on some July evening with the light going orange and the smell of chlorine and cut grass mixed together in a way that, for whatever reason, sticks.
