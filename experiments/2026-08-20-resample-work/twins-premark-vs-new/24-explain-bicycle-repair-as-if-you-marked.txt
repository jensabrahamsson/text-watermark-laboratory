Old man Berglund next door fixed bikes the way other people yell at referees—fast, loud, and like you were personally offending him by not already knowing. I learned everything I know about bicycles standing in his driveway getting handed tools I'd never heard of and told to "just feel it, for Christ's sake."

The chain, for instance. I asked him once how you know if a chain's stretched. He didn't explain. He grabbed the chain at the front ring, yanked it away from the teeth, and said "see that gap, that's it, that's stretch, now you know," and walked back inside to get coffee while I stood there with a chain in my hand trying to reverse-engineer what I'd just seen. That's basically the whole pedagogy. You get shown the symptom once, at speed, and you're expected to build the theory yourself afterward, usually while he's not watching.

Brakes were the same. Pads squealing, he'd just say "toe them in," hand me an Allen key, and vanish. Took me two summers to understand that meant angling the pad so the front edge hits the rim a hair before the back—so it doesn't shudder and scream when you brake. He never once said "front edge" or "shudder." I got there by ruining three sets of pads and finally noticing the wear pattern myself.

He was allergic to anything resembling a checklist. If you asked "what do I check before a ride," he'd say "does it feel wrong," like that was an answer instead of a taunt. Eventually you realize it kind of is one. A wheel with a loose bearing doesn't spin cleanly—it's got this faint grinding hesitation you can feel through your palm if you spin it and touch the axle. He never taught me the word "bearing preload." He taught me the feeling first and made me go find the word later, alone, out of spite basically.

Flats were his one moment of patience, and even that was suspicious. He'd make you find the puncture by ear and touch before he'd let you near a bucket of water—running your thumb slow around the inside of the tube, feeling for the thorn or the sliver of glass still sitting in the tire casing, because "you patch it and don't get the thing that caused it, you'll be back here in a week, and I'm not helping you twice." Only after you'd failed to find it by feel did he let you dunk it and watch for bubbles, and he'd sigh the whole time like the water was doing your job for you.

Gears, derailleur limits, the barrel adjuster—all delivered in that same clipped, half-sentence way. "Turn it till it stops skipping, other way if it's rubbing." No numbers, no diagram, no sequence. Just a direction to move something and a result to notice, and you were on your own to connect the two.

I don't actually know if he was a good teacher. I know I can true a wheel by eye now, that I trust my hands more than any spec sheet, and that somewhere in there I picked up his exact tone of contempt for people who bring their bike to a shop for a flat.
