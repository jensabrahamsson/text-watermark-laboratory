The church hall smelled like radiator dust and coffee left too long on the warmer. Twenty-two chairs in a horseshoe, though only sixteen people had come. Berit counted them twice from the piano, mouthing numbers, then gave up and hit the opening chord for "Härlig är jorden" a half-beat too early.

"From the top," said Torbjörn, tapping his baton against the music stand. He was not a real conductor. He had been a math teacher for thirty-one years and had taken over the choir when Ingrid's hip gave out, and he still counted beats the way he'd once counted out fractions on a blackboard, deliberate, a little pained.

The altos came in late. They always came in late, all four of them, as if they'd agreed beforehand to wait each other out.

"Altos," Torbjörn said, not unkindly.

"We can hear it," said Marianne, who was seventy-eight and had sung alto in this hall for forty years, through three different pastors and two organ replacements. "We can hear it, we just can't find it."

They tried again. This time the tenors, all two of them, drifted flat on the descending line, and Lasse, who worked at the sawmill and had a voice that could fill the hall by itself, stopped singing altogether and stared at the ceiling as if the correct note might be written up there among the water stains.

"Let's take sixteen," Torbjörn said.

Someone's phone went off, the marimba ringtone, apologetic and loud. Gudrun dug through her purse with her music sheet still pinched under one arm, and when she found the phone the sheet slid to the floor, and by the time she'd retrieved both the choir had lost the thread entirely and Berit had stopped playing and was just sitting with her hands in her lap.

"Should we do the Britten instead," someone offered, though nobody had touched the Britten in three weeks and it was worse, much worse, a piece that required eight-part harmony from a choir that could sometimes manage four.

"No," said Torbjörn. "Härlig är jorden. From sixteen."

Outside a car alarm started, stopped, started again. The radiator ticked. Marianne coughed into her elbow, a real cough, and two other altos exchanged a look that had nothing to do with the music.

They got through it on the fourth attempt, more or less, the altos landing somewhere close to the note, the tenors compensating for their earlier flatness by going slightly sharp instead, Lasse coming back in on the last verse like he'd never left. It was not good. It would not have impressed anyone who wandered in from the street. But for about eleven bars near the end, the sound filled the hall in a way that made Berit look up from the keyboard, and Torbjörn's baton hand went still, just holding the beat instead of chopping it, and nobody in the horseshoe seemed to be thinking about their phones or their hips or the drive home in the dark.

Then it ended, and Torbjörn said, "Again," and someone's chair scraped, and the moment was gone.
