Thought for 1s

The press room smelled like hot metal and ozone by four in the morning, which was when it usually started. Bengt had been running the Heidelberg for eleven years and he still couldn't predict it. Some nights the paper fed through clean, sheet after sheet, the folder catching them like a card dealer, and he'd stand there almost bored. Other nights, like this one, it jammed on the first hundred copies and kept jamming.

He killed the feed and opened the guard. A sheet had folded on itself somewhere past the second roller, crumpled into something that looked less like newsprint and more like a fist. He worked it free with two fingers, careful not to tear it further into the mechanism, and checked the tension on the guide bars. Too loose. He'd told Anders about this in the handover notes three shifts running.

The front page that night was a piece about the harbor expansion vote, a photo of the council chair looking uncomfortable under fluorescent light, and a smaller item about a missing cat that the editor had insisted on keeping above the fold because readers liked it more than they liked harbor politics. None of that mattered to the machine. The machine did not care what was printed on the paper, only that the paper moved through it in the right sequence, at the right speed, without buckling.

It jammed again forty minutes later, a different fault this time, ink building up on the blanket cylinder in a way that smeared the last third of every sheet into a gray fog. Bengt swore under his breath, more out of habit than real anger, and reached for the solvent rag. His hands were black by five. They were always black by five. His wife used to ask him about it and now she just handed him the nail brush without asking.

Lasse came in at five-fifteen to help with the insert stacking and found Bengt on his knees in front of the open press, headlamp on, tracing a strip of torn paper back through the rollers to find where it had first caught. "Bad night," Lasse said, not really a question.

"Guide bar's shot. I'll write it up again."

"They'll fix it eventually."

"Eventually the paper folds and it doesn't matter."

Neither of them laughed, though it wasn't really a joke either. The regional chain had already cut the run from six days to five, and there was talk, always talk, about outsourcing the printing to a plant two hours north, one with newer presses that didn't need a man on his knees at five in the morning coaxing torn paper out of a guide roller.

By six they had a stack of papers that would actually reach doorsteps, maybe eighty percent of the run, the rest scrapped or held back for reprint later that afternoon if anyone bothered. Bengt wiped his hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime around 2019 and looked at the finished stack, still warm, ink still faintly wet at the edges if you pressed a thumb to it.

The missing cat, it turned out, had been found. It was on page one, small, next to the harbor vote, printed just slightly crooked because of the jam nobody upstairs would ever know about.
