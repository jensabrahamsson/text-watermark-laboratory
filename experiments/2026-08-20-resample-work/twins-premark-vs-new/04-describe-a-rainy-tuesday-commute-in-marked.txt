The platform smells of wet wool before the train even arrives. Gothenburg in November, half past seven, and the rain has settled into the kind that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air, fine enough that umbrellas seem like an overreaction but everyone carries one anyway, black nylon domes bobbing along the platform edge. Water beads on the surface of a phone screen. A man wipes his glasses with a handkerchief that's already too damp to do the job.

The tram groans in on its rails, brakes hissing, and the doors open with that pneumatic exhale. Inside, the windows are fogged from the inside, condensation running in slow diagonal tracks where someone's breath has met the cold glass. The floor is a mess of tracked-in gravel and leaf mulch, dark water pooling near the doors where boots have shed it. A woman shakes her umbrella once before folding it, spraying the ankles of the man next to her, who says nothing, just shifts his bag.

The smell inside is specific: wet denim, coffee from a paper cup with a plastic lid, someone's damp dog on a leash pressed against its owner's leg, panting with its ears back. The tram's heating is on too high, so the windows steam faster than they can be wiped, and a kid draws a wobbly sun in the fog with one finger before an adult tells them to stop touching the glass.

Outside, the city goes by in smears of color — a yellow tram at the crossing, red brake lights doubled in the wet asphalt, the green of a pharmacy cross blinking through the rain. Streetlights are still on even though it's technically morning; the sky hasn't committed to being light yet, just a flat grey that presses down over the rooftops. Gulls sit hunched on a chimney, feathers slicked flat, not bothering to fly.

At the next stop, more bodies press in. A man in a suit holds his umbrella collapsed but still dripping, careful to angle it away from his trousers, not quite succeeding. Someone's shopping bag has gone translucent with water, the cardboard box inside visibly softening. Conversations are low, clipped — people checking phones, not talking, earbuds in. The rain outside intensifies for a stretch, hammering the roof of the tram hard enough to be heard over the electric hum, then eases again.

A cyclist passes alongside the tram at a stoplight, hood up, reflective vest soaked dark at the shoulders, pedaling standing up through a puddle that throws water up past his knees. He doesn't flinch. At the crossing, a woman waits with a stroller under a clear plastic rain cover, the baby inside dry and staring out at the blurred world through the plastic, unbothered.

The tram doors open again and the cold gets in immediately, cutting through the damp-wool warmth that had built up inside. More people leave than board this time — the university stop, backpacks slung over one shoulder, hoods still up even though they're heading indoors. The floor is wetter now, almost slick, and the doors take a second longer to close, held by a sensor as someone jogs the last few steps, breathless, apologizing to no one in particular.

Further down the line, the rain doesn't let up. It rarely does, this time of year, this far north. The tram continues past shuttered kiosks and a bakery with its lights on, someone inside arranging pastries behind glass already fogged from the oven's heat meeting the cold morning. A woman gets off two stops early, deciding to walk the rest, opening her umbrella the moment the doors part, stepping into it like stepping into water she already expected to be cold.
