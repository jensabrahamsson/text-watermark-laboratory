The desk is too small for the man behind it, or maybe he has just arranged himself to look that way, elbows out, shoulders hunched over a paperback with a cracked spine. His name badge says RAGNAR though nobody calls him that. Above him a clock with a dead second hand reads 11:47, permanently, and has for as long as anyone working the day shift can remember.

He keeps a thermos of coffee that stopped being hot around nine and a small transistor radio tuned low to a talk station in a language that isn't Swedish, isn't English, something with long vowels that fill the empty lobby like water finding the bottom of a glass. The lobby itself is brown — brown carpet gone thin in a path from door to elevator, brown vinyl chairs nobody sits in, a brown ficus that is plastic and dusty in a way that makes the dust look intentional, like a design choice.

Guests come in three kinds after midnight. The ones checking in late off a delayed flight, who want only the key and the room number and to be left alone, and he obliges, sliding the card across without looking up from the register unless the payment fails. The ones who forgot something in their room and need the door opened again, sheepish, apologizing for a thing that requires no apology, and he goes up with them, keys jingling on a wide ring clipped to his belt loop, walks slower than they'd like. And the ones who just want to talk, drunk or sleepless or both, leaning on the desk describing a divorce or a business deal or a football match from 1994, and he listens with his chin on his fist, nodding at intervals, offering nothing, asking nothing, letting them wind down like a music box.

The ice machine on the second floor is broken and has been for two years; guests ask about it maybe twice a month and he tells them, without irony, that there is ice on the fourth floor, though the fourth floor machine only sometimes works and only sometimes has ice. He knows the exact rooms with the loudest radiators, the rooms where the wifi doesn't reach, the rooms where the window won't quite close and in winter he gives those out last, as a kind of unspoken mercy, though nobody has ever thanked him for it because nobody knows.

Around three the lobby empties completely and he does the thing he does every night, which is walk once around the ground floor — past the vending machine that eats coins, past the fire door propped with a phone book, past the breakfast room stacked with chairs — not checking anything in particular, just moving through the space the way you might walk a fence line on a farm, out of habit more than purpose. Then back to the desk, the paperback, the radio murmuring its long vowels, the dead clock holding its one frozen minute while the real hours slide past it, one after another, unremarked.
