The museum opens at ten, though Berit has usually had the lights on since half past nine, going through the motions of unlocking display cases that don't need unlocking, dusting glass that isn't dusty. Tuesdays she does this alone. Wednesday through Sunday there's a rotation — her, sometimes Kjell who used to teach woodshop, sometimes the retired postal worker whose name she can never keep straight in her head, Anders or Andreas — but Tuesday is hers by default, because nobody else wants it and she stopped minding a long time ago.

The museum used to be the old dairy cooperative's office building, and you can still see where the counter was, the wood worn pale in a strip where forearms rested for sixty years while people waited on milk prices. Now that strip of counter has a laminated card on it explaining the cooperative's history in four paragraphs nobody reads. Upstairs is farm equipment, some of it still greased. Downstairs is the room everyone actually comes for, when anyone comes at all — the one with the photographs from the 1962 fire that took out half of Storgatan, before Berit's time but not before her mother's, and her mother used to stand in that room for a long time not saying anything, which Berit understood without being told meant something.

On Tuesdays the radiator ticks. She's brought a thermos twice this month because the coffee machine in the back room broke in March and nobody's fixed it, not because there's no money exactly but because fixing it would mean someone driving to Alingsås for the part and nobody's had a reason to make that drive. She sits behind the counter with a crossword, or with knitting, and around eleven a car sometimes slows outside, reads the sign, doesn't stop. Once a Dutch couple came in on a Tuesday in July, lost, looking for the church, and stayed forty minutes anyway because Berit told them about the fire, unprompted, the way her mother might have.

There's a guestbook by the door. Most weeks it gets two or three signatures, school groups mostly, a page of a class's handwriting all at once and then nothing for days. On Tuesdays it stays open to the same page sometimes for a week straight. She doesn't take this personally. She knows what the museum is and isn't — it isn't a destination, it's a thing that exists because someone decided in 1987 that the building shouldn't be torn down, and the least anyone could do afterward was put things in it and open the door.

At four she counts the till, which never needs counting because nothing was sold, and locks up the same way she opened, methodically, checking the radiator, checking the case with the cooperative's old ledgers, the ink gone brown. She drives home past the church the Dutch couple were looking for. Wednesday someone else will open the door. The dust will have settled again by then, in the exact places it always settles, and someone will dust it, and that will be that.
