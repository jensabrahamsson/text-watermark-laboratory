The Rounds

Marlene takes the leashes in a fixed order, not because the dogs care but because she does. First Biscuit, the beagle with the sinus problem, who snorts through every block like he's clearing static from a radio. Then the two Nillsons, mother and son, coats black and matted no matter how often they're brushed. Last is Pepper, the greyhound who belongs to the man on the fourth floor, the one who never opens the door more than the chain allows.

She's been doing this for three years, since the pandemic loosened its grip and people went back to offices and needed someone to fill the gap in the middle of the day. Twelve dollars a walk, cash or app, doesn't matter. She has nine regulars now and a waiting list she doesn't advertise.

The park in autumn empties out the tourists and fills up with locals who know which paths flood after rain. Marlene knows the paths too. She knows that Biscuit will try to eat a chestnut every single time they pass the third oak, and that if she doesn't correct him fast enough he'll spend the rest of the walk gagging quietly, offended at himself.

The Nillsons pull in different directions, always have, some old grudge between them that predates Marlene's employment. She's stopped trying to referee it. She just lets the leashes tangle and untangle themselves, the two dogs orbiting like something that hasn't decided whether it's a fight or a dance.

Pepper doesn't pull. Pepper walks like a dog who's been told, somewhere, that wanting things is a liability. She keeps pace exactly at Marlene's hip, and when other dogs approach she flattens her ears and looks away, and Marlene has learned not to force the greeting.

There's a bench by the pond where Marlene lets them all rest for a few minutes, watching the water do nothing in particular. An old man feeds bread to ducks that don't need it. A woman practices tai chi in a red coat, unbothered by the cold. None of this is remarkable. That's the thing Marlene has come to value about the job — its lack of remarkableness. Nobody's dog has ever run into traffic. Nobody's dog has ever bitten a child or been bitten. The dogs get walked, the owners get their afternoons back, and Marlene gets paid to notice small things: which dog favors a hip, which one flinches at motorcycles, which one has started, this week, to lean into her leg at the bench like it's asking for something without asking.

She doesn't know what happens to these dogs on the days she doesn't come. She doesn't know much about the owners at all — a name, a door code, a note taped to a bowl. It's a small kind of trust, handed to her in fragments, and she carries it the way she carries the leashes, four at a time, without dropping any of them.

By four the light goes flat and gray. She walks the dogs home one by one, and each door closes the same quiet way, and she goes to get the next set of leashes.
