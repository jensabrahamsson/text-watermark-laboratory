Claude responded: Dear Margit,

Dear Margit,

The gale finally dropped this morning, and I can hear myself think again, so I'm writing before the next one comes in — the glass is still low and Ekengren says another front is stacking up west of Väderöarna.

It started a week ago Tuesday. Nothing dramatic at first, just a wind that wouldn't settle, backing and veering all afternoon like it couldn't decide. By evening it had made up its mind. I logged gusts over 30 m/s twice that night and lost count after that, since the anemometer iced over around two and I wasn't going out to clear it until daylight.

You'd have laughed at me, I think, crawling along the gallery rail with a hammer, chipping ice off the lamp housing while the whole tower groaned underneath me like an old ship. It does that in the big blows — a long low sound, more felt than heard, coming up through the floor. I've stopped finding it frightening. Mostly.

The supply boat couldn't make it out Thursday or Friday, so I was down to tinned herring and the last of the rye bread, which had gone hard enough to knock nails in with. I don't mind this part, honestly. There's a kind of relief in having the decision made for you — no boat, no choice, just wait. I made a pot of coffee and ate crackers and read the same forty pages of that Undset novel three times because I kept losing the thread whenever the tower shuddered.

Wednesday night a fishing boat, the Solveig out of Fjällbacka, tried to run for shelter past the point and got into trouble on the reef south of the light. I saw their flare from the gallery — just a smear of red through the spray — and got the radio going, though by the time the rescue boat reached them they'd already managed to work free on their own, engine flooded but running. Nobody hurt. I keep thinking about that flare though. How small it looked. How the sea just kept doing what it was doing regardless of who was out on it.

The birds have had a hard time of it. Found two eiders dead on the rocks below the tower Friday morning, and a guillemot that had been thrown clean up onto the walkway, stunned but alive — I put it in a crate with a towel over it and by afternoon it had recovered enough to be furious with me, which I took as a good sign, and let it go.

Now it's quiet enough to hear the water dripping off the rail, and the sun came out for about twenty minutes around noon, which felt almost rude after everything. I hung my wet things on the line even knowing they won't be dry before the next front, just for something to do with my hands.

Tell Erik I still have his knife — I found it wedged behind the toolbox where it must have slid during one of the rougher nights. I'll send it back with the boat whenever it decides to start running again.

Love to you both,
Anders
