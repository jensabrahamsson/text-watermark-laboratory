Dear Mikael,

Your letter sat on the kitchen table for three days before I opened it. Not because I didn't want to read it — I knew your handwriting on the envelope right away, that slant you never lost — but because I wanted to have coffee first, and time, and not be interrupted by Anna calling about the rabbits or the boiler making its noise again.

I remember you mostly by the back row, third seat, and the way you'd finish your work early and then just sit there, not bothering anyone, looking out the window at the parking lot like there was something to see. There wasn't. I checked once.

You asked if I remember the class trip to Läckö. I do, though not the way you probably want me to. I remember the bus breaking down outside Lidköping and Elin crying because she thought we'd miss the boat, and you were the one who went and sat next to her, not saying much, just sitting there until she stopped. I don't think I ever told you that I noticed. Teachers notice more than we let on, and say less than we should.

Thirty years is a strange number to hear from someone. You write that you're an engineer now, in Gothenburg, two children, and that you think about that year more than you'd expect to. I won't pretend I have some tidy explanation for why certain years stay with people. I taught for thirty-one years and most of them have gone soft at the edges, one class blurring into the next. But a few stay sharp. Yours was one of the sharp ones, though I couldn't tell you exactly why, and I'm suspicious of anyone who claims they can.

I'm retired now, properly, not the kind of retired where you still get pulled in for parent meetings. Lars and I moved out to the countryside — well, not far, but far enough that I have a garden now and complain about slugs. I read more than I used to and worse things than I used to, mostly. There's a strange freedom in that.

You ask if I have advice for your daughter, who apparently doesn't like maths and won't be told otherwise. I don't have advice, not really. I never did, not for the ones who needed convincing. What I had was time, and the willingness to sit next to someone until they stopped crying, or stopped hating fractions, or just stopped being twelve and furious for five minutes. That's not advice. That's just what the job was.

Write again if you feel like it. Don't feel obligated. I know how these things go — a letter answered out of politeness becomes a habit neither person really wants, and then it just stops, and that's fine too. But I'd like to hear how the engineering is going, and whether your daughter's come around on the maths, though I suspect she won't, not really, not for a while yet.

With warmth,
Birgitta
