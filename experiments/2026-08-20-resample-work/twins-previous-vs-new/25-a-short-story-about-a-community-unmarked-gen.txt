Rehearsal started at seven, which meant it started at seven-twenty, because Barbro's car wouldn't hold a charge below freezing and someone had to go get her.

Lennart tapped his baton on the music stand. Thirty-one people, minus Barbro, minus the two tenors who'd texted the group chat with vague excuses about "work stuff." He had twenty-nine.

"From the top of the Fauré," he said. "Cum Sancto Spiritu."

The sopranos came in a beat late. Not badly late, just late enough that the altos, who'd been counting on them for the entrance, hesitated and came in later still. Lennart stopped them with a flat hand.

"Again. Watch me, not the page."

Second time was worse. Ingrid, first soprano, sang a full step flat on the high passage and didn't seem to notice, and beside her Cecilia adjusted downward to match her rather than hold the pitch, so that by the time they reached the cadence the choir had drifted into some other key entirely, one that didn't have a name.

"Stop, stop." Lennart rubbed his eyes. "Ingrid, that note is A, not G-sharp."

"It felt like G-sharp."

"It's not about feeling."

Someone laughed, not unkindly, and this seemed to embarrass Ingrid more than the correction had.

They tried the piece a third time and got through the whole first section, and for about eleven bars it worked, the sound gathering itself into something that had weight, that pressed against the ribs the way it was supposed to. Lennart closed his eyes briefly. Then Karl-Erik's phone rang in his jacket pocket, the default ringtone, tinny and cheerful, and by the time he'd wrestled it out and silenced it the sopranos had lost their place and come in on the wrong line of text entirely, singing about mercy where the altos were still on glory.

"We could take five," said Barbro, who had arrived by now and was still unwinding her scarf.

"We don't have five to take. Concert's in eleven days."

They didn't take five. They ran the Fauré twice more, each time breaking down in a slightly different place, as though the piece contained some fault they were tunnelling toward from different directions and hadn't yet met in the middle. On the last attempt they made it almost to the end before the basses, who'd been quiet and reliable all evening, came in a bar early on the final "Amen," dragging everyone into a pileup of consonants.

Lennart set down the baton.

"Let's do the Britten instead. Something easier."

Nobody moved for a second. Then Cecilia flipped pages, and the sound of thirty-odd people finding the same spot in a score filled the room, paper and shuffling and someone clearing their throat. Lennart raised his hands. Waited for the room to settle into something like stillness.

"From measure nine," he said. "Sopranos, watch me this time."

They watched him. It didn't help much. But they sang anyway, into the flat fluorescent light, past nine o'clock, past the point where any of them still expected it to come together, and something in the room kept them there past that point too.
