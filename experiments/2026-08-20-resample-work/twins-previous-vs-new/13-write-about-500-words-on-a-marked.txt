The station occupies the back half of what used to be a hardware store, and you can still see the paint-mixing counter under the mixing board if you look. WKPL, 91.3, community license, broadcasting out of a town of maybe four thousand people, though the signal reaches farther than that on a clear night. The CD rack sits against the east wall, floor to ceiling, twelve shelves deep, and nobody has counted the discs in years. Somewhere past three thousand. Jewel cases gone cloudy with dust that isn't quite dust — more like a film, the residue of a building that used to sell drywall compound.

Gary runs the Tuesday night shift, six to nine, a slot called Lower Frequencies that he's hosted since 1997. He still pulls physical discs. Not because the station lacks a digital library — they've got one, built out around 2015 when a grant paid for the software — but because Gary trusts his hands more than a search bar. He knows the rack by feel. Third shelf from the bottom, past the unmarked bootlegs, is where the free jazz lives, alphabetized loosely by a system only he understands. He'll tell you it's not nostalgia. He'll say the digital library is missing half of what's on the shelf, that somebody was supposed to rip everything and never finished, and he's not wrong. There's a box near the door labeled TO BE DIGITIZED that has sat there since a volunteer named Priya moved to Halifax.

The CD player itself is a Denon, rack-mounted, bought secondhand from a station that closed in Regina. It skips on track fourteen of a particular Cecil Taylor record, and Gary works around it, cueing up track fifteen instead and explaining nothing to the three or four people listening. The board operator on Sunday mornings, a retired teacher named Elaine, doesn't touch the CDs at all. She runs entirely off a laptop, playlists built the night before, and she's the one who keeps threatening, gently, to get someone in to finally digitize the whole rack. Nobody minds the threat. Nobody expects it to happen soon.

There's a logic to keeping it around that has nothing to do with sound quality. The rack is a kind of memory the station can't afford to lose any other way — no budget for an archivist, no time, and the discs themselves hold liner notes, handwriting in the margins from hosts who've since died or moved to Portland or just stopped showing up. A show that aired in 2003 is legible on the spine of a case in a way it isn't in any spreadsheet. The manager, a man named Dennis who also fixes the transmitter when it drops out, says he'll deal with the rack eventually. He's been saying that since he took the job. In the meantime the dust keeps settling, fine and constant, the way it does in a building that used to sell paint and now sells, three hours a night, whatever Gary decides people ought to hear.
