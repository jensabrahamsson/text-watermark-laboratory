The panels are still clean on the south side, which is the cruel part. Someone hosed them down in April and the glass held that clarity for a while, throwing light onto rows that no longer justify it. Now the light falls on bolted stems, on tomato vines gone the color of rope, on a basil bed that bolted three weeks ago and turned every leaf bitter and small.

Miriam still comes up on Tuesdays. She has stopped bringing the clipboard. For the first year there was a clipboard, a chart with columns for yield and pest pressure and someone's idea of a five-year projection, taped to the inside of the door where the humidity eventually curled it loose at the corners and it hung there like a shed skin until the facilities guy took it down without asking anyone.

The irrigation system runs on a timer that nobody has recalibrated since the building changed water pressure sometime in the fall. Now it dumps too much on the north beds and nothing at all on the raised boxes by the door, so you get this strange zonation, a rainforest and a desert separated by eleven feet of walkway. The peppers in the desert zone have given up being peppers. They're just gray woody stalks now, still upright, still technically alive if you want to be generous about what alive means.

There's a hand-lettered sign near the entrance, laminated, that says WELCOME TO THE SKY GARDEN — GROWN BY US, FOR US, and under it someone has written in marker, smaller, "Please don't pick anything, ask first," and under that, in different handwriting, "there's nothing to pick." Nobody's taken the sign down. Nobody's put a new one up.

Two of the rain barrels have algae blooming green-black at the waterline, thick enough that it doesn't move when you tip the barrel, just slides as one piece like a lid. The compost bin near the stairwell stopped getting turned sometime around June and now it just steams a little on cold mornings, a private weather system nobody visits.

What still works, more or less: the strawberries in the far corner, planted by someone who left the company in March and apparently forgot they existed, or didn't, and just let them be. They're small and sparse but they're strawberries, red against all that gray and brown, and once a week or so somebody eats one standing there, alone, before going back down the stairs to the office below, where the light is fluorescent and does not depend on the weather.

The elevator up here requires a badge that half the building doesn't have anymore, reassigned or deactivated in some HR system nobody remembers logging into. So mostly it's quiet. Wind gets into the gaps where a pane doesn't quite seat right, and it makes a sound like breathing, in through one gap and out through another, all day, whether or not anyone's there to hear it.
