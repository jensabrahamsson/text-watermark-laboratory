The leashes hang on hooks by the door in the order she uses them: the retractable one for the beagle, who pulls sideways at every smell; the short leather one for the shepherd mix, who walks close and doesn't need much; the two matching nylon ones for the pair of terriers who belong to the same house but hate each other on principle. She takes them down at nine, after the morning rush of owners has left for the train.

The park has three entrances. She prefers the one on Almvägen because it isn't paved and the gravel keeps the dogs' attention on their feet instead of on each other. By the second week of a new dog she's stopped noticing the smell of other people's homes on their fur — cigarette smoke on the beagle, some kind of citrus detergent on the shepherd mix — but the first week it's always there, a small intimacy she didn't ask for.

The terriers are named Sixten and Bo. Their owner works from home and could walk them himself, he's told her twice, unprompted, as if she'd asked. He pays on time. He stands in the doorway while she clips the leashes on and says something about the weather that isn't really about the weather. She has stopped trying to name what it is.

At the far end of the park there's a bench where the other walkers sit, if there are other walkers out. Sometimes there's a woman with three small dogs bred for something ornamental, and they talk about nothing — routes, weather, a dog that bit another dog near the tennis courts last spring. The woman does this full-time, has business cards. She once said, without irony, that she liked dogs more than the people who owned them, and it seemed less like a complaint than something she'd worked out carefully and settled into.

The beagle finds a dead bird under the hedge by the fountain, in October, and she has to pull him off it, and he sulks the rest of the walk with his ears back, and this is not a big event, it happens most autumns. She texts the owner: found a bird, all fine, he's just being dramatic. The owner sends back a laughing emoji. That's the whole exchange.

By November the light is gone before four and she does the last walk of the day with a headlamp clipped to her jacket, the beam swinging over gravel and wet leaves, the dogs' eyes flashing green when they turn to check on her. The shepherd mix has arthritis starting in his hips, she can feel it in how he takes the curb now, and she'll mention it to his owner, and the owner will say they've noticed too, and nothing more will come of it for a while, because that's how these things go — noticed, mentioned, absorbed into the routine, not fixed.

She locks the gate behind the last dog, hangs the leash back on its hook, and walks home the long way because the short way means passing the fountain again.
