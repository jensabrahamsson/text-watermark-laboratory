The first time, I killed it with kindness. I fed the starter every twelve hours like a nervous new parent, kept it on the counter under a tea towel, whispered encouraging things at it, probably. It bubbled up beautifully for about a week, and I thought I'd cracked it — some ancient, forgiving process that just wanted attention. Then I baked. The loaf came out dense and pale, more like a sad brick with a lid than bread, and when I cut into it the crumb was tight and gluey, no open holes, no lift. I'd trusted the bubbles too much. Bubbles just mean gas is happening somewhere; they don't mean your starter has the strength to lift two pounds of flour and water into something with structure. I hadn't tested it — no float test, no sense of whether it had actually doubled in a predictable window. I just liked the look of it and assumed liking was the same as knowing.

The second time I overcorrected, which is its own kind of failure. I got obsessive about numbers — hydration percentages, exact gram weights, a timer for every stretch-and-fold, a chart taped to the cabinet. The dough felt right during bulk fermentation, all silky and full of little bubbles under the surface, but I shaped it too roughly, punishing it back down out of some misplaced idea that more folding meant more discipline. Then I proofed it too long because I got distracted, came back to a puddle instead of a boule, poured it into the dutch oven more than shaped it, and out came something flat and sour in the wrong way — not the pleasant tang of a well-fermented loaf but something closer to vinegar, evidence that the yeast had run out of anything left to eat and the bacteria had taken over the party entirely.

What I've come to think is that sourdough isn't really a recipe you follow, it's a relationship you read. The starter is alive in the loose sense that it's a colony behaving according to temperature, hydration, and time, and your job isn't to control it but to notice it — is it domed or flat, does it smell like yogurt or nail polish remover, does a spoonful float or sink. My two failures were both failures of attention dressed up as different things: the first was inattention pretending to be trust, the second was attention pretending to be control. Neither one was actually looking at the dough in front of me.

I haven't tried a third time yet. Partly because I've been letting the starter just sit in the fridge, getting fed once a week instead of demanding daily performance from it, which feels like the first honest thing I've done in this whole process. There's something almost embarrassing about how much a jar of fermenting flour can expose about your own tendencies — toward either abdication or micromanagement, rarely toward the calm middle place where you just watch something closely and respond to what it's actually doing.
