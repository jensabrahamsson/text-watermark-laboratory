The transmitter hums somewhere behind a plywood partition, and nobody has touched it in eleven years except to swear at it when it drops out during a thunderstorm. The station occupies the second floor of what used to be a hardware store, above a laundromat that still has the old paint cans in the window as decoration, or maybe nobody ever took them down. You climb a metal staircase that rings under your feet and let yourself in with a key that's been copied so many times it barely turns.

The CD rack sits against the far wall, floor to ceiling, the kind of oak shelving unit that was probably bought secondhand in 1994 and never replaced because replacing it would mean admitting something. Every case has a strip of masking tape on the spine with handwriting in at least four different pens, initials of whoever cataloged it, sometimes a genre scrawled underneath in block letters that don't match anything a streaming algorithm would recognize. FOLK/WEIRD. NOT XMAS UNTIL DEC. DJ MARCUS ONLY DO NOT TOUCH, though Marcus hasn't done a shift since 2019.

The dust isn't neglect exactly. It's more like sediment, a record of how often each case gets pulled. The ones near eye level are clean, worn soft at the corners. The top shelf, the stuff filed under things like AMBIENT/DRONE or LOCAL BANDS 90s, has a gray felt on it thick enough to write your name in, and somebody has, in the past, written their name in it, and then somebody else wrote underneath, in the same dust, "leave it."

There's a laptop now, obviously. There's a hard drive with everything digitized, or everything that mattered enough to digitize, which turned out to be maybe sixty percent of the collection. The rest just sits there. Nobody's thrown it out. There was a meeting once — actual minutes were taken — about whether to donate the redundant CDs to the library sale, and it ended without a decision, the way these things do, and the boxes that were packed for donation are still sitting under the mixing board, taped shut, six years now.

The overnight guy, who does a jazz show nobody schedules him for but everyone lets him keep, still pulls physical discs. He says the laptop's search function doesn't know what he's looking for until he already knows what he's looking for, which doesn't make sense until you watch him do it — running a finger along the spines, stopping, tilting his head, pulling one out because of the way the light hit it or because he remembered a Tuesday in 2011. He'll play the same three tracks off a promo copy some label rep hand-delivered decades ago, a band that broke up before finishing a second record, and he'll say their name on air like the audience should know it.

Underwriting keeps the lights on, barely. The board still buzzes on channel four. The rack stays because moving it would mean finding somewhere else to put eleven thousand discs nobody's ready to call obsolete, and because on slow nights, dust and all, it's still faster than scrolling.
