The room smelled like radiator dust and cold coffee. Someone had propped the door with a fire extinguisher so the hallway light bled in a long yellow strip across the floor, and at the far end of that strip sat the piano, lid up, one leg replaced with a stack of paperbacks because the original had cracked in the move from the old building.

Sara hit the middle C and nothing happened. She hit it again, harder.

"That one's dead," said Marcus from the folding chair by the window. He had his script rolled into a tube and was tapping it against his knee. "Whole octave's dead, actually. B below middle C down to the F."

"So what am I supposed to do for the wedding scene."

"Hum it."

"I'm not going to hum it, Marcus."

He shrugged and looked back out the window, where the parking lot lights were coming on one by one, that orange sodium color that makes everyone look a little sick. Behind Sara, Dennis was on the floor doing the thing he did before every rehearsal, lying flat with his knees bent, breathing like he was trying to inflate something inside himself slowly enough that it wouldn't burst.

"We could ask Facilities again," Dennis said, without opening his eyes.

"We asked Facilities in October."

"We could ask again."

Sara pressed down on the dead keys anyway, one after another, watching the hammers rise and fall soundlessly inside the open case, little felt heads nodding like they agreed with her about something. There was a string in there, somewhere, that had gone slack or snapped; you could see it if you leaned in, a curl of wire lying against the others like it had given up.

"It's kind of nice, actually," Dennis said. "The quiet part."

"It's not supposed to be quiet. It's a wedding."

"I know. I'm just saying, structurally, if you think about it—"

"Don't," said Marcus.

Dennis sat up, hurt in the specific way that people who've said something they think is smart get hurt when nobody wants to hear it. He didn't say anything else. Sara moved her hand up the keyboard into the range that still worked and played four bars, the melody thin and correct and completely wrong without the low notes underneath it, like a sentence with all its verbs taken out.

"Try it an octave up the whole way through," Marcus said.

"That's not the song."

"It's close to the song."

"It's not the song, Marcus."

Nobody moved for a second. Outside, a car alarm went off, cycled through its three tones, stopped. The extinguisher by the door had a dent in its side, and Sara found herself looking at that instead of the piano, at the dent and at the little pressure gauge on top, the needle sitting in the green, sure of itself.

"Fifteen minutes," said the stage manager's voice from somewhere down the hall, not unkind, just a fact being stated into the building.

Sara closed the lid partway, so it rested on her fingers, not all the way down. Dennis lay back on the floor. Marcus went back to looking at the parking lot.
