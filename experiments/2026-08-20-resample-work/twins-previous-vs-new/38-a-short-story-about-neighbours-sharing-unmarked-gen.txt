The line ran diagonally across the courtyard, from a hook bolted into the brick above Ingrid's kitchen window to a post near the bins that nobody remembered installing. Four households used it, though only two ever spoke about the schedule, and that was Ingrid and the man from the ground floor whose name she always forgot until he told her again, which he did every few months, patiently, as if it were new information both times.

"Torvald," he said this time, pinning a shirt.

"Right," she said.

The shirt was pale blue with a frayed collar. He wore it to the allotment on Sundays, and she knew this because she'd seen him leave in it and come back in it, smelling of soil and cut grass. He hung his washing early, before eight, before the woman on the second floor came down with her son's football kits, which took up more line than seemed fair for one child's sport.

Ingrid hung sheets. Two sets, rotated weekly, white going grey at the hems no matter how she washed them. She'd stopped trying to fix that. Some things wore down and you let them.

There was an order to it, unspoken but real. Torvald's shirt, then whatever Ingrid had, then the football kits strung up like flags, then, later, sometimes not at all, the fourth household — a young couple on the top floor who worked strange hours and sometimes left their washing out overnight, which Torvald found irritating in a way he never mentioned to anyone but the pigeons that gathered on the bin post.

"They'll get rained on," he told the pigeons.

The pigeons did not hang washing and had no opinion.

One Tuesday the line snapped. Not dramatically — no crash, no shirts flung to the cobblestones — just a slow sag over the course of an afternoon until Ingrid's sheets were brushing the ground and she came out to find the whole thing drooping like a tired smile. Torvald was already there, examining the hook.

"Rusted through," he said.

"Can you fix it?"

"I can fix it."

He went to get tools she didn't know he owned. She held the sheets bunched in her arms while he worked, standing close enough to see the grey in his hair and the steadiness of his hands, which surprised her, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected instead.

"There," he said, after twenty minutes. The line was taut again, a new hook driven into fresh brick dust.

"Thank you, Torvald."

"You remembered my name."

"I did."

They didn't discuss it further. She hung her sheets. He went inside. The football kits appeared that afternoon as usual, and by evening the young couple's washing joined the others, uneven and a little damp-smelling because they'd started the load too late.

The line held. It held through autumn, through the first frost, through a windstorm in November that took down a shutter two buildings over but left the courtyard's washing swaying, undisturbed, four households' worth of ordinary cloth catching the last of the light before someone remembered to bring it in.
