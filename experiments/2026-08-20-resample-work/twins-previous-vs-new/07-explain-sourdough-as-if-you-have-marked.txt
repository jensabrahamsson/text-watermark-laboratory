The first time, I killed it with kindness. I fed the starter every twelve hours like a nervous new parent, kept it on top of the fridge where the pilot light supposedly made a warm microclimate, wrapped it in a towel when the forecast said cold front. It bubbled for four days, smelled like a gym sock had made peace with a pineapple, and I thought I'd done it. I baked. The loaf came out with the structural integrity of a wet phone book. Dense, gray, sour in a flat unpleasant way, like biting into a battery. I didn't understand that "bubbly" and "active" are not synonyms. Mine was bubbly the way a swamp is bubbly. Gas without lift.

The second time I overcorrected. I'd read that discipline was the missing piece, so I got clinical about it. Ratios weighed to the gram, water at a specific temperature, a jar with rubber bands marking rise height like I was monitoring a patient's vitals. I did everything the blog said and the blog's author had a beautiful kitchen and better flour, probably, or better air, or a life with fewer interruptions. My dough sat in bulk ferment while I took a work call that ran forty minutes over, and by the time I came back it had gone slack and sour-smelling in the wrong direction, overproofed, no strength left in the gluten. I shaped it anyway out of stubbornness. It spread instead of rising, a low disappointed puddle. When I cut into it the crumb was tight and gummy near the bottom, the way sourdough gets when you've let the yeast eat past the point of return.

What I've come to think, after both failures, is that sourdough doesn't forgive distraction, but it also doesn't reward vigilance in the way you'd expect. It's not that I wasn't careful enough. I was careful in the wrong places. I watched the clock when I should have watched the dough. I trusted a number — four days, 78 degrees, 1:1:1 ratio — when the actual information was right there in front of me the whole time: the smell, the way the surface looked when I poked it, whether it sprang back slow or fast or not at all. The starter has moods that don't map cleanly onto a schedule. Neither, it turns out, does the dough.

I still have not made a loaf I'd give to someone without a preemptive apology. The starter's alive, technically, sitting in the fridge accusing me quietly every time I open the door. I keep meaning to try again, a third time, and I know already that the mistake won't be an ingredient or a timer. It'll be some small act of not paying attention at exactly the moment attention was required, because that's apparently the whole craft, and I keep discovering how bad I am at it in a new way each time.
