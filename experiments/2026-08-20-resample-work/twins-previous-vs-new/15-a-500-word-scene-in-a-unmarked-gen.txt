Claude responded: The vending machine hummed louder than anything else in the room.

The vending machine hummed louder than anything else in the room. Elin had been staring at it for eleven minutes, at the little coil that hadn't turned since the bag of pretzels got stuck, tilted at forty-five degrees, refusing to fall.

"You want me to hit it?" her brother said.

"No."

"I'll hit it."

"Mattias, leave it."

He sat back down anyway, without hitting it, and put his elbows on his knees. The chairs were the kind that curved wrong for a human back, molded plastic in a color that wasn't quite orange and wasn't quite brown, bolted in rows of four. Across from them a man slept sitting up with his mouth open and a coat draped over him like someone had done it for him and left.

The clock above the reception desk said 2:14. It had said 2:14 for a while now, or maybe Elin just kept looking back before a minute had passed.

"Do you think Dad's asleep," Mattias said. It wasn't really a question.

"He said he'd wait by the phone."

"He always says that."

A nurse came through the double doors, scanned a badge against the wall, and disappeared down the corridor without looking at either of them. The doors sighed shut on their pneumatic arm. Somewhere behind those doors was their mother, and a room, and machines that Elin had decided not to imagine the sound of.

"I'm going to get a coffee," Mattias said.

"There's no coffee. Just the machine, and it's broken."

"There's a machine on the second floor. I saw the sign."

"You're not going to the second floor."

"Why not."

"Because you don't know where the second floor is and I don't want to sit here alone."

He looked at her, and for a second she thought he might argue it, the way he argued things on principle, but instead he just nodded and stayed. He picked at a thread on his sleeve. Somewhere a phone rang twice and stopped.

"Remember when she made us wait like this," Elin said. "At Lisa's recital. Backstage, for two hours, on chairs exactly like these."

"I remember Lisa cried because the shoes pinched."

"They didn't pinch. She just didn't want to go on."

"That's not what she said."

"That's what Mom said, after."

Mattias almost smiled. He looked at the vending machine again, then at the clock, then down the corridor at the doors that hadn't opened.

"You think it's bad," he said. Not a question this time either.

Elin didn't answer right away. She looked at the pretzels hanging in the coil, caught, going nowhere, and she thought that if she said anything at all it would come out wrong, too big or too small for the room.

"I think we wait," she said.

The doors stayed shut. The clock changed to 2:15. Somewhere down the hall, footsteps started, unhurried, and neither of them moved.
