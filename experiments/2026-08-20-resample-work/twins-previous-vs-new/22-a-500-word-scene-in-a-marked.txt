Rain came down hard on the tin awning outside, and the sound filled the laundromat like static. Only two machines were running. Ingrid sat on a plastic chair bolted to the floor, watching her towels turn over behind the round glass door.

A man came in soaked, shaking a golf umbrella that had already given up on him. He wore a suit jacket with the shoulders dark, and he looked at the row of machines like he was reading a menu in a language he didn't speak.

"You need quarters," Ingrid said. "Machine's broken. There's a change place two doors down, but it's closed."

He looked at her. "Closed."

"Sunday."

He sat down two chairs away, holding a plastic bag of clothes against his chest like it might run off. Water dripped from his hair onto his collar. Outside, a car went through the flooded gutter and threw a wave onto the sidewalk.

"I just needed to wash one shirt," he said. "I spilled wine on it before a thing. A dinner."

"You could've worn a different shirt."

"It was the shirt."

Ingrid nodded like this made sense, though it didn't, not really. The dryer beside her clunked, some button or zipper hitting the drum on its rotation, over and over, a small metal heartbeat.

"You got a card?" she asked. "Some machines here take cards now. Not all of them."

He checked his phone instead, thumb swiping, water still coming off him onto the screen. "No signal in here."

"Concrete's thick."

He put the phone away. The lights above them buzzed, one of them flickering in a rhythm that didn't match anything else in the room. A woman came in from the rain, unbothered, wearing a rain hat shaped like a rooster's comb, and went straight to the folding table without looking at either of them.

"Storm's supposed to let up by six," the woman said, to no one, or to everyone.

"That's what they said yesterday," Ingrid said.

"They're always wrong."

The man with the wine-stained shirt stood and walked to the window. Water ran down the glass in ropes, blurring the gas station sign across the street into a smear of red and white. He pressed his palm flat against the glass, not for any reason, just to feel it.

"I could lend you a couple quarters," Ingrid said. "For the one shirt. Machine take exact change."

He turned around. "You don't have to do that."

"I know I don't."

She dug in her coat pocket and came up with a fistful of change, sorted through it on her knee, counted out eight quarters and held them out. He crossed the room and took them, his hand still damp, the coins passing warm from her palm to his.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't spill on it this time."

He almost laughed, then didn't, and went to load the shirt into the machine alone.
