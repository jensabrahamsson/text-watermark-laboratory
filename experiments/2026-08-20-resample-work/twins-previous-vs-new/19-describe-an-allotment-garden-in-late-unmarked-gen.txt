The gate to lot 14 hangs on one hinge now, has for two summers, and nobody has fixed it because fixing it would mean admitting the season is what it is. Inside, the runner beans have gone woody on the frame, pods swollen and spotted, a few split open on the ground where the pigeons have already been at them. Someone left the hose coiled wrong and it's frozen into that shape overnight, a stiff loop you have to step over.

The compost bin at the back corner is full of blackened tomato vines pulled last week, stems still faintly green at the cut end. Windfall apples sit in the grass under the two trees, most of them going brown and soft on one side, wasps working the split skins in the weak sun before ten and after two, sluggish, not bothering to fly when you walk past. The grass between the beds hasn't been cut since the beginning of the month and it's matted down in patches where the rain flattened it.

Someone's dahlias are still going in the plot two rows over, dark red, staked with bamboo canes and green twine, and they look wrong against everything else that's finished — too much color, too upright. Next to them, a squash the size of a rugby ball has been left on the vine so long the vine itself has died back around it, just this pale orange thing sitting in dead leaves.

The shed doors are shut with a bent nail instead of the padlock, which means whoever was last here didn't plan on coming back this week. Inside, if you looked, there'd be seed packets from March still on the shelf, half used, and a trowel with dried clay on the blade. The rainwater butt is green at the waterline and nearly full, the surface skinned over with something you don't want to put your hand in.

Leeks are still standing, the one crop that doesn't seem to know the season's turning — thick white stems, blue-grey leaves fanned out, planted in a double row with string still tied between two canes to keep them straight, though the string's sagging now. A few brussels sprouts stalks nearby, the sprouts themselves small and tight, not worth picking yet, leaves yellowing from the bottom up.

Down the path, someone's put a chair out — a kitchen chair, wooden, one arm gone — and left it facing the plot instead of any view, like they sat there to look at what needed doing rather than to rest. Cigarette ends in the dirt beside one leg. A tarp weighted with bricks covers what might be a bed prepped for garlic, or might be nothing, hard to tell without lifting it, and nobody's lifting it today.

At the far end, the allotment association's noticeboard has a laminated sheet about the winter watering schedule, corners curling, and under it someone's pinned a handwritten note offering free rhubarb crowns, no phone number, just a name.
