The last bus pulls out at 3:52 and the building changes temperature, though the thermostat hasn't moved. I give it ten minutes before I start, in case someone's forgotten a inhaler or a permission slip and a parent comes back for it.

I do the gym first because the gym floor shows everything. Under the north hoop there's a spray of grape juice gone tacky, and I go over it twice with the auto-scrubber, slow pass, then cross-hatch. A kid's retainer case sits by the bleachers, blue, with a name written in Sharpie that's mostly worn off. I put it on the lost-and-found shelf next to four single gloves and a recorder.

The hallways are easier. Wide dust mop, corner to corner, and I watch the dust roll up like a wave that never breaks. Locker 114 has a sticker half peeled off, someone's band logo, and I don't touch it because that's not vandalism, that's just a kid marking territory, and the difference matters when you fill out the maintenance log.

Room 6 is the third-grade room and the chairs are already up on the desks, which means Ms. Aldana did it before she left, which she doesn't always do. I appreciate it without needing to tell her. The wastebasket has an orange peel, three balled-up worksheets, and a note folded into a triangle that I don't open. Not my business. I've found things in triangle notes before that made the rest of the shift heavier, and I've learned to leave the folding alone.

The bathrooms take the longest. Paper towels missed the bin the way they always do, a small drift of them on the tile like fallen leaves nobody's going to rake. I run the mop under the stall dividers, refill the soap, the same brand that clogs the same dispenser on the end, and I make a note to ask the district again about switching suppliers, knowing they won't.

By 5:15 the light outside has gone that flat gray-orange, and the building is fully mine in a way it isn't at any other hour. I like walking the halls when the only sound is my own cart wheel catching on the same floor seam by room 9. Not because it's peaceful, exactly. It's just quiet in a specific, structural way — the quiet of a place built for two hundred bodies currently holding one.

I check the exterior doors, the alarm panel, the gym lights. I leave the recorder and the retainer case and the four gloves where they are, on the chance someone remembers by Monday. Most of them won't. The shelf will get cleared out in June the way it always does, into a bin nobody labels, and by fall there'll be a new set of orphaned things nobody's rushing back for.

I lock the front doors at 5:40 and the motion lights click on behind me in the lobby, lighting up nothing.
