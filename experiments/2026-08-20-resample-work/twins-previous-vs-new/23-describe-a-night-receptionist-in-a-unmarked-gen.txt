The desk is a slab of laminate made to look like wood, and it has stopped looking like anything after eleven years. Bengt sits behind it on a stool with a cushion tied to the seat with two shoelaces, because the stool itself is thirty years old and the padding gave out before he started the job. He works from ten at night until six in the morning, five nights a week, and on the sixth night a woman named Ann-Sofie takes over so he can sleep in an ordinary rhythm at least once.

The lobby smells like coffee that has been on the warmer since the afternoon shift made it, and like the pine cleaner they use on the floor, and underneath that, faintly, like the carpet, which nobody can identify anymore. There is a bell on the desk that guests ring even when he is standing right there. He never says anything about it. He has decided this is not a thing worth having a feeling about.

Most of the guests who check in after midnight are people whose flights got in late or whose cars broke down outside Norrköping or who are driving trucks and need six hours before the next leg. He gives them a key card that sometimes needs two swipes, tells them breakfast starts at half six, and tells them where the ice machine is even though nobody has asked him that in four years. He likes the ones who don't want to talk. He likes the ones who do, too, in a different way, though by two in the morning he has usually run out of things to offer back.

He keeps a paperback under the desk, always something thick, because thin books run out too fast and then he has nothing until the shop opens. Right now it is a history of the Hanseatic League that he is reading slowly, ten pages a night, sometimes less if the phone rings or if someone at the bar down the street sends a drunk friend over looking for a room with no reservation. He has learned to say no to that gently. He has also learned when to say yes.

The security monitor shows four angles of the building in grainy grey, and he glances at it the way you glance at a clock, not because you expect anything different, just to know where you are in the night. Around three the building goes fully quiet, and this is the part of the job nobody warns you about when you take it — not the loneliness, exactly, but the length of it. The way three o'clock has its own weather.

He has worked here since the divorce, first because it paid the rent on the smaller apartment and let him sleep through his daughter's school hours on the weeks he had her, and now mostly out of habit. His daughter is nineteen. She texted him once at 2 a.m. from a party and he answered right away, because he was awake anyway, because he is always awake anyway, and neither of them mentioned it again.

At six he counts the till, writes the number in a book that has never once been wrong, and walks out into a morning that is always colder than he expects it to be.
