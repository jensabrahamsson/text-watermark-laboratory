Claude responded: The doors don't open at nine.

The doors don't open at nine. They open at 8:40 because the first three regulars are already outside, and if you make them wait on the actual dot they get testy about it. One of them wants the newspaper — the physical one, the one that still comes — and if it's late from the distributor he'll ask three separate staff members about it before nine-thirty.

Before any of that, someone's already been in since maybe 7:30, and the first hour isn't spent thinking about books at all. It's checking the overnight book drop, which is always half full of things dropped in wrong — a DVD wedged sideways, someone's library card that fell out of a returned book, once a sandwich. Then it's logging into the circulation system, which on a bad day takes four attempts because the network switches over from the overnight backup weirdly. Somebody checks the thermostat because the HVAC guy who was supposed to come Tuesday didn't come Tuesday.

Then it's shelving. This is most of the actual physical labor of a library and almost nobody who doesn't work there thinks about it. Returned books pile up in bins, and they get sorted by section, and then someone pushes a cart around for ninety minutes putting things back in Dewey order, except the Dewey order on the shelf never quite matches the catalog because patrons pull a book, decide against it, and shove it back three inches to the left. So there's a constant, low-grade entropy that never fully resolves. Every library has at least one staff member who's slightly obsessive about shelf-reading — walking the stacks checking sequence — and everyone else privately thinks it's a bit much until the day they can't find something and are grateful for it.

The desk itself is a mix of things that have nothing to do with each other. Someone wants help printing a boarding pass. Someone's phone died and they need to charge it to show proof of an appointment. A kid comes in alone after school because the library is free and warm and nobody asks questions, and the staff quietly keep half an eye on him without making it obvious, because that's part of the job nobody puts in the position description. Someone asks for tax forms in February. Someone asks to use a stapler. A man asks, seriously, whether the library has a fax machine, and it does, and this delights him.

Programming — the toddler storytime, the teen manga club, the "computer basics for seniors" class — gets planned weeks out but executed by whoever's actually free that hour, which means the children's librarian sometimes runs storytime with a scratchy throat because there's no one to cover. Attendance is unpredictable: eighteen kids one week, three the next, for reasons nobody can pin down.

Money is a constant undertone. The acquisitions budget is fixed and small, and every purchase is a trade-off — a new bestseller everyone's requesting versus replacing the water-damaged encyclopedia set nobody's opened in six years but that's technically reference material. Interlibrary loan requests come in for oddly specific things — a 1970s trade manual, a genealogy record from a county three states over — and someone has to track down which library actually has it and mail it, literally mail it, in a padded envelope.

By afternoon there's a rhythm: the after-school crowd, someone printing forty pages badly because the printer's low on toner and nobody's ordered more, a patron complaint about a fine that's really about something else entirely. Closing means walking the building, turning off the public computers that half the time have three browser tabs frozen mid-search, counting the cash drawer for fines, and locking the book drop so nothing gets dumped in overnight uncollected.

It's not chaos, exactly. It's just a lot of small, unglamorous continuity — the same shelf, the same drop box, the same regulars — held together by people doing several jobs at once and rarely getting credit for the parts that aren't checking out books.
