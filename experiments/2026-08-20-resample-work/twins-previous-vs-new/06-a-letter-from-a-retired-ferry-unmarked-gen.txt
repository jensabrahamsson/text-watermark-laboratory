Dear Elin,

You asked me on the phone last week what fog actually is, and I gave you some answer about condensation that I could tell didn't satisfy either of us. I've been thinking about it since. Let me try again, properly this time, the way I would have explained it to a young deckhand who needed to trust it rather than just understand it.

Fog is water that hasn't decided yet. That's not a scientific way to put it, but it's the truest one I know. Warm air holds moisture the way a hand holds sand — loosely, more than you'd think — and when that air meets something colder, the sand starts falling through the fingers. Over open water, especially in spring when the sea is still cold from winter and the air above it has started to warm, this happens slowly and evenly, and you get fog that sits for hours, sometimes days.

I worked the Vaxholm run for thirty-one years, and if you asked me my most vivid memories, more of them are fog than storm. Storms are dramatic and then they're over. Fog just continues. You'd leave the pier at Strömkajen with sixty passengers and complete confidence, and twenty minutes out you'd be in a world with no edges. No horizon, no shoreline, sometimes no sense of which way was down except for the deck under your feet. The sound changes too — people don't talk about that part. Everything gets closer and duller at once. A foghorn from another vessel could sound like it was fifty meters off your bow when it was a kilometer away. I learned not to trust my ears any more than my eyes.

What you actually navigate by, in real fog, is instruments and patience, in about equal measure. Compass heading, elapsed time, known speed — dead reckoning, they call it, which always struck me as a strange name for something so alive with attention. You are reckoning with the fact that you are, in fact, dead in the water if you get it wrong. I had radar in my later years, which helped enormously and also took away a certain skill that the older captains had, the ones who trained me — men who could read a fog bank by its density and the particular grey of it and tell you whether it would burn off by ten or hold until dusk. I never got that good. I don't think many did.

There was a run in 1987, I think, maybe '88, where I brought the boat in an hour late because I simply stopped and waited rather than push through blind past Rindö. A passenger complained to the company. I didn't mind. The ones who complain about a captain being cautious in fog have never been in fog that didn't lift.

I'm not writing this to teach you a lesson about patience, though I notice I seem to have written one anyway. Mostly I wanted to answer your question properly, since you asked, and since I have the time now that I didn't always have then. The archipelago in fog is still, to my mind, the strangest and most honest version of that place — everything you thought you knew about where you are, suspended, until it isn't.

Come out and see me before the summer gets away from us.

Love,
Morfar
